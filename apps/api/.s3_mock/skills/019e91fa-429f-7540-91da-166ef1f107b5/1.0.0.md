## When to use

Invoke this skill when a manager or people-operations partner is preparing for a new hire's first 90 days. The skill produces a phased plan covering pre-start logistics, week one, and the canonical 30/60/90 milestone structure, calibrated to the specific role and team rather than a generic template. The plan is sized to be usable by both the manager and the new hire as a living document.

The skill is calibrated for individual contributor through senior director hires. It works for technical, operational, commercial, and creative roles, and adapts ramp expectations to the role family. It produces especially strong output for the first hire of a kind on a team (the first designer, the first international hire, the first manager) because the methodology forces explicit naming of what the role will learn and from whom, rather than relying on prior tribal knowledge.

Do not use this skill for executive-level (C-suite, EVP) onboarding, which involves stakeholder mapping, board introductions, and 90-day strategic-assessment patterns that have different conventions. Do not use it as a substitute for company-wide onboarding logistics (HRIS setup, benefits enrollment, security training); those are operational checklists best handled by people-ops infrastructure, and the skill assumes they are running in parallel.

If the success_picture input is missing or vague, surface the gap before drafting. The single most reliable predictor of a successful first 90 days is whether the new hire and manager share an explicit, written picture of what success looks like. Without it, the plan reverts to a list of activities rather than a path to a destination, and ninety days later the manager and the new hire disagree about whether ramp went well.

## How to apply

Most onboarding plans fail in one of three predictable ways: they front-load social and orientation activities into week one and then leave the new hire to figure out the actual work; they list activities without naming the outcomes the activities are supposed to produce; or they treat the 30/60/90 milestones as a checklist of company-knowledge gates rather than a progression of increasing ownership. The methodology below addresses each in turn.

### Step 1 — Anchor the plan to the success picture

Before listing any activity, restate the success picture in two precise forms: what the new hire will own at six months, and what the new hire will own at twelve months. These two anchors govern every ramp activity. If an activity does not contribute to one of them, it does not belong in the plan.

If the hiring brief or JD only provides a vague success picture ("be a great senior engineer"), translate it into two or three specific ownership anchors before drafting the rest of the plan. For example: "By month six, owns the on-call rotation on the payments service end-to-end. By month twelve, has led one platform-level migration as the technical lead."

Without anchors, the plan becomes a list of meetings.

### Step 2 — Calibrate ramp expectations to seniority and domain

Ramp speed varies systematically:

- **Junior IC (0-3 years).** Most of ramp is learning how to work in this team's specific environment; substantive contribution typically lands in months 3-4 for low-complexity domains, months 5-6 for high.
- **Mid-level IC (3-7 years).** Ramp time on a similar stack is months 2-3; on a new stack or domain, months 4-5. The new hire is expected to start meaningfully contributing while still in the learning phase.
- **Senior IC (7+ years).** Ramp time on a similar stack is months 1-2; on a new stack, months 3-4. They should be productively asking sharp questions in week two.
- **Manager (new to managing).** Ramp time to functional management is months 3-4 regardless of seniority; ramp to genuinely good management of this team is months 6-9.
- **Manager (experienced).** Ramp time to running the team is months 1-2; ramp to first organizational change is months 3-4.
- **Senior leader (Director+).** Ramp to functional leadership is months 2-3, plus a structured listening phase in the first 60 days that is itself a deliverable.

These are templates, not rules. The domain_complexity input adjusts: high-complexity domains add 30-50% to every ramp range, low-complexity domains reduce by similar amounts.

### Step 3 — Design the pre-start phase

The pre-start phase (between offer accept and day one) is often left empty and is the highest-leverage low-cost phase to design well. Recommendations that recur across sources:

- **Send equipment and credentials on time.** A new hire who shows up on day one without a working laptop loses two productive days; the issue compounds emotionally because day one is when new hires form their durable impression of company competence.
- **Share a small, optional reading list.** One to three items, no more than four hours of total reading, including the company's mission or strategy document and one piece that signals what the team cares about. Frame as optional but useful; over-reading creates burnout before day one.
- **Schedule the first-week calendar before they arrive.** Calendar logistics done in advance free the new hire to focus on conversations rather than scheduling.
- **Notify the team.** A short note from the manager to the team naming who is starting, what role, and what the team's first interactions with them should be. Reduces the awkwardness of week-one introductions.
- **Connect them with the buddy or mentor.** A 15-minute video chat or message exchange between the new hire and the buddy before day one, framed casually, dramatically reduces day-one social anxiety.

The pre-start phase is six to eight items. It should be assignable: each item has an owner (the manager, the recruiter, IT, the buddy) and a deadline relative to the start date.

### Step 4 — Design week one

Week one is not for substantive work; it is for orientation, social grounding, and forming a working relationship with the manager and team. The temptation is to either over-program it (every hour scheduled) or under-program it (the new hire is left to drift). The right shape is something between: most of the week is structured, with deliberate unstructured time, and one clearly named small deliverable by Friday.

A defensible week-one structure:

- **Day 1:** Manager 1:1 in the morning naming the success picture, the buddy/mentor pairing, and the explicit ramp expectation. Standard HR/IT logistics. Lunch with the team (in person or virtual). One short reading anchor for the next day.
- **Day 2:** Buddy walks the new hire through the team's specific tools, conventions, and quirks. Three to four 1:1 introductions with key cross-functional partners. Reading anchor expanding to the team's recent quarterly plan or roadmap.
- **Day 3:** Shadow a real working meeting (standup, planning, customer call). Mid-week 1:1 with the manager focused on questions the new hire has accumulated, not status.
- **Day 4:** First small concrete contribution starts — a low-stakes deliverable scoped to be completable by end of week one. The contribution should be real (something the team would do anyway) but small (low risk if it takes longer than expected).
- **Day 5:** Complete the small deliverable. End-of-week 1:1 with the manager naming what was learned, what was confusing, and what the next week looks like. Optional team retro or social event.

The "first small concrete contribution by Friday" pattern is consistently the single biggest week-one quality marker. New hires who land their first small win in week one ramp faster across multiple subsequent measurements; new hires who do not develop a "I'm not contributing yet" anxiety that meaningfully slows ramp.

### Step 5 — Days 1-30: learning with structured contribution

The first 30 days are predominantly learning, but with a meaningful contribution arc. Specific shape:

- **Learning anchors.** Three to five named learning outcomes the new hire should achieve, with specific artifacts or conversations as evidence. Examples: "By day 30, can describe the system's three biggest architectural choices and the historical context for each", "Has shadowed at least three customer calls and can name the top three customer-friction patterns", "Has reviewed at least five PRs and produced thoughtful comments on at least one".
- **Contribution arc.** Three to five small deliverables, each individually small but visible. The arc should escalate in scope across the 30 days: week one's deliverable should be smaller than week four's.
- **Mentor cadence.** Twice-weekly check-ins with the buddy or mentor in the first two weeks, weekly thereafter. Buddy is for tactical questions; manager is for trajectory and feedback.
- **Manager cadence.** Twice-weekly 1:1s for the first two weeks, weekly thereafter. The first month's 1:1s focus on questions the new hire has accumulated and feedback in both directions.
- **First feedback exchange.** By the end of week three, a structured two-way feedback conversation: what is working, what is confusing, what the new hire needs more or less of.

### Step 6 — Days 31-60: contribution with reduced scaffolding

The middle phase is the hardest to design because it is where new hires either accelerate or stall. The defining shape is increasing ownership with decreasing scaffolding. Specific moves:

- **First substantive deliverable.** A genuinely visible piece of work the new hire owns end-to-end. For an engineer, a meaningful feature or non-trivial refactor; for a marketer, a campaign or messaging brief; for a CSM, leading the next QBR for one of their accounts; for a manager, the first formal 1:1 cycle.
- **Cross-functional integration.** By day 45, the new hire should have substantive working relationships with three to five cross-functional partners and be inserting themselves into the right meetings without prompting.
- **First independent decision.** Specific moment where the new hire makes a real call that the manager would otherwise have made. Could be technical, prioritization, or interpersonal. Naming the moment explicitly in the plan signals to both manager and new hire that the transition is happening.
- **Mentor cadence reduces.** Weekly or every-other-week.
- **Manager cadence shifts.** Weekly 1:1s remain but increasingly focus on trajectory and obstacle-clearing rather than orientation.
- **Mid-phase feedback exchange.** Around day 45, a more substantive two-way conversation. By this point both manager and new hire have enough data to assess fit honestly. Surface concerns early; new hires who are struggling at day 45 and only hear about it at day 90 rarely recover.

### Step 7 — Days 61-90: ownership and the first independent assessment

The third phase is about closing the ramp. Specific moves:

- **First independent assessment of the role.** Around day 75, the new hire writes a short document for themselves and the manager: what they have learned, what they think is working in the team, what they would change if they had the authority, what they need to be successful in the next quarter. This document is the highest-leverage onboarding artifact for senior hires; it forces synthesis and makes their early observations available before the new hire is fully assimilated and stops seeing the team with fresh eyes.
- **Ownership transition complete.** By day 90, the new hire owns the work outlined in the six-month success picture without ongoing scaffolding from the manager or buddy. If they do not, the plan needs a written extension rather than a graceful fade.
- **Buddy relationship transitions to peer.** The formal buddy role ends at day 90. The relationship persists informally.
- **Probation or evaluation milestone.** If the company has a formal probation period or 90-day review, this is the moment. The review is anchored to the success picture from Step 1, not to a generic performance rubric. Honest feedback in both directions.
- **Next quarter's goals defined.** Days 90 to 180 are the new hire's first quarter of full ownership; the goals for it should be set by day 90 and should match the trajectory the success picture implies.

### Step 8 — Design the manager checklist

The manager has an outsized effect on ramp quality. The skill produces a manager-specific checklist covering:

- **Pre-start.** Confirm equipment and credentials, send the welcome note to the team, share the success picture document with the new hire, identify and brief the buddy.
- **Week one.** Run the first-day 1:1. Introduce the new hire in standup. Run the end-of-week feedback exchange.
- **Day 30.** First formal check-in. Compare progress against the learning anchors from Step 5. Surface any concerns.
- **Day 45.** Mid-phase two-way feedback exchange. This is the highest-leverage manager moment in the entire ramp.
- **Day 60.** Validate the new hire is owning a substantive deliverable. Honest assessment of trajectory.
- **Day 90.** Formal review against the success picture. Set the next-quarter goals.
- **Ongoing.** Weekly 1:1s with a 70/30 split — 70% the new hire's agenda, 30% manager-driven.

### Step 9 — Design the buddy or mentor brief

The buddy role is widely under-specified, which makes it widely under-effective. The skill produces a brief that names:

- **Who.** A peer at the same level or one level senior, not a manager. Chosen for being a recent ramp success themselves, not for being the most senior.
- **What.** The buddy is the social and tactical-question stop. They are not the new hire's manager; they do not provide formal feedback or assess performance.
- **When.** Twice-weekly check-ins in weeks 1-2, weekly in weeks 3-4, every-other-week in weeks 5-8, optional in weeks 9-12.
- **Duration.** 90 days, then transitions to informal peer relationship.
- **Expected time commitment.** Two to four hours per week including check-ins and ad-hoc Slack questions. The buddy's manager should know about this and adjust the buddy's load.

Buddy programs that fail usually fail because the buddy's commitment was never named or because the buddy was chosen as a reward rather than for ramp-fit reasons.

### Step 10 — Calibrate to remote and hybrid contexts

Remote and hybrid ramps differ from in-office ramps in two structural ways: social signal is much harder to absorb passively, and the new hire's first 30 days do not naturally produce the corridor-conversation friction that surfaces confusion. Compensate by:

- **More deliberate social schedule in weeks 1-2.** Multiple short 1:1s rather than one long onboarding day. Coffee chats with named partners. A scheduled team social.
- **Earlier and shorter manager 1:1s.** Daily 15-minute check-ins for the first week or two; tapers to twice-weekly then weekly.
- **A specific "ask anything" channel.** A direct line to the buddy or a small Slack channel framed for new-hire questions reduces the friction of asking.
- **Explicit shadowing.** Schedule the new hire to shadow specific real meetings, with the relevant participants notified.
- **One in-person week early.** Where feasible, an early in-person week with the team accelerates relationship formation by months. If not feasible, name the loss honestly and compensate with synchronous video time.

### Step 11 — Calibrate to senior leader ramps

For director, VP, and senior-leader roles, the 90 days have a different shape. The conventional pattern, well-supported across the leadership-transition literature, is:

- **Days 1-30: structured listening.** The leader's first thirty days are dedicated almost entirely to one-to-one conversations with stakeholders across the org. No major decisions. The output is a synthesis document.
- **Days 31-60: hypothesis formation.** The leader names what they have heard, where they see the largest opportunities and risks, and what they intend to test. This is shared with their boss and their direct reports.
- **Days 61-90: small visible bets.** Two to three small but specific changes that demonstrate the leader has heard the team and is acting on it. Major reorganization or strategy shifts are deferred to the post-90-day period.

For these roles, the success picture in Step 1 should explicitly include the listening output as a 30-day artifact.

### Step 12 — Plan the explicit feedback exchanges

Most onboarding plans fail to name when honest two-way feedback happens, with the result that it does not happen until something is on fire. Calendar three exchanges explicitly: end of week 1, around day 45, and at day 90.

For each exchange, name the format (one-on-one conversation, optionally with a one-page shared document), the questions (what is working for you, what is not, what would you change), and the recipient (the manager owns the first and third; the new hire writes the day-75 document for both).

### Step 13 — Output the deliverables

Produce three artifacts:

- **The onboarding plan.** Phased, with named outcomes per phase, learning anchors, deliverables, and check-in cadence.
- **The manager checklist.** Time-anchored, action-oriented, owner-named.
- **The buddy brief.** Role, expected time, duration, and the explicit non-role (no formal feedback).

All three should fit on a single page each. Longer plans are read once and abandoned.

### Step 14 — Output a debrief on the plan

After the deliverables, a short debrief: the ramp template chosen and why; the highest-risk dependency in the plan (often the buddy's availability or the success picture's specificity); and what feedback at day 45 would prompt a rewrite versus what feedback would prompt staying the course.

## Examples

### Worked example — Senior Backend Engineer joining payments team, remote

**Inputs given to the skill:**

- role_title: "Senior Backend Engineer (L5)"
- team_context: "Five engineers, one EM, partners with infra and the platform tech lead. Reports to the payments EM."
- success_picture: "Month 6: owns the ledger reconciliation pipeline end-to-end. Month 12: has led one zero-downtime data migration as the technical lead."
- company_stage: "Series B, 80 people, fully remote with quarterly onsite weeks in NYC."
- prior_onboarding_assets: "Internal handbook in Notion, recorded architecture talk from six months ago, last new-hire engineer's first-week plan."
- domain_complexity: "high"

**Onboarding plan (abridged):**

**Pre-start (days -7 to 0):**
- IT ships laptop, credentials, and access by day -5.
- Manager sends success picture document and the optional reading list (recorded architecture talk, two pages of company strategy, one external paper on ledger systems).
- Buddy (a mid-level engineer who joined eight months ago) reaches out for a casual 20-minute video chat.
- Manager posts welcome note to team Slack at day -1.

**Week one:**
- Day 1: manager 1:1 (success picture, ramp expectations, buddy intro). HR/IT setup. Lunch with team (virtual). Reading anchor: architecture talk.
- Day 2: buddy walks tooling, conventions, on-call shape. Intros with platform tech lead and infra lead.
- Day 3: shadow standup, shadow one customer escalation call. Mid-week 1:1 with manager.
- Day 4: small contribution begins — review the open PR for the recon pipeline metrics rollout.
- Day 5: complete the PR review with substantive comments. End-of-week 1:1 with manager.

**Days 1-30:**
- Learning anchors: can describe the ledger's partitioning history; can describe top three reconciliation failure modes; can name the team's three on-call hotspots.
- Contributions: ship two small PRs against the recon backlog. Begin shadow on-call by day 14, primary on-call by day 28.
- Mentor cadence: twice-weekly check-ins with buddy in weeks 1-2, weekly in weeks 3-4.
- Manager cadence: twice-weekly 1:1s in weeks 1-2, weekly thereafter.
- Day 21: structured two-way feedback exchange.

**Days 31-60:**
- First substantive deliverable: lead a single reconciliation-pipeline reliability improvement end-to-end.
- Cross-functional integration: substantive working relationship with platform tech lead and infra lead.
- First independent decision: choose between two approaches to the reliability improvement without manager pre-approval.
- Day 45: mid-phase two-way feedback exchange.

**Days 61-90:**
- Day 75: write the new-hire synthesis document.
- Day 85: begin co-designing the next ledger-partition phase with the platform tech lead.
- Day 90: formal review against the six-month success picture. Set the goals for days 90-180, including the first major migration on the calendar.

**Manager checklist (abridged):**

- Pre-start: confirm laptop, send success picture, brief buddy.
- Week 1: run day-1 1:1, post welcome note, run end-of-week feedback.
- Day 21: feedback exchange.
- Day 45: mid-phase feedback exchange — most leveraged moment.
- Day 75: read the new-hire synthesis document and respond.
- Day 90: formal review.
- Weekly 1:1s, 70/30 split.

**Buddy brief (abridged):**

- Buddy is the team's mid-level engineer who joined eight months ago. They are the tactical-question stop, not a feedback channel.
- Expected time: 2-3 hours per week including check-ins and Slack questions, for 90 days. Their manager (the payments EM) has signed off.
- Twice-weekly check-ins in weeks 1-2, weekly in weeks 3-4, every-other-week in weeks 5-8, optional in weeks 9-12.

**Debrief on the plan:**

High-domain-complexity adjustment applied: learning anchors are heavier in days 1-30 and the first substantive deliverable is pushed to days 31-60 rather than week 4. Buddy choice is deliberate — a recent ramp success makes a better buddy than a senior engineer who has been on the team for years and has forgotten what is confusing. Highest-risk dependency: the buddy's two to three hours per week. If the buddy's load spikes (incident, project deadline), the EM needs to either backfill the buddy time personally or extend the plan.

## Limitations

This skill plans the ramp; it does not run it. The single largest predictor of onboarding outcome is the manager's discipline in actually executing the 1:1s and feedback exchanges; the skill produces the structure that makes that discipline easier but cannot enforce it.

The success picture is the load-bearing input. If it is wrong about what the role actually owns, the plan will efficiently ramp the new hire toward the wrong destination. Pair this skill with the job-description-author skill when the success picture is being defined for the first time.

The skill does not produce role-specific learning content. It names the learning anchors and the artifacts that constitute evidence, but the actual documents, talks, and walkthroughs need to be sourced from the team or built. Teams without prior onboarding assets will spend the first new-hire ramp building them.

The skill assumes the new hire has the prerequisite skills for the role. Ramp plans for hires made at a level higher than the candidate actually performs degrade quickly; the manager is responsible for catching that pattern at the day-45 feedback exchange and either adjusting the plan or making a harder call.

The 30/60/90 structure is a useful template, not an absolute. Some roles ramp faster, some slower. The methodology supports extending or compressing the phases by 20-30%; ramps that need more than 50% adjustment usually indicate either a mismatched hire or an unrealistic success picture.

The skill does not handle international onboarding logistics, work-authorization sequencing, payroll-localization, or jurisdiction-specific employment-law training. Those are handled by people-ops infrastructure running in parallel.
