# HIPAA Security Rule Readiness Assessor

> **Important — not legal advice and not a compliance determination.** This skill produces methodology guidance only. Compliance determinations require qualified counsel and, for regulated entities, formal program ownership (Privacy Officer, Security Officer, Compliance Officer, or equivalent). No skill output constitutes legal advice, satisfies a regulator's evidence requirement on its own, or substitutes for jurisdiction-specific analysis. Real submissions and notices must be authored, reviewed, and signed by qualified staff before any external action. The Security Rule is enforced by the HHS Office for Civil Rights; settled cases routinely involve gaps that an organization believed it had closed. Treat every output here as a draft to harden with qualified review.

## When to use

Invoke this skill when a covered entity or business associate needs a structured readiness picture against the HIPAA Security Rule and there is no recent, defensible assessment on file. Typical triggers are the onboarding of a new business associate that will receive electronic protected health information, the launch of a product that newly handles ePHI, the response to a notice of an OCR audit or investigation, the diligence phase of an acquisition involving a healthcare entity, the periodic refresh that an internal compliance calendar mandates, or the cleanup that follows a breach where the post-incident review revealed that prior assessments were stale. The skill also fits the common "we have grown into HIPAA" situation, where a startup that began processing wellness data starts handling diagnostic, treatment, or payment information and discovers it is now in scope.

Do not invoke this skill in lieu of a formal risk analysis when one is legally required. The Security Rule's risk-analysis implementation specification under the administrative safeguards is a regulator-facing artifact; this skill produces a working draft and gap picture that informs that artifact, not the artifact itself. The same applies to the breach-notification rule, the privacy rule, and any state-level overlay (for example California's CMIA or Texas Medical Records Privacy Act). Where state law sets a stricter floor, defer to counsel. Likewise, do not use this skill to assess ePHI handling by a workforce that has not yet been trained on the Security Awareness and Training standard — train first, then assess.

A second, less obvious correct invocation is the rapid prep for a partner-facing security questionnaire. Many covered entities require business associates to attest in writing to specific Security Rule safeguards before signing a business associate agreement. When that questionnaire arrives, this skill produces the inventory of evidence the responder will need and surfaces the gaps that will become deal blockers. Treat it as a preparation aid, not a response generator — answers on a partner questionnaire commit the organization to controls.

## How to apply

Approach the Security Rule as a regulator does: as a framework of standards, each with one or more implementation specifications labeled "required" or "addressable." Required specifications must be implemented as written. Addressable specifications must be implemented, OR the organization must document a reasoned decision either to implement an equivalent alternative or to refrain — and the decision must be defensible based on the organization's size, complexity, technical capability, the costs of the measure, and the probability and criticality of risks to ePHI. "Addressable" is not "optional." This is the most common misconception in the field and the most common finding in enforcement actions. Anchor the entire assessment around this distinction.

The assessment moves through five stages.

### Stage 1: Scope and ePHI inventory

Establish the regulatory role the organization plays and the boundary of the assessment before evaluating any control. Covered entities are health plans, health care clearinghouses, and health care providers that transmit any health information in electronic form in connection with a covered transaction. Business associates are persons or entities that perform functions or activities on behalf of, or provide certain services to, a covered entity that involve access to ePHI. A business associate's own subcontractor that creates, receives, maintains, or transmits ePHI on behalf of the business associate is itself a business associate. Capture the role explicitly because the rule's downstream allocation of responsibility depends on it.

With the role fixed, enumerate the ePHI inventory. The inventory is not a database list — it is every place ePHI is created, received, maintained, or transmitted by the entity, including production systems, staging systems, backups, log stores, ticketing systems where support agents paste case detail, email archives, fax servers, voicemail systems, mobile devices, removable media, printed documents, and the laptops of every employee with access. Do not let the inventory stop at the production database; OCR settlements consistently cite gaps in the long tail. For each location, capture the data classes (demographic identifiers, diagnostic codes, treatment notes, payment information, images), the volume order of magnitude, the access surface (workforce role, system identity, third party), and the in-flight and at-rest protections. A practical heuristic: any record that combines an individual identifier with any health-related fact is ePHI; resolve close calls in favor of inclusion.

Finally, capture the business associate landscape on both sides. Upstream, list the covered entities that flow ePHI to the organization and the status of each BAA. Downstream, list every subcontractor or vendor that touches the ePHI — including cloud providers, observability vendors, AI/ML providers, contract data labelers, and security tooling that ships logs off-site — and the status of each BAA. A surprisingly common audit finding is observability or analytics tooling that processes ePHI under a generic terms-of-service relationship with no BAA in place.

### Stage 2: Administrative safeguards

The administrative safeguards are the largest and most frequently cited section of the rule. They are also where most organizational programs are weakest, because they require sustained operational discipline rather than one-time technical work. Walk each standard.

Security management process. This standard contains the risk analysis specification, the risk management specification, the sanction policy specification, and the information system activity review specification. The risk analysis must be accurate and thorough, must consider all ePHI the entity creates, receives, maintains, or transmits, and must be ongoing — a single dated PDF from three years ago is, in OCR's consistent enforcement posture, no risk analysis at all. Capture both the artifact (does a current risk analysis exist? when was it last updated? does it cover all current ePHI flows?) and the operational hooks (is it triggered by new system rollouts? by acquisitions? by significant infrastructure changes?). The risk management specification then requires reducing risks identified in the analysis to a reasonable and appropriate level — surface the link between analysis findings and remediation plans, because a risk analysis whose findings produced no remediation is itself a finding. The information system activity review specification covers regular review of audit logs, access reports, and security incident tracking reports; this is where many organizations have logs but no review cadence.

Assigned security responsibility. A named Security Official must be designated. Document who, by name and role, and how the role is funded. A common gap is naming a Security Official whose actual time is fully consumed by other duties.

Workforce security. The authorization-and-supervision, workforce-clearance, and termination-procedures specifications collectively require that the right workforce members have the right access for the right duration. Capture the joiner-mover-leaver process end-to-end, with particular attention to mover events that often leave residual privileges in place. Termination procedures are the most enforcement-frequent of the three; access removal must be both documented and timely.

Information access management. Isolating health care clearinghouse functions (where applicable), access authorization, and access establishment and modification. Anchor this to the principle of least privilege and to the workforce roles defined elsewhere. Capture how access is granted, reviewed, and revoked, and how role definitions are maintained.

Security awareness and training. Security reminders, protection from malicious software, log-in monitoring, and password management. All four are addressable specifications, but their absence is consistently cited. Capture training cadence, content currency, completion rates, and the program for non-employee workforce members (contractors, volunteers, temporary staff).

Security incident procedures. The response-and-reporting specification requires identifying, responding to, mitigating, and documenting security incidents. Capture the incident definition the organization uses (it must be broader than "breach"), the on-call routing, the triage thresholds, the documentation discipline, and the link to the Breach Notification Rule for the subset of incidents that become reportable breaches.

Contingency plan. Data backup plan, disaster recovery plan, emergency mode operation plan, testing and revision procedures, and applications and data criticality analysis. The first three are required; the last two are addressable. Many organizations have backups but have never tested restoration. Capture the restoration test cadence and the most recent successful test.

Evaluation. Periodic technical and nontechnical evaluation in response to environmental or operational changes. This is the standard that requires the program to re-evaluate itself; capture the cadence and the most recent evaluation artifact.

Business associate contracts and other arrangements. Capture the existence, currency, and provisions of every BAA, including the satisfactory assurances each party gives that ePHI will be appropriately safeguarded.

### Stage 3: Physical safeguards

Even cloud-native organizations have physical safeguards in scope, because workforce members carry endpoints and because facility security still applies to offices where ePHI is viewed, even ephemerally. Walk the four standards: facility access controls (contingency operations, facility security plan, access control and validation, maintenance records); workstation use; workstation security; and device and media controls (disposal, media re-use, accountability, data backup and storage). The most common gaps are at end of life — disposed or repurposed devices that were never sanitized — and at the workstation use specification, where a written policy governing what workforce members may do at the workstation is absent or stale.

### Stage 4: Technical safeguards

Access control. Unique user identification, emergency access procedure, automatic logoff, and encryption and decryption. The first two are required; the last two are addressable, with the encryption specification being the addressable spec with the largest gap between its addressable label and its real-world expectation. OCR's consistent posture is that encryption at rest and in transit is the reasonable and appropriate measure for ePHI in nearly all modern environments, and the decision to refrain from encryption requires unusually strong written justification.

Audit controls. Mechanisms to record and examine activity in information systems containing ePHI. This is required. Capture which systems produce audit records, what the records contain, where they live, the retention period, and how they are reviewed.

Integrity. Mechanisms to authenticate ePHI to ensure it has not been improperly altered or destroyed. Addressable. Capture how integrity is established for stored ePHI and for ePHI in transit between systems.

Person or entity authentication. Verify that the person or entity seeking access is the one claimed. Required. Capture identity assurance mechanisms for both human and machine identities.

Transmission security. Integrity controls and encryption for ePHI in transit, both addressable. The same posture applies as for at-rest encryption: addressable in name, expected in practice.

### Stage 5: Remediation plan and risk-analysis hand-off

With findings recorded, produce a remediation plan that orders work by severity and by enforcement risk, not by ease. Severity is a function of likelihood, the impact on ePHI confidentiality/integrity/availability, the breadth of ePHI affected, the regulatory exposure of the gap, and the existence of compensating controls. Group remediations into a near-term tier (zero to sixty days, typically required-specification gaps and gaps in encryption, access control, audit, and termination procedures), a medium-term tier (sixty to one hundred eighty days, typically addressable specifications whose alternative-implementation case is weak), and a longer-term tier (six months and beyond, typically program-maturity work like the evaluation cadence and the application-criticality analysis). For each item, name the owning role (not the person), the evidence the remediation will produce, the dependency chain, and the target date.

Hand off into the formal risk analysis. The output of this skill is not a risk analysis; it is the gap picture that the risk analysis depends on and the remediation plan that the risk management process will execute. Make that hand-off explicit. Where the assessment surfaces gaps that materially change the risk picture, flag them as inputs to the next risk analysis refresh and recommend that the refresh be brought forward rather than waiting for the annual cadence.

## Inputs

Provide a plain-language description of the organization — role under the rule, ePHI inventory at the level of system categories, workforce size, geographic footprint, and any known breach or audit history. Optionally include a current control inventory (a list of policies, technical controls, training programs, and prior assessments) and the in-scope business associate relationships. If the engagement is scoped to a single safeguard category or to breach readiness, indicate that; otherwise default to a full assessment.

## Outputs

A markdown assessment report containing executive summary, scope and ePHI inventory, standard-by-standard findings with required-vs-addressable analysis and evidence pointers, the prioritized remediation plan, the risk-analysis worksheet skeleton, and an open-questions log. A parallel JSON object captures the same content in a form a GRC platform can ingest.

## Examples

A digital-health startup recently signed its first hospital customer and is preparing for the customer's vendor security review. The skill produces a readiness assessment, finds that the risk-analysis artifact is two years old and predates the current product architecture, that termination procedures are documented but the revocation evidence trail is incomplete, and that one downstream observability vendor processes log streams containing ePHI under a generic terms-of-service relationship with no BAA. The remediation plan puts the BAA gap in the zero-to-thirty-day tier alongside the risk-analysis refresh; the workforce termination evidence trail is medium-term; and the program-maturity items (evaluation cadence, application-criticality analysis) are longer-term.

A clinical lab acquiring a smaller competitor runs this skill during diligence. The assessment finds that the target's encryption-at-rest decision was documented as an addressable refrain with a justification that no longer survives current OCR posture; that the target's facility security plan was written for an office it vacated; and that three subcontractor BAAs reference the predecessor of the current regulation. These become deal conditions and a post-close remediation backlog.

## Limitations

The skill does not produce the formal risk analysis itself; it produces the gap-and-readiness picture that informs that analysis. It does not evaluate the HIPAA Privacy Rule or the Breach Notification Rule beyond the interfaces that the Security Rule defines. It does not assess state-law overlays. It does not evaluate the contents of business associate agreements at the clause level — that is the work of contract counsel. It cannot replace a Security Official, a Privacy Officer, or qualified outside counsel; treat its output as a working draft that those roles must review and own before any external use.
