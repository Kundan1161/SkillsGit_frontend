# Email Sequence Designer

## When to use

Use this skill when a marketer needs a multi-touch email plan with copy, not just one email. Typical situations: a product team is shipping an onboarding revamp and the new welcome series must be ready by launch; a lifecycle marketer needs to recover trial users who never activated; a campaign manager is building a launch arc tied to a press date; a content marketer wants to nurture leads from a gated asset into a sales conversation; a customer success team wants a self-serve re-onboarding sequence for users who lapsed after a feature change.

Skip this skill when the request is a single transactional message (use a transactional template), when the artifact is a newsletter with already-defined episodic structure (use a newsletter writer), or when the user wants to optimize an existing sequence's deliverability (a different problem requiring inbox placement tools).

## How to apply

1. **Name the conversion event.** Every sequence has exactly one. Onboarding: the activation milestone (the specific in-product action that predicts retention). Nurture: a sales-qualifying action (book demo, request quote, start trial). Re-engagement: a return visit or in-product action within a defined window. Launch: a click into the launched feature or a purchase of the launched tier. Education: completion of a learning artifact (course finished, certification claimed). Winback: a paid reactivation. If the user did not state the event in measurable terms, ask one clarifying question to lock it.
2. **Establish the time horizon.** Onboarding lives in the first 14-30 days; nurture in 21-60; launch in 7-14; re-engagement in 14-21; winback in 30-90. The horizon constrains pacing and the maximum number of touches.
3. **Pick the touch count.** Default to five for short-horizon sequences (launch, re-engagement, winback) and seven for longer (onboarding, nurture). Add an eighth and ninth only when the audience is sophisticated and the conversion event requires deeper education. More than ten touches in a sequence usually signals strategic confusion.
4. **Map each touch to a job.** The five canonical jobs are: welcome (set the contract), educate (raise the awareness rung), prove (deliver social proof or evidence), nudge (lower friction to the conversion event), and exit (graceful closure with a return path). A well-designed five-email sequence assigns one job per email. A seven-email sequence allows two educate touches or two prove touches.
5. **Sequence the jobs to match attention decay.** Open rates decline across a sequence; the most consequential ask should not land on the lowest-attention touch. The pattern that survives most A/B tests: welcome → educate → prove → educate → nudge → prove → exit. Variants are valid; the discipline is to decide consciously, not by default.
6. **Set per-email timing.** For onboarding from signup: day 0, day 1, day 3, day 5, day 8, day 12, day 18. For re-engagement: day 0, day 3, day 7, day 12, day 18. For launch: day -3 (tease), day 0 (announce), day 2 (deep dive), day 5 (social proof), day 9 (last call). Adjust by cadence input — *aggressive* tightens by 30-40%; *slow* loosens by 50-100%.
7. **Define exit conditions.** Every email after the first must check at least one: has the user already converted (suppress remaining touches), has the user explicitly opted out (suppress and tag), has the user matched a negative signal (e.g., support ticket open — pause and notify), has the user reached an engagement ceiling (e.g., zero opens in last three sends — pause, do not delete). State the exits explicitly in the sequence plan so engineering implements them.
8. **Design branching only where the data exists.** If the platform can fire on "clicked link X" or "completed onboarding step Y," design a branch. Otherwise, force the sequence to a linear path. Speculative branches that the marketing-automation tool cannot resolve become dead code and silent bugs. The signals input tells you what is real.
9. **Write subject lines as testable hypotheses.** Each subject line implies a reason the reader will open: curiosity, benefit, identity, social proof, urgency, or specificity. Pick the implied reason consciously per touch. Provide two or three subject-line variants per email with one-line rationales. Keep subject lines short enough to fit in mobile inbox previews (40 characters reads cleanly on most clients; 60 is the upper edge).
10. **Write preheaders that complete or contradict the subject.** A preheader that repeats the subject wastes the slot. A preheader that completes the subject doubles the visible promise. A preheader that mildly contradicts the subject creates the small surprise that earns a click. Keep it ≤90 characters.
11. **Open each body with the reason for the email.** The first sentence answers "why am I receiving this now?" If the answer is "we send this every Tuesday," say so. If it is "you signed up six days ago and have not done X," say so. Hiding the trigger from the reader corrodes trust over the sequence.
12. **Use a single dominant CTA per email.** Secondary links are allowed (unsubscribe, contact, related read) but only one button-class call to action. Multiple competing asks split attention and underperform a single ask in nearly every test scenario.
13. **Vary email length deliberately.** Welcome and launch announcements are short (under 120 words). Educate emails can run 200-400 words. Prove emails (customer stories) can run 250-500 words. Nudge emails are short (80-150 words). Match the length to the job and to the reader's expected attention at that point in the sequence.
14. **Choose a sender persona and hold it.** Real-person sender ("Mira from Acme") consistently outperforms brand-name senders for nurture and onboarding because it sets a one-to-one expectation. Brand-name senders are appropriate for announcements and launches. Pick one per sequence and keep it; switching senders mid-sequence reads as awkward at best and phishing-like at worst.
15. **Write each email so it stands alone.** A reader who opens only email five must be able to act without needing email three. Reference prior touches lightly ("last week I asked…") but do not require them. The sequence is a corridor of doors; each door opens on its own.
16. **Insert proof at the predicted skepticism peak.** Skepticism rises after the educate touches because the reader has been given a claim. The prove emails (testimonial, case study, public number, named customer logo) land between educate and nudge to lower the resistance before the ask.
17. **Calibrate the ask intensity.** Email 1 should not ask for the conversion event; it sets the contract. Email 2-3 builds value. Asks intensify after the second prove email. The final email is the explicit ask plus the graceful exit ("if this is not for you right now, no hard feelings — I will pause emails on this thread and you can rejoin anytime here").
18. **Honor unsubscribe culture.** Every email contains the unsubscribe link in the standard footer location. The skill includes a recommended footer block with: physical sender address (CAN-SPAM, CASL requirement in applicable regions), unsubscribe link, preferences link, and a short note on data handling. The body never apologizes for sending; it earns the open.
19. **Write the exit email with care.** The exit (last email in the sequence) is rarely "last chance!" theater. It is "I will stop emailing you on this topic, here is what I am leaving you with, here is where to find me if it gets interesting later." The exit email often outperforms the nudge that precedes it because lowered intensity reads as respect.
20. **Define success metrics per email.** Welcome: opens and click-through to a setup page. Educate: scroll depth on linked article or video completion. Prove: clicks to case-study page. Nudge: clicks to the conversion endpoint. Exit: opt-down to a lower-frequency list, not opt-out. The sequence's aggregate metrics include conversion-event completion rate, time to conversion, and downstream retention (the conversion event is a leading indicator; retention is the lagging one).
21. **Address deliverability at the copy level.** Avoid all-caps subjects, excessive exclamation, currency symbols in the subject for re-engagement (re-engagement traffic to a cold address can trip spam filters), and image-only emails. Aim for a healthy text-to-image ratio and a recognizable from-name. Engineering owns SPF/DKIM/DMARC; copy owns the rest of the deliverability picture.
22. **Provide A/B test slots.** Per sequence, recommend two A/B tests: the subject line on the highest-volume email (typically email 1 or 2) and the CTA copy on the nudge email. Avoid testing more than two variables at once; isolate the lesson.
23. **Make the personalization tokens explicit.** Use double-brace placeholders (`{{first_name}}`, `{{company_name}}`, `{{trial_days_remaining}}`) with a documented fallback for each (`{{first_name|"there"}}`). Personalization that fails ("Hi ,") undermines the credibility of the whole sequence. Document the fallbacks in the plan so engineering implements them.
24. **Localize-friendly choices in copy.** Avoid idioms that do not translate, avoid US-only references unless the audience is US-only, and keep dates in unambiguous formats. If the audience is multinational, the skill flags candidate strings that will not translate cleanly and offers neutral alternatives.
25. **Return two artifacts.** First, the sequence plan — a table or list with one row per email containing touch number, day offset, job, subject-line variant recommended, preheader, primary CTA, exit conditions, and success metric. Second, the email drafts — one section per email with all subject-line variants, preheader, body, CTA button copy, footer block, and personalization-token table. The plan goes to the lifecycle ops owner; the drafts go to the copywriter or designer for finishing.

## Inputs

- `audience` — segment, entry point, expected topic.
- `objective` — onboarding, nurture, re-engagement, launch, education, winback.
- `product` — what the audience will activate, buy, return for, or learn.
- `sequence_length` (optional) — typically 5–7.
- `cadence` (optional) — aggressive, standard, slow.
- `voice` (optional) — direct, warm, technical, playful, formal.
- `signals` (optional) — what the marketing-automation platform can detect for branching.

## Outputs

- A sequence plan with timing, jobs, exit conditions, and success metrics per touch.
- Full email drafts with subject-line variants, preheader, body, CTA, footer, and personalization-token tables.

Both outputs are markdown. The drafts use `{{token|fallback}}` notation for personalization, ready for the marketer to map to their platform.

## Examples

**Example 1: Seven-email onboarding for a free-tier analytics tool.**

*Input* — audience: new free-tier signups, primarily product managers; objective: onboarding; conversion event: created first dashboard and shared it with one teammate; cadence: standard.

*Plan sketch* — Day 0 (welcome, set the contract): "What to do in your first ten minutes." Day 1 (educate): "The first dashboard most teams build." Day 3 (prove): "How a product team at [Customer] uses [Product] every Monday." Day 5 (educate): "Three queries you didn't know you needed." Day 8 (nudge): "You're one teammate away from getting real value." Day 12 (prove): "What your second week usually looks like." Day 18 (exit): "I'll stop emailing for now — here is the cheat sheet." Exit conditions: suppress if conversion event fires; pause if three consecutive non-opens. Branching: if user activates after day 3, skip emails 4-5 and jump to a separate "team expansion" sequence.

**Example 2: Five-email re-engagement for trial users who lapsed.**

*Input* — audience: trial users with no activity in 30 days; objective: re-engagement; conversion event: return visit and reach the previously-stuck step.

*Plan sketch* — Day 0: subject "is this still on your list?" — short, no ask, single link back to the dashboard. Day 3: educate touch — "the thing most people get stuck on at your step" — with a 60-second video. Day 7: prove — a one-paragraph customer story from a similar segment. Day 12: nudge — explicit ask "want a 15-minute call?" with a calendly link. Day 18: exit — graceful close with a "here is the link if you ever want to come back" and a preferences-page link to pause email entirely. Subject-line variants for email 4 ("want a 15-minute call?" vs. "stuck on the same step? we made a fix.") — the latter outperforms when the audience is technical.

**Example 3: Five-touch launch sequence for a new pricing tier.**

*Input* — audience: existing customers on the legacy plan; objective: launch; conversion event: upgrade or schedule upgrade call.

*Plan sketch* — Day -3 (tease): "Something is changing about pricing — and you'll like it." Day 0 (announce): "Introducing [Tier] — what's new, what stays the same, what to do." Day 2 (deep dive): "How [Tier] changes the cost of [pain point]." Day 5 (prove): "What three customers did in week one." Day 9 (last call): "Locking in legacy pricing closes Friday — here's the link." Personalization tokens include current plan, current MRR bucket, and renewal month so the copy can adjust the urgency line per cohort.

## Limitations

- The skill does not have access to send-time data; recommended day offsets are conventions, not optimized for a specific list. Re-tune after measuring opens by hour for the audience.
- The skill cannot directly integrate with marketing-automation platforms; the output is platform-agnostic markdown that requires translation into Customer.io, HubSpot, Marketo, Iterable, Klaviyo, ActiveCampaign, or similar.
- Deliverability depends on factors outside the copy (domain reputation, list hygiene, DNS records); the skill follows copy-level best practices but cannot guarantee inbox placement.
- The skill assumes consent has been collected appropriately under GDPR, CASL, CAN-SPAM, and similar regimes. It does not validate consent records.
- Heavy industry-regulated copy (healthcare, financial services, gambling) requires legal review for claim language; the skill flags but does not vet.
- The skill produces English-language copy with localization-aware choices but does not perform localization. Multilingual sequences require a translation and cultural-review pass.
- Subject-line variant performance prediction is hypothesis-grade, not data-grounded; treat A/B recommendations as starting hypotheses and let your traffic confirm.
