# Privacy Impact Assessment (PIA / DPIA)

> **Important — not legal advice.** This skill produces a structured privacy impact assessment that supports — but does not replace — legal analysis by a qualified Data Protection Officer, privacy counsel, or equivalent. Whether a DPIA is mandatory under GDPR Article 35 (or the analogous obligation under other regimes), whether a specific lawful basis is available, whether a transfer mechanism is adequate, and whether the residual risk is acceptable are decisions that require legal judgment in context. Where the assessment concludes that "prior consultation" with a supervisory authority is required, that consultation is itself a regulated procedure with strict content requirements that this skill does not implement. Use the output as a working draft for your DPO and counsel to review, refine, and sign off on. Do not rely on this output to demonstrate compliance to a regulator without independent legal review.

## When to use

The right moment to start a privacy impact assessment is the earliest moment at which the processing is concrete enough to describe. In practice that is the design review for a new feature, the discovery stage for a new product, the procurement stage for a new vendor that will process personal data, the integration planning for a new analytics or marketing technology, or the introduction of a new AI/ML capability that will see personal data either at inference or at training time. The principle in the regime is "privacy by design and by default" — the assessment is a forcing function for that principle, not a paperwork exercise that follows once the architecture is frozen.

Use this skill specifically when any of the following apply: the processing involves systematic large-scale monitoring of a publicly accessible area; it involves systematic large-scale processing of special-category data or data relating to criminal convictions and offences; it involves automated decision-making with legal or similarly significant effects on the data subject; it involves the use of a new technology applied to personal data (most AI deployments qualify under most current regulator guidance); the data subjects are children, employees in monitored work contexts, or other vulnerable populations; or the processing involves cross-border transfers to jurisdictions without adequacy decisions. Many supervisory authorities publish lists of operations that require a DPIA; consult your authority's list as the authoritative source.

Also use this skill for the inverse case — a threshold analysis that concludes a full DPIA is not required. The threshold analysis is itself a documented decision and counts as evidence of accountability. A regulator that asks "did you consider whether a DPIA was needed?" will accept "yes, here is the documented threshold analysis showing why we concluded it was not" more readily than silence.

Do not use this skill in place of a security risk assessment when the question is purely about technical controls (network segmentation, key management, vulnerability handling); those analyses overlap with a PIA but are not substitutes for it. Do not use this skill to retrofit legitimacy onto a decision already made; a PIA whose conclusion is foregone is not a PIA, and a regulator will read it as such. If the design is already deployed and you suspect the answer is uncomfortable, run the assessment honestly and let the residual-risk decision route to the right level of authority.

## How to apply

A defensible PIA proceeds in stages and produces a document that another reasonable privacy professional could read, follow, and sign. The sections below correspond to the sections of the output document; produce them in order.

### 1. Threshold analysis: is a full DPIA required?

Open with a short analysis that decides whether the processing triggers a mandatory DPIA under the controlling regime. Under GDPR, Article 35(3) lists explicit triggers (systematic and extensive evaluation of personal aspects based on automated processing with legal effects; large-scale processing of special categories; systematic monitoring of publicly accessible areas) and supervisory authorities publish "must-DPIA" lists. Under UK GDPR the structure mirrors the EU regime. Under HIPAA the analog is a risk analysis under the Security Rule (45 CFR 164.308(a)(1)(ii)(A)). Under LGPD a Relatório de Impacto à Proteção de Dados Pessoais (RIPD) is required in similar high-risk circumstances.

State the conclusion as one of: "DPIA required by law," "DPIA prudent though not mandatory," or "Threshold not met; lightweight assessment only." Record the basis. If the conclusion is the third, the document still proceeds — but with proportionate depth.

### 2. Describe the processing

Describe the processing in plain prose: the purposes, the categories of data subjects, the categories of personal data, the recipients, the retention periods, the data flows from collection through deletion, and the technologies in use. Be specific. "We will collect device identifiers" is weaker than "we will collect a hashed device identifier derived from a browser-fingerprint algorithm at signup, stored in PostgreSQL for thirteen months, and shared with our analytics processor under SCCs." Specificity is the diagnostic of a well-run assessment.

Include a small diagram in words: source of data → controller storage → processors → onward transfers → retention end → deletion verification. The reader should be able to follow the flow without consulting any other document.

### 3. Necessity and proportionality

Two distinct questions. First, is the processing necessary for the stated purpose? "Necessary" in regulatory practice is closer to "reasonably the least intrusive way to achieve the legitimate aim" than to "absolutely required." A purpose that could be served by a non-personal-data approach (aggregated metrics, on-device computation, ephemeral processing) cannot justify collecting personal data merely because it is convenient. The agent should suggest specific data-minimization alternatives the design team should consider, even if some are ultimately rejected; rejection on the record is itself useful documentation.

Second, is the processing proportionate to the purpose? A purpose of "improving user experience" rarely justifies indefinite retention of behavior across the entire web. The proportionality analysis weighs the intrusiveness of the processing against the value of the outcome and asks whether the design intervenes at the minimum necessary level.

Identify and record the lawful basis for processing under the controlling regime. Under GDPR Article 6 the options are consent, contractual necessity, legal obligation, vital interests, public interest, and legitimate interests. If consent is the basis, confirm that the consent is freely given, specific, informed, unambiguous, and as easy to withdraw as to give; consent that does not meet these criteria is not a basis at all. If legitimate interests is the basis, run an LIA (legitimate interests assessment) and record the balancing. For special-category data under Article 9, a second basis from a more restricted list is required. For children's data, the regime imposes elevated requirements that should be called out explicitly.

### 4. Risks to data subjects

Enumerate the risks specific to the data subjects (not to the controller's reputation or revenue — those are secondary to a DPIA's purpose). The agent should think along several axes: confidentiality risks (unauthorized access, breach), integrity risks (incorrect data driving incorrect decisions), availability risks (loss of access to one's own data), discrimination and unfair treatment risks (especially from automated decision-making and profiling), chilling-effect risks (where processing inhibits the data subject from exercising other rights or behaviors), and second-order risks where data exposed in this processing combines with other data sets to enable more invasive analysis.

For each risk, estimate the likelihood and the severity from the data subject's point of view. The combined risk level (high/medium/low or a numeric score) drives the mitigation plan and the residual-risk decision. A useful test for severity is the "regret test" — if this risk materializes for a single data subject, what is the worst plausible outcome for that person? An employee monitoring tool that leaks could end a career. A health symptom tracker that leaks could prejudice insurance. A children's app that leaks could enable targeted exploitation. Calibrate severity to these stakes rather than to abstract data categories.

### 5. Cross-border transfers and special factors

For each onward transfer to a jurisdiction outside the data subject's regime, identify the transfer mechanism and document the Transfer Impact Assessment (where required by GDPR or UK GDPR). The TIA is not optional in the EU regime and is one of the most common gaps a regulator finds. Cover: the destination country and the relevant laws affecting government access, the SCCs or other instrument in use, the supplementary technical and contractual measures (encryption with keys not held by the importer, pseudonymization, audit rights), and the conclusion on whether the transfer can proceed.

Document the special factors: presence of children, special categories, automated decision-making with significant effects, novel technologies (this includes most modern AI deployments), and any factor that puts the processing on the supervisory authority's must-DPIA list. Each special factor elevates the residual-risk threshold for approval.

### 6. Mitigations and design changes

For each identified risk, propose mitigations — data minimization changes, retention reductions, pseudonymization, encryption with key separation, access controls, audit logging, employee training, processor selection, opt-out flows, in-product transparency layers, model design changes for AI systems, human-in-the-loop checkpoints for automated decisions, and any other measure that reduces likelihood or severity. State the residual risk after mitigation, not the gross risk; the assessment is about what is left after the controls.

The mitigation plan should be actionable. Each mitigation has an owner, a target date, and a verification method. Mitigations that are "we will be careful" are not mitigations.

### 7. Consultation

Document who was consulted in producing the assessment: the DPO (mandatory for many DPIAs under GDPR), product and engineering owners, security, legal, customer-facing teams, and where relevant the data subjects themselves or their representatives. Where the residual risk remains high after mitigation, GDPR Article 36 requires prior consultation with the supervisory authority before the processing begins; record whether prior consultation is needed and route accordingly.

### 8. Residual risk decision and sign-off

State the residual risk in one short paragraph and a category (acceptable, acceptable-with-conditions, not acceptable). State who has authority to accept the residual risk; for material risks this is generally an executive plus the DPO, not the engineering manager who built the feature. List the conditions of approval where any. List the review cadence — annually, on material change, or on a trigger such as a regulator inquiry or breach.

Sign-off should be specific names and dates, not "the team." If the regime requires it, prior consultation with the supervisory authority must precede launch, not follow it.

## Inputs

The skill expects a plain-language description of the processing and benefits substantially from organization context and any known special factors. If the controlling regime is ambiguous, the agent should call out the ambiguity and produce the assessment against the most stringent applicable regime, with notes on where other regimes diverge.

## Outputs

The Markdown report is a complete DPIA document suitable for internal sign-off and for production on regulator request. The JSON output is suitable for piping into a privacy program register and includes the threshold outcome, regimes in scope, lawful bases, retention summary, transfers, risks with severity and mitigation, residual risk, whether prior consultation is required, who needs to sign off, and the review cadence.

## Examples

A product team is building a feature that lets a manager view employees' calendar density to plan capacity. The skill runs the threshold analysis (employee monitoring + systematic processing → DPIA prudent), describes the processing, runs the necessity test (the same outcome could be achieved with aggregated team-level data — propose that as an alternative), identifies discrimination and chilling-effect risks from individual visibility, recommends granularity reduction and a consultation with the works council, and concludes acceptable-with-conditions with three mitigations and a six-month review.

A team plans to use a third-party LLM to summarize customer support transcripts. The skill flags novel technology + special factors, requires the threshold conclusion to default to DPIA, describes the processing including prompt-and-response handling, identifies that the inference provider's training-data retention default is in scope and requires contractual prohibition, runs the transfer impact assessment for the inference provider's region, proposes mitigations including pre-processing redaction, residual-risk category acceptable-with-conditions, and a list of contract amendments to demand before launch.

## Limitations

This skill produces a structured assessment based on the inputs you supply. It cannot determine whether your supervisory authority's published guidance imposes additional requirements specific to your sector, cannot perform the technical security review that complements a PIA, and cannot substitute for the DPO sign-off that many regimes require. It does not draft the prior-consultation submission to a supervisory authority where that procedure is triggered; that submission has specific format requirements published by each authority. Where the conclusion is "DPIA required by law," route to qualified counsel before relying on this output as the final document.
