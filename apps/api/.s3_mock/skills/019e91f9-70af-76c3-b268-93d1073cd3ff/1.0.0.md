## When to use

Use this skill when the team's vendor diligence process is either too heavy for everyone or too light for the riskiest few — usually both at once. The pathology is familiar: a 60-question security questionnaire is sent to every vendor including the office-snack supplier, the questionnaire-recipients take six weeks to respond, the security team takes another month to read the answers, the procurement queue backs up, and meanwhile the critical SaaS vendor that processes customer PII gets through on a single page because the executive in question knew the salesperson. The mismatched diligence depth is the symptom; missing tiering is the cause.

The skill produces a tiering framework — four tiers with clear criteria for assignment — plus a tier-by-tier prescription for what diligence depth, review cadence, and exit-readiness expectation applies. It is appropriate for any organization that has more than about 20 active vendors, or any organization that operates in a regulated industry where vendor-risk programs are an audit concern. It is not the right tool for a single-vendor decision (use the scorecard for that) and it is not a substitute for the team's GRC or third-party-risk-management platform — the framework is what gets configured into that platform, not a replacement for it.

The skill is opinionated about proportionality. The dominant failure mode of vendor-risk programs is treating risk as binary (vendor is risky / vendor is not) and applying uniform diligence to a heterogeneous vendor population. Tiering breaks that — explicitly trades depth for cycle time on lower-tier vendors, and explicitly invests depth on higher-tier ones. The team's total diligence capacity is finite; tiering allocates it where the marginal return is highest.

## How to apply

1. **Identify the three risk axes that drive tiering: data, criticality, dependency.** Vendor risk is not one number; it is three. Most failed tiering frameworks try to collapse into one composite score and lose the structure. Keep the axes separate:
   - **Data sensitivity** — what classification of data the vendor accesses, stores, or processes. PII, PHI, financial, trade secret, customer-confidential, public-only.
   - **Operational criticality** — what happens if the vendor is unavailable for a day, a week, a month. Does the team's product break? Does revenue recognition break? Is it an inconvenience?
   - **Financial / strategic dependency** — what fraction of the team's operations or revenue runs through this vendor; how hard would replacement be; is the team's product effectively co-branded with the vendor's capability.
   Two axes can be high without the third — a vendor that processes a small set of highly-sensitive records is data-high but criticality-low. A vendor that runs the team's primary cloud is criticality-high and dependency-high but data-medium for some workloads. Score each axis independently before computing a tier.

2. **Score each axis on a simple 1–4 scale with concrete descriptors.** Avoid finer granularity. For data sensitivity:
   - **4 — Regulated sensitive.** Vendor processes regulated data (PHI under HIPAA, PCI cardholder data, personal data triggering GDPR special-category rules, classified information).
   - **3 — Customer or employee personal data, or material business confidential.** Customer PII, employee records, board materials, M&A documents, source code.
   - **2 — Internal-confidential business data.** Pricing, contracts, internal communications, customer lists at a name-only level.
   - **1 — Public-only or no customer data.** Vendor handles no team data, or only data the team would publish.
   Equivalent descriptors for criticality (4 = product breaks within hours; 1 = nice-to-have) and dependency (4 = strategic anchor with no realistic substitute in 12 months; 1 = swap-out is a one-week project).

3. **Combine the three scores into four tiers using a max-and-floor rule, not an average.** Averaging hides the highest-axis score, which is the one that drives diligence. Rule:
   - **Tier 1 (Critical)** — any axis score of 4, OR any two axes scoring 3 or higher.
   - **Tier 2 (High)** — any axis score of 3 not already in Tier 1.
   - **Tier 3 (Standard)** — any axis score of 2, with all others ≤ 2.
   - **Tier 4 (Low)** — all axes ≤ 1.
   This rule means a vendor that handles regulated data lands in Tier 1 even if criticality and dependency are low. Conversely, a critical-but-no-data vendor (e.g., a payments routing partner with no PII access) lands in Tier 2 unless dependency is also high. This is intentional: data sensitivity and operational criticality are different risk shapes and the program treats them differently.

4. **Define what each tier means for diligence, in concrete artifacts.** A tier without consequence is a label. For each tier specify:
   - **Pre-contract diligence required** — what artifacts the team collects and reviews before signing.
   - **Approval level required** — who signs off (procurement only, security review, executive sponsor, board notification).
   - **Ongoing review cadence** — how often the vendor is re-assessed during the contract.
   - **Exit-readiness expectation** — what the team has prepared if this vendor needs to be replaced.
   - **Monitoring level** — what continuous monitoring (vendor security ratings, SLA reporting, financial-health watch) is in place.
   Without these prescriptions, the tiering exercise is filing.

5. **Tier 4 (Low) — proportionate diligence is almost none.** A vendor that handles no team data, fails would not affect operations, and is easily replaced should clear with a one-page intake: legal entity, payment terms, basic insurance certificate. No security questionnaire. No annual review. Approval at the buying team's level. Examples: office supplies, branded swag, the catering service. Allowing fast clearance on Tier 4 protects the team's capacity for the cases that matter and earns goodwill with the rest of the business that has been waiting six weeks for low-stakes vendors.

6. **Tier 3 (Standard) — light security review, annual touch.** A vendor with internal-confidential data exposure but no regulated data and modest criticality clears with: short security questionnaire (15–25 items, no full SIG/CAIQ), evidence of a SOC 2 Type 2 (or equivalent) but without deep evidence review, standard contract terms (DPA if applicable), procurement-team approval, annual recheck of SOC 2 currency. Most software vendors land here. The questionnaire is bounded so a vendor can complete it in under two hours.

7. **Tier 2 (High) — deep diligence, security review, semi-annual touch.** A vendor with material customer data exposure or operational criticality clears with: full security questionnaire mapped to a recognized framework (CAIQ subset or SIG-Lite), evidence review (SOC 2 Type 2 read in detail, including reference to the auditor and the dates), pen test summary or attestation, named security owner at the vendor, signed DPA without redlines on critical clauses, semi-annual operational review (SLA performance, incident history, financial signal). Approval by named security lead plus procurement. Exit posture: documented "what we would do if this vendor failed for two weeks" — even a one-page plan changes the conversation when it has to happen.

8. **Tier 1 (Critical) — deep diligence, security and executive review, quarterly touch, exit drilled.** The dozen-to-twenty vendors that, between them, hold the team's regulated data or sit on the critical operational path. Full SIG questionnaire, on-site or virtual audit when available, contractual audit-right exercised at least once per multi-year term, named executive sponsor, quarterly business review with the vendor including SLA review and roadmap alignment, financial-health monitoring (public filings or private signal), concentration-risk tracking (this vendor and competitors' market share), documented exit plan with named alternative and an estimated transition timeline. Approval involves executive sponsor sign-off; board notification if the vendor is in the team's published "critical-vendors" disclosure. The work is real; the cost is justified because the consequence of failure is also real.

9. **Build the assignment intake as a five-minute form, not a forty-minute survey.** The fastest way to kill the tiering framework is to make intake heavier than the diligence. The intake form has at most ten questions, framed to land the vendor on the right tier:
   - What service does the vendor provide? (one line)
   - Will the vendor have access to customer PII, PHI, PCI data, or financial data? (yes/no — and if yes, which)
   - What systems will they integrate with? (multi-select)
   - What is the expected annual spend? (range)
   - If this vendor were unavailable for one week, what would the impact be? (three-option dropdown)
   - Is the vendor a sole-source or are there 3+ realistic alternatives? (single-select)
   - Who at the buying team will be the operational owner? (named person)
   - Is this vendor going to be on a customer-facing critical path? (yes/no)
   That set of questions resolves about 80% of vendors to a tier automatically. The remaining 20% — typically novel or borderline cases — get a 30-minute conversation rather than a 40-question form for everyone.

10. **State the inheritance rule for vendors of vendors (sub-processors).** A team's vendor often relies on its own vendors. The framework's position should be explicit: the vendor's sub-processors inherit the team's tier of that vendor for purposes of data flow, but the team relies on the vendor to manage its own sub-processor diligence (via contractual flow-down clauses). This is the only practical position for organizations that cannot directly diligence the long tail of sub-processors, but the team must demand: a documented list of sub-processors, contractual notification of new sub-processors, and the right to object. Without those three, the inheritance rule is not credible.

11. **Define the de-tiering and re-tiering triggers.** Tiers are not permanent. A vendor that started at Tier 3 may move to Tier 1 if the team starts sending them PII it did not originally send. A Tier 1 vendor may move down if the team replaces critical functions internally. The framework names the triggers: a contract amendment that changes data classifications, a measurable change in the vendor's financial health, a publicly-disclosed breach, a usage expansion that changes the criticality calculation. Each trigger fires a re-tier review. Without triggers, the tier assignments calcify and become misleading by year three.

12. **Build the critical-vendor register and review it quarterly with leadership.** Tier 1 vendors get a register: name, service, primary contact at the vendor, executive sponsor on the team, contract end date, last reviewed date, current monitoring signal, named exit plan owner, last exit-drill date. The register is not a spreadsheet for the security team's eyes only; it goes to the COO or CTO quarterly for a 30-minute review. The cadence prevents Tier 1 vendors from becoming invisible and forces the team to keep exit plans current.

### Standard tiering framework layout

The output document uses these sections in this order:

1. `# Vendor Risk Tiering Framework`
2. `## Purpose and scope` — which vendor types are in scope, which are excluded (e.g., government-mandated vendors, parent-company arrangements).
3. `## Risk axes and scoring` — the three axes with 1–4 descriptors.
4. `## Tier assignment rule` — the max-and-floor combination.
5. `## Tier 1 (Critical) — diligence prescription` — pre-contract, approval, cadence, monitoring, exit.
6. `## Tier 2 (High) — diligence prescription`
7. `## Tier 3 (Standard) — diligence prescription`
8. `## Tier 4 (Low) — diligence prescription`
9. `## Vendor intake form` — the 10-question form that resolves the tier.
10. `## Inheritance: vendor of vendors` — the sub-processor rule.
11. `## Re-tiering triggers` — events that fire a re-assessment.
12. `## Critical-vendor register template` — fields, owners, cadence of leadership review.
13. `## Examples` — five sample vendors with their tier assignments and reasoning.

### Composition rules

- **The framework is reproducible.** Anyone reading a vendor's intake should arrive at the same tier; assignment is rule-driven, not intuition-driven.
- **Diligence prescriptions are concrete artifacts.** "Security review" is not a prescription; "completed SIG-Lite questionnaire and a current SOC 2 Type 2 reviewed by the security lead within 30 days of contract" is.
- **Cadences are dates, not feelings.** "Annual review on the anniversary of contract signature; reminder fires 60 days ahead in the GRC tool."
- **Every tier has an exit-readiness expectation.** Tier 4 is "none required." Tier 1 is "named alternative plus drilled transition plan within last 18 months." Cadence and rigor scale by tier.
- **The framework integrates with the team's existing GRC or TPRM tool.** The skill produces the policy and prescriptions; the tool stores the records.
- **The framework is reviewed annually.** Vendor categories change shape over time; the framework that worked in 2023 may need adjustment by 2026 (regulatory shifts, the rise of new sensitive data types, new vendor business models).

## Inputs

- **Vendor description (required, text).** What the vendor does, what data they touch, what they integrate with, how critical they are.
- **Organization risk appetite (optional, text).** Regulated-industry constraints, customer commitments, board-mandated controls. The skill uses this to calibrate where to draw tier lines.
- **Existing program (optional, text).** Current intake and questionnaire, so the framework integrates rather than replaces.
- **Vendor inventory (optional, text).** A batch list for bulk tiering. The skill returns a tier and a one-line rationale per vendor.

## Outputs

A reusable tiering framework document with all four tier prescriptions; a per-vendor tier-assignment table when an inventory is provided; and a critical-vendor register template for the Tier 1 cohort. The framework is the artifact the team configures into their TPRM tool; the assignment table and register are the operational state.

## Limitations

- The framework is a starting point; specific regulatory regimes (HIPAA, PCI, FedRAMP, FFIEC, NYDFS) prescribe specific diligence elements that override general tiering. The skill outputs a general framework that the team adapts under the lawyer's and the compliance lead's guidance for the regimes that apply.
- Tier assignments are judgment calls at the margin. A vendor scored 3 on data and 2 on others is solidly Tier 2, but a vendor scored 3-3-1 is Tier 1 under the rule — which can feel heavy. The framework chooses the conservative reading because near-miss errors at the boundary are cheaper than scoring something Tier 2 that should have been Tier 1. Teams may calibrate the rule with explicit reasoning, but uniform downgrading defeats the framework.
- The skill does not produce the security questionnaire content. It points to the framework (SIG, CAIQ, ISO 27001 mappings) and prescribes which depth is appropriate per tier; the actual question text comes from those source bodies or from the team's existing program.
- The framework is opinion-bearing on inheritance. The view that sub-processor diligence is the vendor's responsibility (managed via contractual flow-down) is the practical norm; some regulated environments require direct diligence on named sub-processors, and the team should adjust accordingly.
- Re-tiering is the most common failure mode of a tiering program. Vendors get assigned once at intake and never reviewed even when usage or data flow changes substantially. The skill prescribes triggers, but operationalizing them requires a tool — typically the GRC platform — that detects the trigger conditions. Without that, manual quarterly sweeps are the backup.
- Concentration risk and the "many small vendors who together do something critical" pattern is not directly addressed by per-vendor tiering. A separate concentration analysis is required: which functions or data classes are held across many Tier-3 vendors that collectively constitute a critical exposure. That analysis is its own work and not produced here.
