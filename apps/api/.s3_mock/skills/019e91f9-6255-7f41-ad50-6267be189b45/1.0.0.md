# AI Video Post Integration Pipeline

## When to use

Use this skill when AI-generated clips are about to enter a finishing pipeline alongside (or instead of) traditionally-acquired footage. The skill produces the ordered pipeline plan — ingest, conform, uprez and interpolate, deflicker, grade, composite, audio, deliver — with the rationale for each step and the conditions that make a step skippable. It is the right skill for any commercial spot, music video, social piece, narrative short, or documentary insert that combines AI plates with live-action footage, or that has to be delivered to a higher spec than the AI model produces natively.

This skill produces methodology guidance for AI video generation. The skill does not address copyright status of training data or generated outputs in any jurisdiction; consult counsel for commercial use. The skill explicitly does not produce or recommend non-consensual likeness use (deepfakes), CSAM, defamatory content, or content that violates platform policy or applicable law. The skill names finishing software families generically. Specific tool choices depend on the studio's existing stack.

The skill complements the shot architect (which designs the plates) and the consistency architect (which keeps the plates feeling like one piece). It is the layer that turns the plates into the final deliverable.

## How to apply

The skill walks eight pipeline stages in order. Each stage names the chosen approach, the alternatives, and the conditions for skipping.

### Stage 1 — Ingest, conform, and a clean source library

1. AI-generated clips arrive in formats that vary by model — common output is 24 or 30 fps, 1024-by-576 to 1920-by-1080, an h264 mp4 with rec709 colour. Live-action clips arrive with their own metadata, often log-encoded.
2. Conform every clip into a single working format before any finishing work begins. The skill's default working format: ProRes 422 HQ, 24 fps, the project's working colour space (typically ACEScct for high-end work, rec709 for social pieces), with a consistent file-naming convention that encodes shot number, take number, and AI-vs-live-action provenance.
3. Decoding and re-encoding the AI clips lifts them out of the lossy delivery codec and into a finishing-grade container. The single re-encode at ingest is cheap; round-tripping through h264 at every step compounds artefacts.
4. Provenance metadata travels with each clip in the file name and in a sidecar that the finishing project reads. The C2PA disclosure skill picks up this metadata at delivery.

### Stage 2 — Uprez to delivery resolution

1. AI clips at 1024-by-576 or 1280-by-720 do not survive a 4K delivery without uprez. Three families of approach.
2. **Diffusion uprez.** Run the clip through a video-aware diffusion upscaler. Strong on natural textures, can introduce hallucinated detail (extra eyelashes, extra stitches in fabric, extra blades of grass). Best for B-roll and atmospheric shots; weaker on hero faces because hallucinated detail on a face reads as identity drift.
3. **Optical-flow uprez.** Older and more conservative. Lower hallucinated-detail risk; softer output. Pair with a light sharpening pass after.
4. **Mixed-strategy uprez.** Mask the hero subject and process the subject with the more conservative uprez, process the background with the more aggressive uprez. The composite goes back together in the grade. Most expensive, highest quality on hero shots.
5. The uprez factor matters. A 2x uprez from 1080p to 4K is comfortable; a 4x uprez from 540p to 4K is asking for trouble. Where the brief calls for a 4x uprez, regenerate the plate at higher resolution if the model supports it before reaching for uprez.

### Stage 3 — Frame-rate conform and interpolation

1. AI clips often render at 24 fps; delivery may demand 25, 30, 50, 60, or higher. Three useful tactics.
2. **No interpolation.** When source and delivery match, no work is needed. The pipeline plan documents this and moves on.
3. **Optical-flow interpolation.** Generate intermediate frames by warping existing frames along optical flow vectors. Reliable for smooth motion; struggles on occlusions, motion blur, and rapid limb motion. The standard workhorse for 24-to-60 fps conforms.
4. **Diffusion interpolation.** Use a video-aware diffusion model to synthesize the intermediate frames. Stronger on rapid motion and occlusions; expensive in compute. Reserve for hero shots where optical-flow interpolation visibly breaks.
5. Interpolation runs after uprez, not before. Uprezzing the lower-frame-rate clip costs less than uprezzing the interpolated higher-frame-rate clip, and interpolation off uprezzed source produces cleaner motion than interpolation off low-res source then uprezzing the interpolated frames.

### Stage 4 — Temporal-flicker repair

1. Temporal flicker is the dominant artefact on AI-generated video — small changes in brightness, hue, or fine texture across consecutive frames that the model could not lock down. Three useful repairs.
2. **Temporal denoise.** A motion-compensated denoiser that averages across frames removes high-frequency flicker. Cheap; first reach. Risks softening the image.
3. **Per-channel temporal stabilisation.** Track regions of the image and stabilise their per-channel mean and contrast across time. Good for grade-drift flicker (the orange chair flickers slightly orange-to-red); weaker on geometry flicker.
4. **Targeted regeneration.** When a region flickers because the model fundamentally couldn't lock the structure, regenerate the shot with a different seed, a slightly different prompt, or a different conditioning input. The drift audit from the consistency skill marks shots that need this; the post pipeline does not paper over them.
5. Run deflicker before grade, not after. Flicker that survives the grade is harder to fix because the grade has compressed the contrast range the deflicker needs to operate in.

### Stage 5 — Grade-match across AI and live-action

1. AI clips and live-action clips will not match out of the box. The grade is where they become one piece. The pipeline plan covers three layers.
2. **Technical match.** Normalise every clip to the working colour space at ingest (Stage 1). Without this, any creative grade is fighting noise.
3. **Hero look development.** Develop the look on a hero live-action clip first — the look that defines the piece. A look is the contrast curve, the colour balance, the saturation, and the texture treatment (film emulation, grain, halation).
4. **AI clip conform to the hero look.** Apply the hero look to every AI clip and then make per-shot adjustments. AI clips often render flatter than live action and over-saturated in midtones; bring contrast up and pull midtone saturation back as the starting adjustment. AI clips often have a slightly different black point than live action; match the black point before matching anything else.
5. **Skin tones, neutrals, and signature props.** A skin-tone vectorscope reading on every AI shot with a person plus every live-action shot with the same person makes drift visible. A neutral grey on a product shot (a wall, a tabletop) is a reference for the white balance. Signature props (the company's product, the show's hero prop) deserve a dedicated correction layer that locks the colour across every shot.
6. Film emulation and grain run last in the grade stack. Adding a film-emulation LUT before flicker repair will lock the flicker into the grade.

### Stage 6 — Mask, roto, and composite

1. Many AI clips need partial fixes — a face that drifted, a logo that the model hallucinated, a region that flickered, a background that needs to be swapped for the live-action plate. Three composite patterns.
2. **Region paint and patch.** For a small distracting artefact (a hallucinated logo, a sixth finger), mask the region in the editor or finishing tool and paint or patch from a clean frame elsewhere in the clip. The cheapest fix.
3. **Face mask and swap.** When a face has drifted on one shot but is correct on adjacent shots, rotoscope the face on the drifted shot and composite a face-swap from a good shot, or generate a corrected face plate and matte it in. The consistency skill flags the drift; this stage executes the swap.
4. **AI subject into live-action plate.** Generate the subject on a clean controllable background (solid colour, simple gradient), key the background out, and composite the subject onto the live-action plate. The shot architect skill notes when to commit to this pattern up front. The compositor's job is the edge — a refined matte, motion blur matched to the plate, an integration grade that puts the subject in the plate's light.
5. **Live-action subject into AI plate.** The inverse: shoot the subject on a green screen, key, and composite onto the AI-generated environment. The AI plate's lighting drives the integration grade applied to the keyed subject.
6. Every composite gets a final pass that matches the integration grade, the focus and motion blur, the noise and grain. A clean key that is sharper than the plate it sits on reads as fake; a clean key with the plate's grain and a touch of motion blur reads as real.

### Stage 7 — Audio design layered on top

1. AI-generated video is almost always silent or arrives with placeholder audio. The audio pass is the cheapest single improvement to the perceived quality of the piece.
2. **Sync sound design.** Foley for footsteps, fabric, hand contact, environment ambience tracked to the visible action. The audio anchors the visual motion that the AI plate alone does not credibly motivate.
3. **Music.** Score or licensed track that matches the energy of the cut. AI-generated music is one option; bear in mind the same copyright considerations as AI-generated video and consult counsel for commercial pieces.
4. **Voice.** Dialogue or voice-over recorded against the cut. AI voice generation is an option and carries the same consent and disclosure obligations as AI video — disclose if the voice is synthetic, secure consent if the voice clones a real person, and document the consent.
5. **Loudness.** Master to the delivery platform's loudness target (broadcast LUFS targets, social-platform loudness norms). The pipeline plan names the target for the chosen platform.

### Stage 8 — Delivery, export, and disclosure

1. Export from the working format to the delivery spec — codec, container, frame rate, colour space, loudness, captions, audio layout.
2. Render multiple deliverables when the brief calls for them (a 16:9 master, a 9:16 social cut, a square cut, a 6-second teaser). Build the alternative cuts off the finished master, not off the source clips.
3. Embed C2PA Content Credentials at delivery for any piece that contains AI-generated material. The content-policy-and-disclosure skill covers the manifest authoring; the pipeline plan records the trigger.
4. Archive the project at the working format level (a ProRes master, the project file, the source AI clips, the source live-action clips, the LUTs, the audio sessions) so a future re-finish does not have to start from a recompressed deliverable.
5. Captions and accessibility — subtitles, audio descriptions, sign-language inserts — are part of delivery for many platforms and are required by law for broadcast in many jurisdictions. The pipeline plan names whether captions are burned in (forced) or carried as a sidecar (selectable). For platform deliveries, sidecar captions are nearly always preferred.

### Stage 9 — QC pass and final review

1. The last stage is a structured quality-control pass that catches anything the per-stage work missed. The pass runs at delivery resolution, on a calibrated monitor, with the delivery-platform's audio loudness reference.
2. **Technical QC.** Resolution, frame rate, codec, container, colour space, loudness, captions, audio layout. Each spec is read off the master file and matched against the delivery spec line by line.
3. **Continuity QC.** Cut points, dissolve durations, hold lengths, end-card timing. Re-watch with continuity in mind; small jumps and missed cuts surface only on a focused pass.
4. **AI-specific QC.** A pass dedicated to artefacts the AI generation may have introduced and the earlier stages may have missed — extra fingers in a wide shot, hair clipping through clothing, text in the background that the model invented, eyes that briefly cross. The reviewer is looking for the "AI tell" that breaks the immersion.
5. **Disclosure QC.** The C2PA manifest is verified on the export. The on-screen disclosure (if present) is at the right placement, duration, and contrast. Platform-tagging is documented in the upload checklist.
6. QC findings go into a punch list ordered by severity. Severity 1 (must fix before delivery) and severity 2 (should fix before delivery) are addressed; severity 3 (note for next revision) is logged.

## Examples

A 30-second commercial spot mixes 12 AI-generated B-roll plates with three live-action interview shots. The AI plates come in at 1080p / 24fps and the delivery is 4K / 25fps for broadcast. The pipeline plan: ingest everything to ProRes 422 HQ at 25fps in rec709 working space; uprez the AI plates 2x with diffusion uprez on the backgrounds and optical-flow uprez on the few that contain hero subjects; interpolate from 24 to 25 with optical-flow (a one-frame nudge); a temporal-denoise pass for high-frequency flicker; grade development on the lead interview shot, hero look applied to all AI plates, skin-tone match between the interview shots; no composite work needed; sync sound design, original score, broadcast loudness master; export to ProRes for broadcast plus an h264 social cut; C2PA manifest authored at delivery.

A music video composites an AI-generated character into a live-action band plate. The pipeline plan: generate the character on a green screen background (decision routed from the shot architect); ingest, conform, uprez 1.5x; pull a clean key with a Keylight-class tool, refine the matte; grade-match the character's lighting to the live-action plate; add motion blur matched to the plate's shutter; integrate grain; sync sound design; delivery in the platform's preferred format. The pipeline plan flags that the character's edge needs a hand-roto pass on three shots where the key alone is not clean enough.

A wholly AI-generated 60-second piece delivers at 4K / 24fps with surround audio for a film-festival short. The pipeline plan: ingest at the highest fidelity the generation tool exports; minimal uprez; no interpolation (24fps source matches delivery); aggressive temporal-denoise pass to suppress flicker that is visible at theatrical scale; full grade developed from a reference look; minor mask repair on two shots flagged by the consistency drift audit; full audio design with score, foley, and ambience; 5.1 mix to theatrical loudness; ProRes 4444 master with embedded C2PA manifest; on-screen "AI-generated" credit at the head and tail. The pipeline plan calls out theatrical-scale review as the QC gate, because artefacts invisible at phone scale become visible at cinema scale.

## Tooling notes

The skill avoids prescribing a specific tool stack — finishing pipelines vary widely by studio, by region, and by the team's existing investments. The decisions in the pipeline map onto features in any modern finishing suite. A few notes on what to look for.

**Conform and ingest.** Any finishing tool with project-level format settings will do. The watch-out is to set the working format once and verify that every clip in the bin reads as the working format, not as the source format. Many tools default to interpreting source rather than converting it, and the resulting timeline has mixed-format clips that grade and composite badly.

**Uprez and interpolation.** Standalone AI uprez and interpolation tools exist; some finishing suites integrate them as plug-ins or native nodes. The choice depends on the project's volume and the team's GPU access. For small projects, a standalone tool with manual per-clip processing is fine. For high-volume work, a node-based integration that runs as part of the timeline is faster.

**Deflicker.** Modern motion-compensated deflickers are available as plug-ins in major finishing suites. Older deflickers (frame-blend, simple temporal blur) work but soften the image; a motion-compensated deflicker preserves more detail. Test on a representative clip before committing.

**Grade.** Any node-based or layer-based colour suite works. The working colour space is set once at project creation; the LUTs and the look-development clips live in a project-level shared library. The skin-tone reference and the signature-prop reference are calibration assets the team builds once.

**Mask and composite.** Roto, keying, planar tracking, and compositing nodes are standard in modern finishing suites. The face-swap and region-paint workflows benefit from AI-aware tools (segment-anything-style masks, generative fill for region paint) where available; manual rotoscoping still works and is the fallback.

**Audio.** Audio post lives in a dedicated audio workstation, with the picture cut imported as reference. The deliverable from audio post is a mix that conforms back to the picture timeline; the final master is rendered with the conformed audio in the finishing tool.

**C2PA signing.** Several tools support C2PA manifest creation. The signing is most often done at export time from the finishing tool; some teams sign as a separate step after master export. Either approach works as long as the signature covers the final deliverable bytes.

## When to revise the pipeline

The pipeline plan is not static. Three triggers that cause the plan to be revised mid-project.

**Trigger 1 — A shot fails QC late.** A shot that passes per-stage review may fail the Stage 9 QC pass. The cost of regeneration plus repeat finishing is real; the team decides whether to absorb the cost or to ship with the flaw documented. The pipeline plan records the decision and the rationale.

**Trigger 2 — A new artefact pattern emerges.** Mid-project, the team may discover that a specific artefact (a particular kind of flicker, a particular face drift) recurs across many shots. The pipeline plan is revised to address the pattern at the appropriate stage rather than per-shot — for instance, adding a global temporal denoise pre-process before grade rather than per-shot deflicker.

**Trigger 3 — Delivery spec changes.** Clients change delivery specs. The pipeline plan is recalculated from the new spec backwards. Most stages absorb the change cleanly; a frame-rate change forces re-interpolation, a resolution change forces re-uprez, a colour-space change forces re-grade. The archive at working format makes these changes tractable; teams archived at delivery format have to re-finish from source.

## Limitations

## Common pitfalls

**Pitfall 1 — Skipping conform.** Mixing source frame rates and colour spaces in the editor and trusting the editor's interpretation produces inconsistent results. Conform at ingest. Always.

**Pitfall 2 — Aggressive uprez on hero faces.** Diffusion uprezzers hallucinate detail. On hero faces, hallucinated detail reads as identity drift. The mixed-strategy uprez (Stage 2, tactic 3) exists for this reason; teams that single-pass everything end up with unrecognisable hero shots.

**Pitfall 3 — Interpolating low-res source then uprezzing.** The order is uprez first, interpolate second. Reversing the order locks in low-resolution motion artefacts that the higher frame rate then makes more visible.

**Pitfall 4 — Deflickering after grading.** The grade compresses contrast; deflickering after the grade has less signal to work with. Deflicker is Stage 4 for a reason.

**Pitfall 5 — Grade-matching by eye only.** Eyes drift; vectorscopes and waveform monitors do not. Match skin tones on the vectorscope, neutrals on the waveform, and signature colours with a colour-picker.

**Pitfall 6 — Composites without integration grade.** A clean key sitting on a graded plate without an integration grade reads as a sticker on a picture. The integration grade matches contrast, colour, focus, and grain.

**Pitfall 7 — Audio added last with no time budget.** Audio design is half the perceived quality of the piece. Teams that leave audio to the last day under-invest in foley and ambience and the piece feels thin. Audio gets a real budget and a real timeline.

**Pitfall 8 — Single-format delivery and a forgotten archive.** Delivering one h264 to a platform and shipping the project file alone makes a re-finish or a re-cut painful. Archive the master in finishing format with all source clips, project files, LUTs, and audio sessions.

**Pitfall 9 — Manifest authored as an afterthought.** The C2PA manifest captured at export records the AI involvement; if the team waits until after delivery to think about provenance, the chain of custody is broken and the manifest's value is diminished. The manifest is part of the delivery, not a postscript.

## Handing off to other skills

The post-integration pipeline is the last methodology step before delivery. Its handoffs are upstream.

**From the shot architect.** Per-shot duration, resolution, aspect ratio, and the accept-or-repair calls from Decision 8 of the shot architect feed the per-shot treatment table.

**From the consistency architect.** The drift audit's remediation list is direct input to Stage 6 (composite). Face-mask repairs, region paints, and grade-matching needs each map to specific operations in the post pipeline.

**From the content-policy-and-disclosure skill.** The C2PA manifest contents, the on-screen disclosure design and placement, and the platform-tagging requirements feed Stage 8.

The post pipeline does not feed downstream methodology skills directly; its output is the deliverable file or files. Records and archives feed a future re-finish or re-cut, which re-enters the workflow at Stage 1 with a working-format archive as source.

## Tuning the pipeline to project scale

The pipeline plan adapts to project scale. A few common scales and how the stages flex.

**Social-media short (15 seconds, vertical, single platform).** Stages 1, 4, 5, 7, 8 are mandatory. Uprez is often unnecessary (the platform's target resolution is moderate). Interpolation is rarely necessary at social-media frame rates. Composite work is shot-by-shot rather than systematic. Audio is dialogue or licensed track plus minimal foley. QC is a single review pass. Total turnaround can be hours.

**Brand commercial (30–60 seconds, multiple cuts for different platforms).** All nine stages run. Uprez and interpolation handle the broadcast or theatrical platform; the social cuts are downrezzed from the master. Composite work may be substantial. Audio is a full design with score, foley, ambience, and platform-loudness mastering. QC includes a technical review against the brand's deliverable spec.

**Narrative short or music video (3–10 minutes).** All nine stages, with extra time on Stage 5 (look development takes longer when the piece has its own visual identity) and Stage 6 (more composite repair is typical when more shots exist). Audio is a full design plus colour-grading review.

**Festival short or long-form (10+ minutes, theatrical).** All nine stages at high fidelity. Theatrical-scale review surfaces artefacts that lower-scale review missed. Audio is mixed in a calibrated mix stage. The C2PA manifest may include detailed edit history for evidentiary purposes.

The pipeline plan's first job is to scale the per-stage investment to the project. A 15-second social short does not need a theatrical-scale audio mix; a festival short does not survive a single-pass review.

## Limitations

The skill plans the pipeline; it does not execute it. Execution lives in the finishing software the team uses. The skill names tool families rather than specific products to avoid binding the plan to a stack the team does not own; the pipeline summary leaves the tool slot open for the studio's choice. The deflicker recommendations assume modern motion-compensated denoisers and per-channel stabilisers; older deflicker plug-ins may not produce comparable results and the team should verify on a test clip before committing. The audio guidance is high-level; for genre-specific audio design (sound for narrative, for documentary, for advertising), pair this skill with a dedicated audio-post practice.

## Documenting the AI source clips for the editor

Editors who finish AI-generated material benefit from richer source metadata than live-action editors typically receive. The pipeline plan specifies what travels with each clip into the edit.

**Prompt and conditioning record.** The prompt used to generate the clip and the conditioning inputs (reference image, reference video, LoRA, structure reference). The editor uses this to understand why the shot looks the way it does and to spot artefacts that originated in the prompt rather than in the model.

**Seed and sampler settings.** The numerical settings that produced the clip. Useful if a regeneration is required; the editor can hand back to the generation team with exact parameters rather than a vague "make another one".

**Generation pass history.** Which pass of the narrowing loop produced the clip, what was changed since the previous pass, what the team accepted versus repaired. The history lets the editor know what is already known to be fragile.

**Known artefacts.** A list of artefacts the generation team noticed but accepted — the sixth finger in a wide shot, the slight flicker in the foreground, the text on a sign that is illegible. The editor knows what to expect and can plan the cut to minimise their visibility.

The metadata travels in a sidecar file (often a JSON keyed by clip filename) and is loaded into the editor's project. Tools that support per-clip notes display the metadata directly; tools that do not are augmented with a separate reference document the editor reads alongside the project.

## Working colour space and the finishing chain

The choice of working colour space is the foundation of the grade. A brief note.

**rec709 working space.** Suitable for social-media-first pieces and quick-turn commercial work. The colour space matches the delivery for most digital platforms; less colour-management overhead. The cost is a smaller colour gamut for grading, which constrains the grade's creative range on high-dynamic-range material.

**ACEScct working space.** The reference for high-end finishing. Wider gamut, more headroom, the right choice for theatrical and HDR-broadcast pieces. The cost is more colour-management discipline — every input gets an input transform, every output gets an output transform, and the team handles the transforms consistently.

**Project-specific log space.** Some projects work in the log space of the dominant camera. Suitable for live-action-heavy projects where AI footage is a supporting element; the AI footage gets transformed into the log space at ingest and the rest of the chain runs as a standard live-action finishing chain.

The skill recommends ACEScct for any theatrical or HDR work, rec709 for social-media-first work, and project-specific log for projects with a single dominant live-action camera. Mixed projects pick based on the highest-fidelity delivery target.

## Failure recovery in the pipeline

Things go wrong mid-pipeline. A short list of common failures and the recovery.

**Failure 1 — A re-finish is required because the client changed the spec.** Recover from the working-format archive; the master goes through the affected stages again. Time cost: hours to days depending on the stage.

**Failure 2 — A shot is rejected at QC and must be regenerated.** Recover by sending the shot back to the shot architect for prompt revision or seed change; new generation lands; re-runs through Stages 1–6 of the post pipeline; QC re-runs. Time cost: a day per round-trip.

**Failure 3 — A delivery render fails platform technical QC.** Recover by inspecting the platform's reject reason, fixing the affected attribute (loudness, codec, container, captions), re-rendering. Time cost: hours.

**Failure 4 — Disclosure-related rejection.** A platform's AI-content policy enforcement flags the piece after upload. Recover by reviewing the disclosure plan, adding or strengthening on-screen disclosure or platform-tagging, re-uploading. The C2PA manifest carries forward; the new disclosure layer is added. Time cost: hours to days depending on whether re-rendering is required.
