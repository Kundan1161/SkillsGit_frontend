# Quarterly Planning Facilitator

## When to use

Use this skill when a team has a planning ritual approaching — quarterly, six-week, half, or annual — and the asker is the facilitator. The output is three artifacts: an agenda, a pre-read, and a post-meeting template. The skill is opinionated about pacing, about reading before deciding, and about turning meetings into commitments rather than discussions.

Engage when the team has at least a rough sense of last cycle's results and at least one candidate bet to discuss. The skill is most useful when the asker has *too much* input — multiple stakeholder asks, several candidate bets, capacity constraints — and needs help organizing them into a session participants can navigate.

Decline or push back when:

- There is no clear team or scope. Planning sessions for an undefined group spiral into territory fights. Recommend a charter conversation first.
- The decisions are already made and the meeting is theater. The skill will surface this as a tell; if the asker confirms, propose a written communication instead of a meeting.
- The cycle being planned is too short for the work the team does. A platform team planning month-by-month will stall — most platform investments take longer than a month. Suggest a longer cycle.
- The team is in crisis. Quarterly planning assumes a roughly stable system. If the team is firefighting a major incident, the right answer is incident management and a short-cycle reset, not a quarter plan.

The skill works at any cycle length but is calibrated for quarterly. Adjust pacing and content for `cycle_length` — the structure stays the same, durations shrink.

## How to apply

Run the methodology in six phases.

### Phase 1 — Frame the cycle

1. **State the cycle's purpose.** One sentence at the top of the pre-read: "This planning cycle commits Q3 work for the platform team, including KTLO, two candidate bets, and a learning investment in observability." A planning session without a stated purpose drifts into general discussion.
2. **Name the constraints.** Dates, headcount, budget, dependencies on other teams, anything fixed before the conversation starts. Constraints reduce false optionality; pretending they don't exist wastes meeting time.
3. **Name the stakeholders.** Who needs to weigh in, who needs to be informed, who is in the room, who is not. Stakeholders missing from the room are often the source of "wait, can we really do that?" reversals weeks later.
4. **Set the success criterion for the session itself.** "By end of session, we have committed bets, named the cuts, and identified asks of partner teams." If the asker cannot name what success looks like for the meeting, the meeting will not produce it.

### Phase 2 — Build the lookback

5. **Score last cycle's commitments.** For each OKR or major commitment, three columns: target, actual, delta. Where the team missed, one-sentence explanation; where the team overperformed, one-sentence explanation. Avoid blame language; describe the system, not the people.
6. **Surface the unanticipated work.** Most teams spend 20–40% of their cycle on work that was not in the plan. Naming this work explicitly prevents the next plan from pretending all capacity is discretionary.
7. **Identify the lessons.** Three to five sentences. What is true now that we did not know at the start of last cycle? What process broke? What worked better than expected? Lessons unlocked: this is the most-skipped section in planning rituals and the most valuable for the next cycle.
8. **Avoid the recap trap.** Lookback is not retro. The lookback exists to inform the *next* set of bets, not to relitigate the past. Keep it short and forward-pointing.

### Phase 3 — Normalize the candidate bets

9. **For each candidate in `candidate_bets`, produce a one-paragraph brief.** The brief answers: what is the problem, who is the user or beneficiary, what is the expected outcome, what is the rough size, what depends on it, what does success look like. Without a brief, the planning room debates words instead of substance.
10. **Estimate size in T-shirt buckets.** Small, medium, large, very large. Resist precision; the conversation is about which bets, not which week.
11. **Tag confidence per bet.** High (we know the problem and have a credible solution path), medium (we know the problem; solution path is hypothesis), low (we have a hypothesis about the problem and want to invest in finding out). All three are valid; the asker decides the mix.
12. **Tag the bet's relationship to last cycle's commitments.** Continuation, course-correction, new bet, or KTLO. A bet labeled "new" without a plausible story for why now is suspicious — name the timing rationale.
13. **Surface duplicates and conflicts.** If two candidate bets serve the same outcome, name it; the team may want to merge or pick one. If two bets directly conflict (one improves a flow the other deprecates), name that too.

### Phase 4 — Do the capacity math

14. **Start with raw capacity.** People × cycle length × productive-time fraction (a useful default is 0.6–0.7 of nominal time after meetings, support rotation, etc., though the asker may have a calibrated number).
15. **Subtract committed work.** KTLO, support, on-call, planned releases of last cycle's work, training, hiring, performance cycles. What remains is *discretionary* capacity.
16. **Compare discretionary capacity to candidate-bet sizes.** This is the most useful artifact for the room. A team with 12 person-weeks of discretionary capacity and 25 person-weeks of candidate bets has a 50% cut to make. Naming this in the pre-read prevents the room from pretending all bets can fit.
17. **Reserve a buffer.** Default 10–20% for the unanticipated work the lookback surfaced. Teams that plan to 100% miss; teams that plan to 80% commit reliably.
18. **Treat each candidate bet as a range, not a point.** Bets are estimates; building reality is messier. Where appropriate, present a low-mid-high size band and let the room negotiate the band.

### Phase 5 — Compose the agenda

19. **Open with a 5-minute frame.** Restate the cycle's purpose, the success criterion, the agenda, and the time discipline. Five minutes invested here saves twenty over the meeting.
20. **Lookback block: 15–25 minutes.** Numbers, lessons, unanticipated work. No relitigation. Anchor the room in the facts before the optionality conversation starts.
21. **Theme summaries: 10–20 minutes.** If the team has 2–4 strategic themes for the cycle (set at the level above), state them and confirm alignment. If the team is the one setting themes, this block becomes a 20-minute discussion to converge on 2–3 themes.
22. **Candidate-bet review: 30–60 minutes.** Each bet gets a brief read-out (assume the room read the pre-read; do not re-read it aloud) and 5–8 minutes of discussion. Goal of this block: each bet is well-enough understood to be cut or committed.
23. **Capacity reality check: 10–15 minutes.** Show the math. The room sees what fits and what does not.
24. **The cut conversation: 30–45 minutes.** Most planning sessions stall here. The facilitator's job: enforce that every cut produces either an explicit "won't do this cycle" entry (named, dated for revisit) or an explicit "stretch if capacity opens" tag. Avoid the limbo of "maybe."
25. **Commitments and asks: 15–20 minutes.** Convert discussion into commitments: who owns each bet, when the kickoff is, what the first deliverable looks like. Name asks of other teams and asks of leadership. A commitment without an ask is often a hidden dependency.
26. **Close with the post-meeting plan: 5–10 minutes.** Who will fill in the post-meeting artifact, when it will be shared, what the next checkpoint is. The meeting is the start of the cycle, not the end.
27. **Build in two breaks for a half-day; more for a full-day or offsite.** Cognitive endurance is a planning input; if the meeting is too long without a break, the last decisions will be the worst decisions.

### Phase 6 — Compose the pre-read and the post-meeting artifact

28. **The pre-read should be readable in 15 minutes.** Lookback summary, theme summaries, candidate-bet briefs (one paragraph each), capacity math, asks summary. Aggressive editing wins; participants who do not read short pre-reads will not read long ones either.
29. **Include pre-read questions.** Three to five questions each participant should think about before the meeting: "What is your conviction on bet X?" "Are there bets you would add or strongly cut?" "What are the asks you'll bring to the meeting?" Questions force participants to come prepared.
30. **Send the pre-read with enough lead time.** Default: 48 hours before the meeting. Less than 24 hours and you have not actually given participants a chance to read; more than a week and they will forget.
31. **The post-meeting artifact has six sections.** Commitments (named bets, owners, target outcomes), cuts (named, with rationale), asks of others (with owners and target dates), asks of leadership (with owners), unresolved (the small set of items that did not get decided, with a follow-up plan), and next checkpoint (date and topic).
32. **Pre-fill the post-meeting artifact where possible.** Commitments and cuts will be filled live; asks and unresolved get filled in the closing 10 minutes. The pre-fill is the candidate-bet briefs and the lookback summary. Speed in the close-out matters; tired teams ship messy artifacts.

### Phase 7 — Facilitator notes for the session

33. **Suggest a co-facilitator.** The asker is probably also a participant. One person cannot both run the time and engage substantively; suggest a co-facilitator (the EM, the team's tech lead, or a peer PM).
34. **Suggest a scribe.** A dedicated scribe filling the post-meeting artifact in real time means the meeting actually ends when it is supposed to. Without a scribe, "we'll send out notes" becomes "no notes."
35. **Suggest a parking lot.** A live document where off-topic items go. Without it, important tangents derail the agenda. With it, tangents get captured and the meeting stays on track.
36. **Anticipate the failure modes.** Three to surface in the pre-read: "If we run long on lookback, the cut conversation will get squeezed and we will not converge." "If a stakeholder ask appears for the first time in the room, we will need a sidebar before committing." "If capacity math is contested in the meeting, we will defer to a follow-up rather than re-estimate live."

## Inputs

- **`team_context`** — required. Who the team is, what they own, their stage.
- **`lookback_data`** — optional. Last cycle's commitments and results. Without it, the lookback is generic.
- **`candidate_bets`** — optional. Without it, the bet-review block is replaced by a generative discussion.
- **`capacity`** — optional. Without it, the capacity math uses defaults and is flagged.
- **`stakeholder_asks`** — optional. Drives the asks section and the conflicts-with-internal-bets check.
- **`cycle_length`** — optional. Defaults to `quarter`.
- **`session_format`** — optional. Defaults to `half-day`. Single-meeting agendas compress everything to ~90 minutes; multi-day offsites stretch and add structured breaks.

## Outputs

- **`agenda`** — the time-boxed session agenda.
- **`pre_read`** — the document participants read before the meeting.
- **`post_meeting_artifact`** — the template filled in during and right after.

## Examples

### Example 1 — A team with 6 candidate bets and capacity for 3

**Source.** Team of 5 engineers + 1 PM + 1 designer; 6 candidate bets ranging from medium to very large; capacity math shows 14 person-weeks discretionary after KTLO and a planned launch of last cycle's work. Stakeholder asks include 2 from sales and 1 from CS.

**Output highlights.**

- **Pre-read.** Six bet briefs, T-shirt sizes (S, M, M, L, L, XL — sum > 30 person-weeks). Lookback shows 3 of 4 OKRs were hit; the missed one was a capacity issue, not an execution issue.
- **Agenda.** Half-day with a 45-minute cut-conversation block; facilitator co-pilot is the EM; scribe is the designer.
- **Capacity math.** Shows discretionary capacity 14 person-weeks against ~32 person-weeks of candidate work — a 50%+ cut required. Recommended split: commit S + M + M (8 weeks) + reserve buffer (2 weeks) → 10 of 14, leaving 4 for unanticipated.
- **Post-meeting template.** Pre-fills the candidate briefs and lookback; commitments / cuts / asks / unresolved get filled live.

### Example 2 — Annual planning for a four-team product group

**Source.** Asker is the group PM lead. Five candidate annual themes, ~30 candidate bets across teams, capacity math at the group level.

**Output highlights.**

- **Format.** Multi-day offsite (2 days). Day 1 is theme convergence (5 candidates → 3 themes); day 2 is per-team bet commitment within themes.
- **Pre-read.** Long form (~10-minute read), with explicit pre-read questions for each team lead.
- **Agenda.** Day 1 includes a "what we are not doing" exercise — explicitly name the bets that survive the year only as backlog items. This is the most under-used technique in annual planning.
- **Notes.** Suggests a co-facilitator from outside the group to keep the most senior person from dominating the room.

### Example 3 — A short cycle for a small team

**Source.** Six-week cycle, four people, two candidate bets.

**Output highlights.**

- **Format.** Single 90-minute meeting. Lookback is short. Cut conversation is brief because the math allows both bets.
- **Capacity check.** Yes, both fit at 70% of the cycle, leaving buffer for unanticipated. The cut conversation becomes a "could we add a third small bet" exercise instead.
- **Notes.** For small teams, the post-meeting artifact is a single page; suggest a Slack post rather than a doc.

## Limitations

- The skill does not run the meeting. It produces the artifacts the facilitator brings. A bad facilitator with great artifacts still has a bad meeting; a good facilitator can pull off a meeting with worse artifacts.
- Capacity math is only as good as the inputs. Without `capacity`, defaults are used and flagged; the team should validate before using the number to commit work.
- The skill defaults to product/engineering team conventions. Adapt for design, research, or marketing teams — the structure holds, but the inputs and decision artifacts vary.
- The skill cannot decide cuts. The cut conversation is a human conversation; the artifacts give the room a clean place to land, not the answer.
- Cross-team coordination is partial. If the team's bets depend on a partner team that the asker did not name, the skill cannot raise the dependency proactively. Repeat: capture asks of others explicitly.
- The skill assumes good faith. If a stakeholder will bring a surprise ask to the room and refuse to take it offline, no agenda will fix the meeting; the right response is a pre-meeting alignment with that stakeholder.
