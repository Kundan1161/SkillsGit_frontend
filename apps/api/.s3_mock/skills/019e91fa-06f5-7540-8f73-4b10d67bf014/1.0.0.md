# Active Learning Loop Designer

## When to use

Use this skill when a team needs a written plan for the *loop* that grows a computer-vision dataset under a finite labelling budget. The loop selects which unlabelled items become labels, runs the labels back through training, and decides when to stop. The plan covers the acquisition strategy (which examples to send to the labelling queue and why), the batch design (how many at once, how diverse), the cohort budgeting (how labels are allocated across rare conditions, demographic slices, geographies, and sensor variants), the production-traffic hard-example mining (how on-board telemetry feeds the queue), the stop conditions (when the loop pauses, refits, or terminates a cohort), and the regression guards (how the loop avoids forgetting what was already solved).

The skill is downstream of `cv-dataset-curation-architect` (which produces the corpus plan and the ODD coverage matrix) and runs alongside `annotation-quality-program-designer` (which keeps the labelling signal trustworthy). It feeds the modelling team a queue rather than a pile, and it feeds the data-engineering team a reproducible recipe for "what to label next." It is appropriate for detection, segmentation, classification, keypoint, tracking, 3D detection, and multi-task heads; the acquisition functions differ but the loop structure is shared.

**Mandatory safety disclaimer.** This skill produces methodology guidance. CV systems deployed to safety-critical contexts cause physical harm when they misperceive. Every recommendation must be validated in simulation, on representative real cohorts, and reviewed by qualified ML engineers before deployment.

## How to apply

The skill walks a fourteen-stage pipeline. Early stages set the loop's intent and the baselines it grows from. Middle stages design the acquisition function, the batch composition, the pool management, and the cohort budgets. Late stages cover production-traffic mining, stop conditions, regression guards, instrumentation, and the deliverable.

### Stage 1 — Restate the loop's purpose

1. From `task_and_baseline` derive the precise metric the loop is improving and the operating point that metric is measured at. "Lift mean average precision" is too vague. "Lift recall on pedestrians at precision 0.99 in low-light conditions" is a target a loop can be designed against.
2. Identify the *consumer's blast radius*. A loop that grows a search index can tolerate misallocated labels far more than a loop that grows a pedestrian detector for a safety-critical platform. Higher blast radius justifies tighter cohort budgets, stricter regression guards, and longer review cycles.
3. Identify the *failure modes the loop is meant to fix*. Plateauing recall is one problem; demographic under-performance, rare-condition collapse, distribution drift, or labelling-noise contamination are different problems and call for different acquisition strategies. Stage the loop around the dominant failure mode first.
4. Set the *cycle cadence* — weekly, fortnightly, monthly. Cycles too short do not give the model time to absorb labels; cycles too long under-use the labelling capacity. The cadence is a function of model retrain time, labelling vendor turn-around, and how quickly the consumer needs to see improvement.

### Stage 2 — Baseline and pool inventory

5. Snapshot the current model with a frozen weight hash, the dataset version it was trained on, the eval-set version it was scored on, and the headline numbers per cohort. The loop's progress is measured against this frozen baseline; the snapshot is reproducible.
6. Inventory the unlabelled pool. Count items, list source channels, record per-item metadata, document the cost of pulling an item to the labelling queue, and document the cost of *scoring* an item with the current model (which dominates compute when the pool is very large).
7. Decide whether the pool fits in memory for embedding-based diversity selection or whether streaming or sub-sampling is required. Pools of millions of items typically need a two-stage filter — cheap pre-filter on metadata, expensive scorer on a candidate subset.
8. Document the *staleness horizon* of the pool. Frames collected three years ago under a retired sensor configuration are less valuable than fresh frames; the loop discounts staleness when scoring.

### Stage 3 — Choose acquisition function family

9. Acquisition functions fall into three families: uncertainty-driven (pick items the model is unsure about), diversity-driven (pick items that, together, cover the input distribution), and hybrid (combine the two so a batch is both informative and non-redundant).
10. Uncertainty-driven options include least-confidence (one minus the top-1 probability), margin (difference between the top two probabilities), entropy of the predictive distribution, Monte-Carlo dropout variance, deep ensemble disagreement, Bayesian Active Learning by Disagreement (BALD), and learning-loss prediction. The right choice depends on task type and how calibrated the baseline is — uncalibrated softmax scores are misleading and should be replaced with ensemble variance or temperature-scaled probabilities.
11. Diversity-driven options include k-means clustering on embeddings followed by closest-to-centroid selection, k-centers (coreset) greedy selection, and BADGE (gradient-embedding-based clustering). Diversity prevents a single uncertain region from dominating a batch.
12. Hybrid options include diverse mini-batch active learning (pre-filter by uncertainty, then cluster), BatchBALD (joint-mutual-information acquisition), and uncertainty-weighted coreset. Hybrid is the default for batch sizes above a few dozen; pure uncertainty over-collects redundant examples; pure diversity over-collects easy examples.
13. For detection and segmentation, score uncertainty at the *prediction* level (per-box confidence, per-pixel entropy, per-instance disagreement) and aggregate to image-level scores. Image-level only loses too much signal; pure pixel-level overweights cluttered scenes.

### Stage 4 — Calibration discipline

14. Before scoring the pool, calibrate the baseline on a held-out calibration set. Temperature scaling, isotonic regression, or histogram binning produce a model whose confidence matches its accuracy. Uncalibrated confidences make uncertainty sampling unstable; the loop will burn budget on items the model was confidently wrong about for the wrong reasons.
15. For ensembles, run at least three or four members trained with different seeds. Disagreement is the dominant calibrated uncertainty signal; ensembling is expensive but is the most robust option for safety-critical loops.
16. For Monte-Carlo dropout, run at least twenty stochastic forward passes per item and average. MC-dropout is cheaper than ensembles but its variance estimates are noisier; it is acceptable when ensembles are infeasible.
17. Audit calibration each cycle. As the model improves, its confidence shifts; the loop re-calibrates rather than re-using stale temperature parameters.

### Stage 5 — Diversity through embeddings

18. Compute a fixed-dimension embedding per item — a global-pool feature from the model's penultimate layer, a CLIP-style image embedding, or a task-specific embedding. The embedding is the substrate over which diversity is measured.
19. For very large pools, approximate-nearest-neighbour structures (HNSW, IVF-PQ) keep diversity selection tractable. For smaller pools, brute-force k-means or k-centers on the full embedding matrix works.
20. Combine the uncertainty score with embedding diversity in a single acquisition pass: rank by uncertainty, take the top N*beta candidates, cluster into N groups, pick the most uncertain item per cluster. Beta of ten to fifty is the typical pre-filter ratio.

### Stage 6 — Batch design

21. Choose the batch size to match the labelling vendor's intake cadence and the modelling team's retrain cadence. Too small wastes coordination cost; too large outpaces the model's ability to integrate before the next selection.
22. Stratify each batch by cohort. A batch is not a flat list; it is a vector across the cohorts in `cohort_priorities`. A cohort that is under-performing receives a larger slice; a cohort that is saturated receives a maintenance slice.
23. Reserve a *control slice* per batch — items selected at random rather than by acquisition function. The control slice is the baseline against which the acquisition function's lift is measured. Without a control, the loop cannot prove the acquisition strategy is paying for itself.
24. Reserve an *exploration slice* per batch — items selected for diversity even if uncertainty is low. The exploration slice prevents the loop from getting stuck in the model's known unknowns and never discovering the unknown unknowns.
25. Reserve a *replay slice* per batch — items the model previously solved that share characteristics with the targeted cohort. The replay slice is the primary defence against catastrophic forgetting (see Stage 11).

### Stage 7 — Cohort budgeting

26. Take `cohort_priorities` and allocate the per-cycle budget across cohorts using a rule that combines current performance gap, deployment-criticality, and historical learning rate. Cohorts with the largest gap and highest deployment-criticality get the most budget; cohorts already meeting target performance get maintenance budget.
27. Recompute cohort allocations each cycle. As cohorts converge, budget shifts toward cohorts that have not. The reallocation rule is explicit and reproducible so a contributor can predict next cycle's allocation from this cycle's metrics.
28. Cap any single cohort's slice at a maximum (e.g. forty percent of the cycle's budget) so the loop never starves the rest of the cohort matrix in pursuit of a single hard target.
29. For demographic cohorts, apply a *fairness floor* — a minimum slice that protects under-represented groups from being deprioritised by performance-gap heuristics that would otherwise favour cohorts with more existing data.

### Stage 8 — Hard-example mining from production traffic

30. Wire telemetry from the deployed fleet (or staging environment) into the unlabelled pool. The on-board signals that matter are: low-confidence predictions, near-miss flags, operator-initiated overrides, customer complaints, disagreement between primary and shadow models, disagreement between modalities (camera and LiDAR), and rule-engine flags.
31. For each signal, define an *eligibility filter* — what privacy regime governs the frame, what consent applies, what retention horizon allows the frame to be re-used. Production-traffic mining is only as legitimate as the consent chain that sourced it.
32. Apply a *de-bias filter*. Production telemetry over-represents whatever the model is currently failing on, which is informative, but it also over-represents whatever the consumer happens to use most. The loop pairs production-mined items with deliberate cohort-coverage items to keep the corpus balanced.
33. Treat near-miss flags with extra weight. A near-miss in a safety-critical deployment is the most expensive signal in the loop and gets the shortest path to the labelling queue.
34. Document the *latency* from production-event to labelled-corpus inclusion. Long latency means models are catching up to last quarter's failures; the loop reports this metric per cycle.

### Stage 9 — Pool management

35. Maintain a *pool ledger* — a per-item record of which items have been scored, when, by which model version, with which acquisition score, and whether the item was selected, labelled, or returned to the pool.
36. Returning items to the pool after a reject is acceptable but recorded. The next cycle's scorer is a different model; items that scored low last cycle may score high this cycle.
37. Items that have been selected and labelled exit the pool permanently. Re-selecting an already-labelled item wastes budget and signals a ledger defect; the loop guards against this.
38. Items whose licence, consent, or privacy status has expired or changed exit the pool. The ledger records the reason.
39. Periodically *re-embed* the pool with the current model's penultimate features. Embeddings from an old model represent the old model's view of the world; diversity selection against stale embeddings under-covers regions the new model has learnt to distinguish.

### Stage 10 — Stop conditions

40. Define per-cohort stop conditions explicitly. A cohort stops receiving budget when one of: (a) its target performance is reached and held over two consecutive eval cycles; (b) marginal lift per labelled item has fallen below a threshold (the labels are paying for less and less); (c) labelling-quality on the cohort has degraded enough that more labels would hurt; (d) the cohort's eval-set has saturated and further measurement is uninformative until the eval-set expands.
41. Define a *loop-level stop condition*. The loop pauses (not terminates) when the headline metric has not moved beyond noise for K consecutive cycles, prompting a strategy review rather than mechanical continuation.
42. Define a *pivot trigger*. If a cohort that was on-track regresses by more than a threshold for two cycles, the loop pivots — diagnoses whether the regression is from labelling drift, distribution drift, taxonomy drift, or model-architecture change, and chooses one of (re-label, re-curate, re-train, roll back).
43. Stop conditions are documented in the cohort metadata and are auditable. A loop that runs forever because no one set a stop condition burns budget for diminishing returns.

### Stage 11 — Regression guards against catastrophic forgetting

44. Maintain an *anchor eval set* that does not grow with the loop. The anchor measures whether the model can still do what it could do before the loop started. The anchor is frozen at the loop's start and is only ever extended deliberately, never replaced.
45. After each retrain, compare per-class and per-cohort metrics against the previous model. A drop beyond a threshold (typically two to three standard deviations of the baseline noise on the anchor) is a regression and blocks promotion.
46. Maintain a *replay budget* — labels from previously-solved cohorts re-included in each training run. The replay budget is small enough to avoid bloating the corpus but large enough to keep the gradient signal for solved cohorts present in each retrain.
47. For continual-learning style loops where the model is updated incrementally, use rehearsal of prior cohort exemplars; for full-retrain loops, the replay budget naturally appears in the training mix.
48. Audit regression by *condition* as well as by class. A model that improves on average but regresses at night, in rain, or on a specific demographic slice has not improved overall — the loop catches this by stratified eval.
49. Document the *acceptable-regression* policy. Some cohorts are allowed to regress slightly if the gain elsewhere is large; that policy is set in advance, reviewed by qualified ML engineers, and is not negotiated retroactively after a bad release.

### Stage 12 — Labelling-quality coupling

50. Pair the loop with the annotation-quality programme. Items routed by uncertainty sampling are often the hardest items to label; if labelling quality on those items is poor, the loop amplifies labelling noise into model noise.
51. Route uncertainty-sampled items to *senior reviewers* or to a *double-pass* workflow rather than to the standard single-annotator pipeline. The cost is higher per item but the signal is cleaner.
52. Monitor inter-annotator agreement on the loop's items separately from the corpus baseline. Loop items where IAA is low feed back into taxonomy revision, guideline clarification, or labelling-tool change — not back into training as if they were trusted labels.
53. When a batch's labels arrive, *audit the label distribution* before training. A batch that is supposed to grow rare-condition coverage but came back majority-easy-cases is a sourcing or labelling defect, not a training input.

### Stage 13 — Instrumentation and metrics

54. Per-cycle metrics, recorded in a dashboard or tracking system: headline metric on the frozen eval set, per-cohort metrics, anchor-eval metrics, marginal lift per labelled item, acquisition-vs-control lift, batch-composition adherence to plan, labelling latency, labelling cost per item, IAA per batch.
55. Per-cycle artefacts: model weight hash, dataset version, eval-set version, acquisition-score distribution, embedding-cluster distribution, calibration curve, regression report.
56. Per-cycle decision log: which cohort budgets shifted, why, which cohorts stopped, which were paused, which were pivoted. The log is the loop's narrative.
57. Quarterly retrospective: are the acquisition functions still beating control? Are the cohort priorities still aligned with consumer needs? Has the unlabelled pool refreshed enough to keep up with the model? The retrospective re-tunes the loop rather than letting it drift.

### Stage 14 — Compose the deliverable

58. Open with a one-paragraph *loop intent* statement: what cohort gaps the loop is closing, what metric it is improving, against what frozen baseline, on what cadence.
59. Render the plan as a markdown document covering acquisition strategy, calibration discipline, batch design, cohort budgets, production-traffic mining policy, pool management, stop conditions, regression guards, labelling-quality coupling, instrumentation, and the risk register.
60. Emit `plan_json` with structured fields: `acquisition` (functions chosen, parameters, calibration policy), `batch_design` (size, slices, stratification), `pool_management` (ledger fields, embedding refresh cadence), `cohort_budgets` (allocation rule, fairness floor, caps), `production_mining` (signals, eligibility filters, latency targets), `stop_conditions` (per cohort, loop-level, pivot triggers), `regression_guards` (anchor eval set, replay budget, acceptable-regression policy), `instrumentation` (metrics, artefacts, decision log), `risk_register`.
61. Close with the mandatory safety disclaimer and a "what this loop does not cover" note that points the user at the `annotation-quality-program-designer` for label trust and at the `synthetic-data-augmentation-strategist` for when the loop cannot source enough real data.

## Examples

**Input (placeholder):**

`task_and_baseline`: "Detection of pedestrians, cyclists, and small obstacles for an autonomous yard tractor; baseline is a 65M-parameter detector trained on 120k labelled frames; current pedestrian recall at precision 0.99 is 0.94, target is 0.99; headline plateau over the last two retrains."

`unlabelled_pool`: "12M frames from six months of fleet recordings across four yard sites, metadata includes site, time-of-day, weather, sensor variant. Per-frame scoring cost is 4 ms on a single GPU. Pool fits in approximate-nearest-neighbour index but not in memory."

`budget_and_horizon`: "40k labels over four monthly cycles, single labelling vendor with 2-week turnaround."

`cohort_priorities`: "Pedestrian-at-night, pedestrian-in-rain, cyclist-with-cargo, child-sized actor; geographic split across the four sites with site D under-represented."

`production_signals`: "Low-confidence flags from on-board model, near-miss overrides from safety operator, disagreement between camera and LiDAR-based detectors. Consent and retention chain is documented."

**Plan (abbreviated):**

- Loop intent: close pedestrian-at-night and child-sized-actor gaps over four cycles, hold all other cohorts at or above baseline.
- Acquisition: hybrid uncertainty + diversity. Uncertainty via four-member deep ensemble disagreement on image-level aggregated per-box variance, after temperature-scaled calibration. Diversity via k-means clustering (k = batch size) on penultimate-layer embeddings of the top-50x pre-filter.
- Batch design: 10k items per cycle; 60% targeted cohort, 15% production-mined hard examples, 10% control (random), 10% exploration (diversity-only), 5% replay of solved cohorts.
- Cohort budgets cycle 1: pedestrian-at-night 35%, child-sized 20%, cyclist-with-cargo 15%, pedestrian-in-rain 10%, site-D coverage 15%, fairness floor for under-represented operator-vest variants 5%.
- Production mining: near-miss flags routed within 48 hours to senior-reviewer pipeline; low-confidence flags batched weekly; camera/LiDAR disagreement routed to a structured triage queue.
- Pool management: ledger records score, model version, status; embedding refresh after every retrain; stale items (older than nine months) discounted by 0.5 in scoring.
- Stop conditions: cohort stops on two consecutive cycles at target precision-recall; loop pivots if headline does not move beyond noise for two cycles; child-sized cohort gets explicit human-review gate before promotion.
- Regression guards: anchor eval frozen at start; replay budget 5%; per-condition regression report on every retrain; >2σ regression on any cohort blocks promotion pending ML-engineer review.
- Labelling-quality coupling: uncertainty-sampled items routed double-pass with senior adjudication; per-batch IAA monitored separately; low-IAA items returned to taxonomy review rather than training.
- Risk register: feedback-loop bias (mitigated by control + exploration slices); under-represented operator-vest variant (mitigated by fairness floor); silent regression at night (mitigated by stratified anchor eval); production-mining consent drift (mitigated by quarterly audit).

**Output excerpt:** the markdown plan plus a JSON object whose `acquisition` enumerates the functions and calibration policy, whose `batch_design` lists per-slice fractions, whose `cohort_budgets` shows the per-cycle allocation rule with caps and fairness floor, whose `stop_conditions` enumerates per-cohort and loop-level conditions, and whose `regression_guards` describes the anchor eval, replay budget, and acceptable-regression policy.

## Limitations

- The skill plans the loop; it does not run the loop, score the pool, or train the model. It is the contract between data-engineering, modelling, and labelling-vendor coordination.
- Acquisition-function choice is task-dependent and is heuristic. The plan recommends a default and a fallback; final selection should be validated by a pilot cycle against a control slice on representative data.
- Calibration is necessary but not sufficient. A poorly-calibrated baseline plus a well-designed acquisition function still produces unstable selections; the plan flags calibration as the first thing to fix when the loop misbehaves.
- Hard-example mining from production traffic is only as legitimate as the consent chain and retention regime that sourced the frames. The plan flags eligibility filters; the legal review of those filters is outside scope.
- Active learning can amplify feedback loops — the model defines what is uncertain, the loop labels what the model says is uncertain, the corpus reflects the model's blind spots rather than the world's. The plan mitigates with control and exploration slices and with stratified eval, but does not eliminate the risk.
- Catastrophic-forgetting guards are heuristic. For high-stakes deployments, the regression report is reviewed by qualified ML engineers before any model is promoted.
- For very small datasets or very small budgets, full active-learning loops cost more in coordination than they pay back in labels; the plan flags an "active learning is not yet justified" threshold.
- The skill assumes the upstream curation plan and the downstream annotation-quality programme exist. Without either, the loop will route mis-targeted items to noisy labels and produce worse models faster.
