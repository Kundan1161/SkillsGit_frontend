## When to use

Invoke this skill when an agent is asked to plan, review, or execute a retouching pass on a developed photo and the work goes beyond global RAW corrections. Typical triggers:

- A portrait needs skin work, eye and hair refinement, and shaping before it is delivered.
- A product needs blemish removal, surface evenness, edge cleanup, and shadow shaping.
- A retouched file came back with concerns ("looks plastic", "loses pore detail", "halos around hair") and needs a methodology audit.
- A team is standardising retouch practice and wants a layer stack and QC pass written down.

Do not invoke this skill for RAW development decisions (use the development-pipeline skill), for colour profile setup (use the colour-management skill), or for compositing across multiple source frames (that is a different methodology — composition rather than retouch). Defer to a forensic-imaging skill if the request is to recover hidden detail rather than to improve perceived quality.

This skill assumes a layer-capable editor with masking. The names of the operations are written generically because the same operations exist in every serious image editor under different labels: a "curves adjustment layer" is the same idea in every tool, even if some call it "tone curves" or "curves layer".

## How to apply

Run the retouch in passes. Each pass produces a labelled layer group. Never collapse groups during the work — flattening loses the ability to revisit a decision.

### Pass 1: Establish the file

1. Open the export from the RAW developer at the bit depth and colour space the brief calls for. Sixteen-bit is mandatory for any work that touches skin, sky, or gradients — eight-bit posterises under aggressive curves and frequency separation.

2. Set up the layer stack template before retouching. From bottom to top: a locked background layer holding the original pixels; a "spot work" group for blemish and dust removal; a "structure" group for frequency separation or surface work; a "shape" group for dodge and burn; a "colour" group for selective colour and hue adjustments; a "tone" group for global curves and contrast adjustments scoped to the retouch; an "output" group for final sharpening and any export-specific tweaks. Always work additively into this template.

3. Establish reference. Open the brief's reference image, the prior delivered frame from the same series, or the brand standard alongside the working file. Retouching without reference produces drift across a series.

### Pass 2: Spot work

4. Resolve every dust spot, sensor blemish, lint thread, stray hair, and obvious distraction. Use a healing tool on a separate empty layer with "sample all layers" enabled. Never paint on the background.

5. Treat the spot pass as binary: either a thing is there in the brief's world or it is not. Defer aesthetic decisions (is this mole an identity feature) to a separate pass. Spot work should be a few minutes; deliberation belongs elsewhere.

6. For repeating patterns (fabric weave, wood grain, sensor dust) check that the heal source is not itself contaminated before sampling. A single bad sample propagates across dozens of spots.

### Pass 3: Surface and texture

7. Choose the surface technique by subject. Frequency separation suits skin, smooth product surfaces, and sky. Dodge and burn suits subjects where texture must be preserved (hair, fabric, food, woodgrain). The two techniques are not interchangeable; the most common retouch failure mode is using frequency separation where dodge and burn was the right tool, producing the characteristic plastic look.

8. If using frequency separation: split the image into a low-frequency layer (colour and tone) and a high-frequency layer (texture and detail) using a Gaussian blur radius matched to the smallest feature you want to keep on the high-frequency layer. Work the low-frequency layer with a soft brush at low opacity to even out colour and tone transitions. Touch the high-frequency layer only with a clone or healing tool to repair individual texture defects, never with a soft brush. Painting on the high-frequency layer destroys the technique's only virtue.

9. If using dodge and burn: create two curves adjustment layers, one lightening, one darkening, each masked to black. Paint into the masks with a soft low-opacity brush to redistribute light. Work at small brush sizes and low opacities — two to ten percent on a one-hundred scale. Many small strokes compound to a believable result; few heavy strokes look painted.

10. For skin specifically: work pore-preserving. Never apply a global blur to skin. The defensible operations are (a) heal individual blemishes, (b) even out colour with frequency separation's low-frequency layer, (c) shape light and shadow with dodge and burn, and (d) selectively desaturate redness with a hue-saturation layer masked to skin. Everything else is suspect.

11. For product surfaces: work edge-aware. The most common product retouch tell is a clean centre meeting a soft edge from a heal brush that bled outside the product. Mask retouching layers tightly to the product silhouette before any surface work.

### Pass 4: Shape

12. Sculpt the subject with dodge and burn at low contrast. For portraits, the standard moves are: brighten the inner eye and tear-duct slightly; darken the lash line; brighten the bridge of the nose subtly; darken the sides of the nose subtly; brighten the upper lip and darken under the lower lip to add dimension; brighten the cheekbone slightly. Every move should be invisible on its own and visible only in aggregate.

13. For products, shape the form light. Read where the light is coming from, then enhance the highlight on that side and deepen the shadow on the other. Avoid adding light that does not exist in the original frame — the viewer reads inconsistent lighting as fake before they read it consciously.

14. Resist liquify. Liquify is a powerful tool with a high failure rate. If the brief requires a body or product reshape, do it in small increments on a smart object, verify against a straight-line reference (a vertical from a doorframe, a horizontal from the horizon) and stop short of where you think the goal is. Most over-retouched portraits are over-liquified, not over-frequency-separated.

15. Apply hair refinement only on visibly damaged hairs. Add stray hairs back with a one-pixel brush sampled from the subject's hair colour if the cull cost the portrait its life — but recognise that fabricated hair never sits as naturally as real hair, and prefer reshoots when possible.

### Pass 5: Colour

16. Run selective colour adjustments through hue-saturation layers, channel mixer, or LAB curves, depending on the editor. The choice matters less than the principle: every colour layer must be masked to where it is wanted. Global colour shifts are RAW development decisions, not retouch decisions.

17. For skin tones, watch the red and yellow balance. The most defensible target is the photographer's intent for the series rather than a universal "correct" skin tone — but a common smell test is whether skin reads warmer than the background warm. If skin is cooler than the background, the lighting was probably mixed and the white balance compromise during RAW development needs revisiting.

18. For product colour, match the brand's reference values when supplied. A printed Pantone reference and a screen-only sRGB target are not the same thing; ask which one the brief means before chasing numbers.

### Pass 6: Content-aware and fill

19. Content-aware fill and generative fill are last-resort tools for backgrounds that the camera did not capture cleanly. Constrain them: feed a tight selection, prefer to fill in small areas with multiple passes, and inspect at one hundred percent for repeated patches.

20. For brand-sensitive subjects (logos, faces, recognisable products), do not let a generative fill produce content that resembles a different brand, person, or product. The retouch is responsible for what is in the frame, even if a model produced it. Verify.

21. Distinguish between repair fill (rebuilding what was actually there but occluded) and invention fill (creating content that was not). Invention fill needs explicit client approval and a written note in the deliverable record.

### Pass 7: Global tone

22. After all local work, evaluate the global tone. The retouched file should be slightly softer in contrast than the eventual delivered file, because output sharpening and any web-encoder contrast crunch will firm it back up. Aim a touch under, not a touch over.

23. Add a final curves adjustment to set the black point and white point precisely. Verify on a calibrated display that the deepest shadow has visible structure if it is meant to and is clipped to zero if it is meant to be.

24. If the brief calls for film emulation or a stylised colour grade, apply it as the final tone-group layer. Keep the grade reversible — a flattened grade is unrepairable in revision.

### Pass 8: QC and delivery

25. Run the QC checklist below before flattening or exporting. Each item is a known failure class observed across surveyed practice.

26. Save the layered master at full bit depth before exporting any deliverable. A layered master is the only insurance against a revision request three weeks later that asks for something only the layers can answer.

27. Export deliverables to the spec from the brief: dimensions, colour space, bit depth, file format, filename pattern. Verify a sample by opening it in a clean viewer.

## QC checklist before delivery

- Skin: pores visible at one hundred percent on at least the face's well-lit side. No blur halos. No hue shifts at the skin edge against background.
- Eyes: catchlights present and consistent in direction across both eyes. Whites neutral, not blue-tinted from over-desaturation. Iris detail preserved.
- Hair: no smearing at the edge against the background. No erased flyaways that the brief wanted kept.
- Edges: at one-hundred percent zoom, the subject silhouette has no halo, no contamination from a healing brush, no jagged mask boundary.
- Colour: brand or skin-tone reference matches within the agreed tolerance. No unexpected casts in neutral regions (whites of eyes, white fabric, white background).
- Surfaces: product surfaces hold texture where the original had texture. No bald patches from over-frequency-separation.
- Shape: liquify movements are reversible and proportional. Vertical and horizontal references in the frame remain straight where they should be.
- Background: any fills are visually consistent with surrounding pixels. No repeated patches. No invented features the brief did not ask for.
- Metadata: copyright, photographer, and rights metadata embedded. GPS scrubbed if delivery is public.
- File: bit depth, colour space, dimensions, format, filename all match the brief. Sample export opened in clean viewer reads correctly.
- Layered master: saved at full bit depth with layer groups intact for revision recovery.

## Reference layer stack templates

### Portrait template

From bottom to top:

- Background (locked): original developed pixels.
- Spot work group: healing-on-empty-layer for blemishes, lint, sensor dust.
- Skin colour group: low-frequency layer from frequency separation; selective desaturation of stray reds.
- Skin shape group: dodge curves layer (masked black); burn curves layer (masked black).
- Eye work group: dodge for iris and tear-duct lift; sharpen for iris detail; whites neutralisation.
- Hair group: clone for stray fixes; selective darkening for body.
- Tone group: global curves for final contrast; black-point and white-point set.
- Output group: output sharpening, sized to delivery.

### Product template

From bottom to top:

- Background (locked): original developed pixels.
- Edge group: silhouette-tight selection saved as path; edge cleanup layers masked to silhouette.
- Spot work group: heal of dust, lint, surface marks.
- Surface group: dodge-and-burn for shape (textured surfaces); frequency separation for smooth surfaces only.
- Catchlight group: highlight enhancement on reflective surfaces with care to preserve direction.
- Background uniformity group: levels to peg background to brief's required value.
- Tone group: contrast finalisation.
- Output group: output sharpening per medium.

### Editorial composite template

When a retouch combines two or more source frames (a focus stack, a face swap from a parallel frame, a sky replacement):

- Each source frame as its own smart-object base.
- Per-source local adjustments inside that smart object so the source remains revisitable.
- Merge group above the sources for blending, mask, and combined output sharpening.
- The composite is then run through the standard retouch stack above.

## Common failure modes

- **Plastic skin**: skin lacks pores at viewing distance. Caused by frequency separation applied to the high-frequency layer, or by global blurring of skin. Fix: rebuild from the master with frequency separation restricted to the low-frequency layer, and use dodge and burn for shape work.
- **Halo around hair against background**: a soft fringe of background colour around hair edges. Caused by oversharpening combined with imprecise selection, or by aggressive content-aware fill of the background that pulled colour into the hair region. Fix: refine the hair edge mask, reduce sharpening at the hair edge, and constrain background fills to the area beyond the hair.
- **Catchlight removed by accident**: eyes lose their highlight after dodge-and-burn or after frequency separation. Caused by painting through the catchlight rather than around it. Fix: catchlights should be preserved as a hard rule; mask them out of any darkening layer.
- **Symmetrical eyes**: portrait subject's eyes look identical in size and shape after liquify. Caused by over-correction. Fix: deliberate slight asymmetry is natural; revert and accept the original asymmetry.
- **Bald product surface**: a product that had natural surface texture (leather, brushed metal, wood) looks featureless. Caused by frequency separation applied to a surface that needed dodge-and-burn instead. Fix: use dodge-and-burn for textured surfaces; reserve frequency separation for smooth surfaces.
- **Repeated content-aware patches**: backgrounds show obviously repeating texture. Caused by content-aware fill at too coarse a granularity. Fix: smaller selections, multiple passes, manual cloning where the fill cannot resolve cleanly.
- **Colour cast on whites**: white shirt or background reads warm or cool after global colour grading. Caused by an unmasked colour layer. Fix: mask colour layers to where they are wanted; whites should be checked specifically.

## Examples

Example invocation: "Plan a portrait retouch for an editorial cover. Editor's policy: pores must remain visible, no liquify, no AI fill. File comes in as a 16-bit Adobe RGB TIFF."

Expected output: a plan that uses pore-preserving frequency separation only on the low-frequency layer for redness evenness, dodge and burn for all shaping, hue-saturation for selective desaturation of stray reds, and an explicit "no liquify, no generative fill" constraint reflected in the layer stack. The QC checklist emphasises pore visibility, neutral whites, and untouched silhouette.

Second example: "Jewellery on white, 60 SKUs, catalogue spec demands consistent edges and exact background white at value 254." Expected plan: a templated layer stack repeated per SKU; edge work using a path-based selection traced once per SKU rather than a healing brush near the edge; a curves layer in the tone group that pegs the background to RGB 254 explicitly; a QC checklist that includes background-value sampling at three points per frame and edge inspection at 100% along the silhouette.

Third example: "Food photography for a quarterly menu update — twenty plated dishes, natural light, magazine reproduction." Expected plan emphasises preserving surface texture (food rarely benefits from frequency separation), dodge-and-burn to enhance the light direction, selective colour on garnish elements where the freshness reads in the colour, and a QC checklist that includes "no fabricated steam, no fabricated condiment that was not on the plate" because food retouch is a frequent site of invention-fill that creates legal exposure.

## Limitations

This skill does not replace a retoucher's eye or the brief. The order, the layer stack, and the QC checklist make work auditable and reversible; they do not decide what the final image should look like. The skill does not cover compositing across multiple source frames, focus stacking, exposure blending for HDR, or large-scale background replacement — those are adjacent disciplines with their own methodologies. AI-assisted retouch tools change rapidly; the principle that fabricated content must be flagged for client approval applies regardless of which tool produced it. For images that will be used as evidence (journalism, scientific imaging, legal exhibits) much of this methodology is out of scope: those contexts forbid most local changes.
