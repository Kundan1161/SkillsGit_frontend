# Cut Decision Methodology

## When to use

Use this skill when the user is making cuts inside a scene or sequence and wants more than a list of transition names. Good cutting is a hundred small decisions in service of a single story intent; the methodology below is the discipline that turns those decisions from impulses into craft.

Trigger this skill when the input describes:

- A scene the editor is building and wants critiqued or planned.
- A sequence with mixed coverage and uncertainty about which angle to live on.
- A montage that needs structure beyond "fast cuts to music."
- An interview-with-b-roll passage where the cut-aways feel arbitrary.
- Phrases like "cut on action," "j cut," "l cut," "match cut," "180 rule," "eyeline," "pacing," "scene editing," "where do I cut."

Do not trigger this skill when the user wants:

- The whole-pipeline plan (use the video-editing-pipeline-architect skill).
- Sync mechanics for multicam clips (use the multicam-and-proxy-workflow skill).
- A handoff to sound (use the sound-design-handoff-architect skill).
- Pure visual effects or color decisions — those are downstream of the cut.

If the input is a finished cut and the user wants validation, ask a sharper question: what is the scene trying to do, and what are you uncertain about? The methodology below works best when applied to specific decisions, not whole scenes blanket-checked.

## How to apply

Work through the steps in order. The output is decision-by-decision, not impressionistic.

### Step 1. State the scene's job

Before placing any cut, write one sentence on what the scene is doing in the story. The sentence has three parts:

- **Whose perspective leads.** Who the audience is closest to, emotionally, by the end of the scene.
- **What changes.** The information, relationship, or stakes that are different on the way out from on the way in. A scene without a change is a scene that should be cut.
- **What the audience leaves feeling.** One word, ideally — uneasy, hopeful, suspicious, vindicated, exhausted.

If any of the three is unclear, ask the user. Cutting before the scene's job is clear produces a scene that looks technically clean but never lands.

### Step 2. Audit the coverage against the job

Walk the available coverage. For each setup, label whether it serves the scene's job, serves a structural need (establishing space, resetting eyelines, providing a cutaway), or is decorative. Setups that are purely decorative should be the last resort, not the first.

Identify gaps in the coverage. Typical gaps:

- No clean establishing shot — the audience will not know where they are spatially.
- Both halves of a conversation covered close but no two-shot — no way to anchor the geometry.
- Coverage from only one side of the 180-degree axis — limits cutting freedom.
- No insert of the object the scene is about — the audience cannot see what the characters see.

Name the gaps. Sometimes the cut plan will work around them; sometimes the user will need to admit a pickup shoot or accept a structural limitation. Either is honest; ignoring the gap is not.

### Step 3. Establish the geometry

Before placing cuts inside the scene, commit to the spatial geometry the audience will read:

- **The 180-degree axis.** Draw an imaginary line between the two principal subjects (or between the protagonist and the direction of their attention). Plan that the camera stays on one side of that line for the scene, so all cuts maintain left-right consistency. If a take crosses the line, decide whether to exclude it, use a neutral reset shot (a centered straight-on, or a clear movement across the line on camera) before the offending angle, or accept the cross with creative intent.
- **Screen direction.** A character moving left-to-right in one cut continues moving left-to-right in the next unless something in the scene motivates a flip. Action sequences live or die on screen-direction discipline.
- **Eyelines.** Character A's gaze must hit a plausible position where character B is, and vice versa. Off-eyeline cuts produce that subtle "wrong" feeling even when no audience member can articulate why.

State the axis explicitly in the cut plan. "Camera right of the axis; character A faces left-to-right; character B faces right-to-left." The discipline keeps the rest of the work coherent.

### Step 4. Sketch the pacing curve

Cut length is the strongest pacing tool in editing. Sketch the pacing intent as a shape:

- **Establishing.** Longer cuts. The audience needs time to read space and characters. Three to seven seconds is common for a non-action scene's opening shot.
- **Escalation.** Cuts shorten as stakes rise. Move from medium-shot rhythm to closer coverage and tighter intervals.
- **Climax / pivot.** The shortest cuts (or, paradoxically, the longest single shot) of the scene. Both can work; the wrong choice is "the same rhythm as before."
- **Release.** Cuts lengthen. The audience needs a beat to absorb what happened.

A useful constraint: a scene's cut-length envelope should change by at least a factor of three from longest to shortest. A scene cut at uniform rhythm reads as flat regardless of content.

### Step 5. Place cuts on motivation

For every cut, name what it is "on." Acceptable motivations:

- **Cut on action.** The cut hits during a physical movement (a head turn, a hand reach, a step). Movement covers the cut; the audience reads continuity rather than discontinuity. Default for action and many dialog scenes.
- **Cut on a look.** The cut goes from a character looking to what they see (or to the next character reacting to them). One of the most reliable narrative beats in editing — the look is the question, the cut is the answer.
- **Cut on a line.** The cut lands at the end of a phrase, often before the last beat of breath. Combine with a J-cut: the next character's voice starts under the outgoing image, and the cut to their face arrives slightly behind. The conversation feels natural rather than ping-pong.
- **Cut on a beat.** In montage and rhythmic sequences, the cut lands on a musical accent. Use sparingly outside montage; in dialog, music-driven cuts pull the audience out of the conversation.
- **Cut on a motivated sound.** A door slams off-screen; cut to the door. Sound that motivates a picture cut is one of editing's quietest, most powerful tools.

Cuts that are on nothing — placed because "we have been on this angle a while" — are the most common amateur mistake. Refuse to plan them.

### Step 6. Place J-cuts and L-cuts in dialog

For any dialog scene, the cut pattern has two layers — picture cuts and audio cuts — and they should rarely coincide. Use J-cuts (audio from the next clip arrives before the picture cut) and L-cuts (audio from the outgoing clip continues over the next picture) liberally:

- **J-cut into a reaction.** Speaker B's first word starts under Speaker A's tail; cut to B mid-word. The cut feels like the audience leans toward B.
- **L-cut for emotional weight.** Speaker A finishes the sentence, then we hold on their face for a beat, then we cut to B. The hold tells the audience what to feel about what was just said.
- **Hard sync cut.** Reserve for moments of disruption — an interruption, a slap, a revelation. The audience reads the sync match as a hit; use it on purpose.

Aim for a rough ratio: in a six-cut dialog passage, expect three to four J-cuts or L-cuts, one or two hard syncs, and at most one straight cut without audio overlap.

### Step 7. Discipline b-roll and cutaways

B-roll exists to do narrative work, not to hide the cut. Three legitimate jobs for a cutaway:

- **Showing what the speaker is describing or remembering.** The b-roll motivates the cut by content.
- **Compressing time.** A cutaway lets the editor return to a tighter version of the speaker without an obvious jump.
- **Modulating pace.** A cutaway lets a long stretch of talking head breathe by adding texture.

Illegitimate jobs:

- Hiding a bad performance moment with arbitrary scenery.
- Filling time the speaker doesn't fill.
- Looking pretty.

For every cutaway, state which legitimate job it does. If none, cut the cutaway. The viewer's attention has a budget; spend it on shots that mean something.

### Step 8. Plan match cuts and montage transitions deliberately

Match cuts (shape, motion, sound, or composition match across the cut) are powerful because they imply a relationship the script may not state. Plan them deliberately:

- Identify the two ideas being linked.
- Identify the visual or auditory hook that carries the audience across (a shape, a movement, an object, a sound).
- Confirm the cut serves the story, not just craft. A match cut that exists for its own beauty often reads as showing off.

Montage sequences need their own structure: an A→B→C progression where each chunk is doing something different (introducing, escalating, paying off), even if the cutting feels fast. A flat montage of "stuff" is unwatchable; a montage with internal structure compresses time without losing the audience.

### Step 9. Audit the cut plan against continuity

Before finalizing, walk through the plan and check:

- **180-degree axis.** Every cut respects the chosen side, or has a documented neutral reset.
- **Eyelines.** Every cut between two characters has matching gaze directions.
- **Screen direction.** Every cut involving motion preserves direction or has documented intent in flipping.
- **Time continuity.** No accidental jumps that imply more time has passed than the scene supports. Conversely, no cuts that imply less time than the audience needs to absorb a beat.
- **Audio continuity.** Room tone or ambient bed continues across cuts within the scene; the absence of bed reads as a hard cut. Plan for the sound designer to extend, but mark which cuts need it explicitly.

### Step 10. Produce alternative passes

Deliver at least one alternative for the scene — typically a longer, more contemplative version (cuts later, holds on faces, fewer cutaways) and a shorter, more aggressive version (cuts earlier, more cutaways, tighter audio). The alternative is for the conversation with the director or producer; it is also a hedge against the primary plan landing wrong.

For each alternative, name what it gains and what it costs in one sentence. "Longer pass: gains weight on the moment of recognition; costs three seconds of total runtime and may slow the act break."

### Step 11. State what you will not decide

The methodology produces a plan, not a finished cut. Name what only the editor and director can decide in the room:

- The exact frame of each cut. Plans say "cut on her head turn"; the editor finds the frame.
- Take selection among multiple performances of the same line. The cut plan can say "favor the alt where she breaks eye contact"; the editor chooses.
- The pace of dialog inside a line (tightening pauses, shortening breaths). This is a within-clip decision, not a cut decision, and benefits from the editor's ear.

## Inputs

- `scene_or_sequence_brief` (required): the scene's story job.
- `coverage_available` (required): what is in the bin to cut from.
- `scene_type` (optional): dialog, action, montage, interview, etc.
- `pacing_intent` (optional): the felt shape the scene should produce.
- `surrounding_context` (optional): the scenes before and after.
- `hard_constraints` (optional): fixed shots, bad takes, duration targets.

## Outputs

- `cut_plan`: a scene-walk with specific cut placements and motivations.
- `pacing_curve`: a simple shape of cut length across the scene.
- `continuity_audit`: a check against axis, eyelines, screen direction, time, and audio continuity.
- `alternative_passes`: one or two alternate approaches.

## Examples

### Example 1 — a two-hander dialog scene in a kitchen

Brief: "Two siblings, late thirties, arguing about whether to sell their parents' house. By the end of the scene, the audience should feel that the younger sibling has lost ground but is not ready to admit it. Their relationship is the real subject; the house is the surface."

Coverage available: "Two-shot from camera right of the breakfast bar; matched single on each, medium close-up; a wider establishing of the kitchen with practical action (coffee pot); cutaways of the coffee, two mugs, an old photograph stuck to the fridge."

Cut plan: open on the establishing wide, hold three seconds while the coffee pours — establishes space and gives audio a chance to lead. J-cut to the two-shot as the older sibling starts the conversation; her line lands on the cut from the two-shot to her single. L-cut on her last syllable, hold on her, then cut to the younger sibling's reaction (cut on look, not on line). Inside the scene's escalation, alternate singles with two short cutaways to the photograph — they motivate the conversation about the parents without naming it. Climax: the younger sibling delivers their concession-line on a J-cut that arrives before we see them — the audience hears the line on the older sibling's face, then we cut. Release: hold on the older sibling for two seconds longer than the rhythm of the scene wants. Cut out on the photograph.

Pacing curve: open 3s, descend to 2s, then to 1.5s through escalation, the climax J-cut is on a 0.8s rhythm, then the hold on the older sibling is 4s — the longest cut of the scene at its end. Envelope ratio is 5:1, well within the methodology's threshold.

Continuity audit: axis stays on the right of the breakfast bar; eyelines confirmed by the matched singles; the photograph cutaway is a centered neutral and does not violate axis; audio bed (kitchen fan, refrigerator hum) carries through every cut.

Alternative passes: longer pass holds on the wide for five seconds and cuts to two-shot only at the first sharp word; gains the patience the scene needs but loses three seconds of runtime. Shorter pass drops the photograph cutaways and lives entirely in singles; gains aggression but loses the parents-are-here subtext.

### Example 2 — a 90-second montage compressing two days

Brief: "A character training for a competition. The montage compresses two days into 90 seconds. The audience should leave understanding the character has the skill and is exhausted."

Coverage: "Wide establishing of the gym; medium of the character at three different exercises; close-ups of hands, feet, the chalk bucket; a coach figure in the background; a wall clock; sweat-on-towel; the gym empty at end of day."

Cut plan: structure as three movements — entry (six cuts, longer holds, establishing skill exists), escalation (twelve cuts, sharper rhythm, intercut close-ups of effort with mediums of execution), exhaustion (five cuts, longer again, lingering on the empty gym and the sweat-on-towel). Match-cut a wipe of sweat from one exercise to the next as a structural device that signals "this is montage, time is fluid." Cut on physical beats — feet hitting, hands gripping, a weight dropping — not on musical accents alone. Music should support cuts, not dictate them. The wall clock appears twice: once at the end of movement one, once at the start of movement three. The repetition tells the audience "this is the next day" without a title card.

Pacing curve: 2.5s average in movement one, 0.8s in movement two, back to 2s in movement three. End on a 4s hold of the empty gym.

Continuity audit: screen direction of the character's movement preserved within each exercise; cuts between exercises are not continuity cuts and so do not need direction match. The coach figure is a continuity anchor — the audience can re-read the space when he appears.

Alternative passes: longer contemplative version replaces the second appearance of the wall clock with a sustained shot of the character resting alone; gains exhaustion but costs the temporal clarity. Aggressive version drops the empty-gym ending and lands on the towel-on-floor instead; gains punch but loses release.

## Limitations

- The methodology assumes adequate coverage. If the coverage cannot support the scene's job, the cut plan can only mitigate, not save — and should say so plainly.
- The skill cannot watch the footage. Cut placements are described against the user's description; the editor must verify each frame in the timeline. A "cut on her head turn" plan is only as good as the head turn in the take.
- Cultural conventions about screen direction and eyeline grammar are not universal. The 180-degree convention is dominant in Western narrative film but is broken intentionally in some traditions and avant-garde work; the methodology should adapt when the user names a different convention.
- Documentary cutting often breaks continuity by design — jump cuts in interviews, intentional crossing of the line. The skill can apply the methodology and then explicitly relax the continuity audit; do not force narrative-fiction grammar onto material that doesn't want it.
- Music-driven cutting in trailers, music videos, and short-form social is a different methodology with different priorities. The skill can adapt, but the user should flag the form.
- Final cut decisions are collaborative between editor, director, and producer. The methodology is a planning tool, not an authority. Where the room disagrees with the plan, the room wins.
