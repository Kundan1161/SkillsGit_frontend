# Embedding Data Lifecycle Architect

## When to use

Use this skill when an organisation has stored vector embeddings and needs a written lifecycle policy: what enters the index, how it is identified and tagged, when it gets re-embedded, when and how it gets deleted, what relationship the index has to the model that produced it, and how the index evolves when that model changes. The output is a policy document plus a structured representation suitable for embedding into operational runbooks and data-governance registers.

This skill is appropriate when the index has crossed the prototype threshold: scale is large enough that "rebuild it" is not free, regulatory pressure makes data lifecycle a compliance concern, and at least one model upgrade is foreseeable. It complements the vector-index operations runbook (which executes specific procedures) by setting the *policy* under which procedures are chosen.

It does not produce architecture decisions about vector store selection (use the vector DB architecture designer) or embedding-model choice (use the embedding-model selector). It assumes those decisions exist and writes the policy that governs the data inside them.

PII handling is a topic this skill addresses at the policy level — what enters the index, how it is tagged, how it is removed. The skill produces governance language; legally binding interpretations of any specific data-protection regime must be confirmed with qualified counsel for the jurisdiction in question.

## How to apply

The skill walks a fifteen-stage policy-design pipeline. Stages 1-3 frame the corpus and the regulatory surface; stages 4-8 design the ingestion and tagging policy; stages 9-12 design the versioning and re-embedding policy; stages 13-14 design the model-upgrade and shadow-index strategies; stage 15 assembles the deliverable.

### Stage 1 — Characterise the corpus

1. Restate the corpus in one paragraph: type of content (long-form documents, support tickets, product descriptions, code, user-authored messages, conversation transcripts), scale in objects and in vectors (those are different — a document chunks into many vectors), and ingestion velocity (events per second, batches per day).
2. Identify the **change profile** of source content. Some corpora are append-only (web articles, sensor logs). Some mutate in place (knowledge-base articles, product descriptions). Some have time-bound content (event listings, ticket conversations that close). The change profile dictates re-embedding cadence and retention defaults.
3. Identify the **sensitivity profile** of source content. Does it contain direct identifiers (names, emails, phone numbers, account ids)? Indirect identifiers (location plus timestamp, employer plus role)? Special categories (health, finance, biometric, minors)? The sensitivity profile dictates the PII policy in Stage 4.

### Stage 2 — Map the regulatory surface

4. List the jurisdictions whose data subjects can be represented in the corpus. Where the corpus contains identifiers attached to natural persons in a jurisdiction with a data-protection regime, that regime applies to the embeddings as well as to the source content — derived data is not exempt.
5. For each regime, capture the operational obligations the policy must satisfy: right to access, right to rectification, right to erasure (with its specific window), data-portability, breach-notification timelines, cross-border transfer constraints, retention limits.
6. Translate obligations into capabilities the index must provide: deterministic mapping from data-subject identity to all owning vector ids, ability to delete a vector inside the erasure window, ability to re-derive a vector after a source rectification, ability to produce an export of a data subject's contribution to the index.
7. Note where the regulatory regime is unsettled with respect to vector embeddings specifically. Some authorities treat the embedding as personal data when reversible to source content (and most chunked embeddings are reversible-enough by similarity inversion); others treat the embedding alone as anonymised. The policy assumes the conservative interpretation by default and names where a legal review is needed to relax.

### Stage 3 — Define data classes

8. Define a small set of data classes the policy will refer to: **public** (no person attached, public content), **internal** (corporate content, not personal), **personal** (linked to an identifiable natural person), **sensitive personal** (special categories), **secret** (must never enter the index — credentials, keys, regulated content of certain categories).
9. State the mapping from source object to data class. The mapping is deterministic and is computed at ingestion time, not retrieval time. Each vector carries its class tag in payload.
10. State the **secret detection** pipeline at ingestion: regex and entropy checks for credentials, allow-list of permissible identifier patterns, hard reject for anything flagged. The policy treats failure of secret detection as a security incident, not a data-quality issue.

### Stage 4 — PII handling at ingestion

11. **De-identification policy.** For content classified personal or sensitive personal, run a named-entity recognition and redaction pass before embedding. Replace identifiers with placeholders that preserve semantic role ("person_name_1", "email_1") so the embedding retains useful signal without storing the literal identifier.
12. **Reversible versus irreversible de-identification.** If the consumer system must be able to display the original content (a support-ticket search that surfaces the ticket), keep the original source identifier in a separate access-controlled store and join at display time, never at embedding time. If display is not needed, the original content is deleted from the system after embedding.
13. **Embedding-as-identifier risk.** Even after redaction, an embedding can act as a quasi-identifier — uniqueness and similarity together can re-link. The policy states the mitigations: avoid embedding very short or unique strings, prefer chunking that mixes content, and treat the vector itself as personal data when the source was personal data even after redaction.
14. **Payload minimisation.** Vector-store payloads (the metadata stored alongside the vector) carry only what the consumer needs at query time. Free-text excerpts in payload re-introduce the identifiers redacted at embedding time. The policy lists permitted payload fields and forbids others.
15. **Tenant and jurisdiction isolation.** Where the corpus spans tenants or jurisdictions, the index is partitioned by tenant and jurisdiction. Queries cross-partition only through an explicit, audited path. A single global index is forbidden when cross-border transfer is restricted.

### Stage 5 — Vector-to-source mapping

16. **Source-to-vector ledger.** Maintain an explicit mapping table outside the vector index: source_record_id, chunk_id, vector_id, model_version, embedded_at, source_hash, data_class. The ledger is the system of record for "what is in the index" and is the primary lookup for erasure.
17. **Source hashing.** Store a content hash of each chunk. The hash is the change-detection key: if a source is re-ingested and the hash matches, the existing vector is reused. The hash is computed on normalised content (whitespace, casing, boilerplate removed) so trivial edits do not trigger churn.
18. **Many-to-many embeddings.** A single source can produce many vectors (chunks, multiple representations, multiple models). The ledger is many-to-many on (source, vector). Erasure cascades through all vectors a source owns; re-embedding refreshes them in a single transaction.
19. **Sealed vector ids.** Vector ids are allocated by the system, are immutable, and are not reused after deletion. Consumer caches that key on vector id are safe to rely on the id never silently changing meaning.

### Stage 6 — Retention policy

20. **Time-to-live per data class.** State the default retention per class: public content has no system-imposed TTL, internal content has a configurable TTL aligned with the source's lifecycle, personal content has a TTL aligned with the lawful basis for processing.
21. **Activity-based retention.** Some content is kept while in active use and deleted on dormancy (closed support tickets after N years, departed-employee content after offboarding). The policy names the trigger and the grace period.
22. **Legal-hold overrides.** Where a legal hold applies, retention is paused for the held subset and resumed when the hold lifts. The policy specifies how holds are applied to the vector ledger and how they are audited.
23. **Soft delete and hard delete.** A two-phase delete is standard: soft delete (the vector is excluded from queries, payload is scrubbed, the ledger row is marked deleted) and hard delete (the vector and ledger row are physically removed). The window between them allows recovery from accidental deletion; it must not exceed the regulatory erasure window minus a safety margin.

### Stage 7 — Right-to-erasure workflow

24. **Erasure intake.** Erasure requests arrive through the organisation's data-subject-rights desk and are translated to a structured cascade: identify the data subject's identifiers, look up owning sources, look up owning vectors via the ledger, schedule the deletes, confirm completion, notify the data subject inside the regulatory window.
25. **Cascade fan-out.** A single request can implicate vectors across many tenants, many shards, many indexes (live, shadow, snapshot). The cascade must enumerate every place a vector lives and confirm delete from each.
26. **Snapshots and backups.** Snapshots and backups are part of the lifecycle: a vector deleted from the live index may still live in a backup. The policy specifies the policy: either backups are within scope of the erasure cascade (preferred), or the regulator-acceptable practice (delete-on-restore with documented procedure) is named and justified.
27. **Audit of erasure.** Each erasure event is recorded in an append-only audit log with: request id, scope of identifiers, vectors deleted, snapshots affected, timestamps, operator. Erasure completion is a compliance artifact and must be queryable.
28. **Erasure SLA.** State the internal SLA inside the regulatory window — typically targeting two-thirds of the regulatory window to leave slack for exceptional cases. Operations are accountable to the internal SLA.

### Stage 8 — Drift in source content

29. **Source-change detection.** When a source object is updated, the source-to-vector ledger is consulted. If the new content hash differs, the owning vectors are scheduled for re-embedding.
30. **Re-embedding cadence policy.** Three modes: **eager** (re-embed immediately on source change, suitable for hot content), **batched** (collect changes and re-embed on a schedule, suitable for medium-traffic content), **lazy** (re-embed only on query miss or on-demand, suitable for cold content). The policy specifies which mode applies to which data class and content type.
31. **Chunking stability across edits.** A small source edit should not invalidate every chunk — chunk boundaries should be stable under small perturbations. Use semantic or anchor-based chunking with stable identifiers per chunk; document the chunker version as part of the ledger.
32. **Update-versus-replace policy.** When a source is small and changes are large, replace all owning vectors. When the source is large and the change is local, update only the affected chunks. The threshold is the chunk count and the change ratio; the policy gives concrete numbers.

### Stage 9 — Embedding-model version pinning

33. **Identity of an index.** An index is defined by the tuple (model_id, model_version, normalisation, chunking_strategy, distance_metric). Two indexes that differ in any of these are not interchangeable; their vectors are not comparable.
34. **Version pinning.** Every vector carries its (model_id, model_version) tag in the ledger and ideally in payload. Queries against the index are by definition against a specific tuple; mixing model versions inside a single query returns nonsense.
35. **Hosted-model silent upgrade risk.** Hosted embedding APIs sometimes silently change the model behind a stable name. The policy requires periodic equivalence checks against a fixed evaluation set; a drift over a small tolerance triggers a versioned re-pin and a planned re-embed.
36. **Self-hosted model freeze.** For self-hosted models, the model weights, tokenizer, and inference runtime are pinned and snapshot-able. The policy names the storage of the pinned artifact and the redeploy procedure.

### Stage 10 — Live, shadow, and snapshot indexes

37. **Index roles.** The policy distinguishes three index roles: **live** (serves consumer traffic), **shadow** (a parallel index against which live traffic can be replayed in shadow mode for evaluation), **snapshot** (a point-in-time copy for backup or audit). Each has different retention, security, and erasure obligations.
38. **Shadow index purpose.** A shadow index exists to evaluate a model upgrade, an index hyperparameter change, or a chunking change before it touches consumers. Shadow indexes hold real personal data and are inside the same governance perimeter as live indexes.
39. **Shadow lifecycle.** A shadow index is created on demand, populated by replaying source content through the new pipeline, evaluated against a fixed evaluation set, then either promoted (to live) or destroyed. Destruction is treated as a hard delete of all vectors and the ledger entries associated.

### Stage 11 — Dual-index cutover for model upgrades

40. **Cutover preconditions.** Before starting a cutover: the new model is pinned and benchmarked, the new chunking and normalisation are frozen, the new index is sized, an evaluation harness exists with the success and failure metrics from the architecture decision, the right-to-erasure cascade is updated to include the new index.
41. **Backfill from source.** Re-embedding goes through the source-of-truth, not through translation of existing vectors. Embedding spaces are not interchangeable; "translating" a vector from one model to another is not supported.
42. **Dual-write window.** During backfill and the subsequent ramp, every new source ingestion is embedded by both the old and the new model. Dual-write ends only after cutover plus a cooldown that allows fast rollback.
43. **Shadow read evaluation.** Before any consumer traffic moves, shadow-evaluate the new index by replaying real queries (with appropriate access controls) and measuring recall, latency, and result-set overlap.
44. **Graduated traffic shift.** Ramp consumer traffic to the new index in stages (typical: 1 percent, 10 percent, 50 percent, 100 percent) with hold periods sized to detect regressions in the metrics defined in the cutover preconditions.
45. **Cooldown.** After 100 percent cutover, keep the old index queryable but read-idle for a documented cooldown (typically two weeks). The cooldown is the fast-rollback path; cutting it short trades safety for storage.
46. **Tear-down.** Tear-down of the old index is the last step and is irreversible. The policy requires an explicit sign-off, a snapshot of the old index retained for the regulatory-audit window, and a final verification that the ledger no longer references the old (model_id, model_version) tuple for any live row.

### Stage 12 — Cross-cutting concerns

47. **Encryption.** Vectors and payload are encrypted at rest. The policy names the key-management approach and the rotation cadence. Key rotation does not require re-embedding; it requires re-encryption of the storage layer.
48. **Access control.** Queries against the index are authenticated and authorised. The policy names the unit of access (per tenant, per data class, per consumer system) and the audit-log requirements per query.
49. **Quotas and rate limits.** Consumer systems have per-tenant rate limits to prevent enumeration attacks (where an adversary issues many queries to reconstruct the corpus). Rate-limit breaches are logged and alerted.
50. **Observability.** Per-tuple (model_id, model_version) metrics are exported: vector count, ledger consistency, average payload size, query rate, erasure backlog. Without per-tuple visibility a multi-version environment becomes opaque.

### Stage 13 — Operational drills

51. **Erasure drill.** Quarterly, run an end-to-end erasure drill with a synthetic data subject: every step from intake to verification is exercised, including snapshot cascade. A drill that takes longer than the internal SLA triggers a remediation.
52. **Model-drift drill.** Quarterly, run the equivalence check against the hosted-model baseline (Stage 9) and against the pinned self-hosted baseline. A drift beyond tolerance is a re-embed trigger.
53. **Restore drill.** Annually, restore a snapshot of the index into a non-production environment, verify a sanity query set, and exercise the right-to-erasure cascade against the restored snapshot. A snapshot that fails restore is a snapshot that does not exist.
54. **Cutover dry-run.** Before any planned model upgrade, dry-run the cutover in staging end to end, including rollback. Production cutover cannot start until the dry-run has passed.

### Stage 14 — Roles and accountability

55. **Data steward.** Owns the ledger, the data-class definitions, the retention defaults, and the erasure SLA. Decides on retention exceptions.
56. **Model owner.** Owns the embedding-model pinning, the equivalence-check cadence, and the upgrade backlog. Decides on model upgrades.
57. **Index operator.** Owns the live, shadow, and snapshot indexes; executes runbooks; is on call for index health. Decides on operational tuning.
58. **Compliance liaison.** Owns the regulatory-surface mapping, the audit-log review cadence, and the regulator-facing extracts. Decides on regulatory interpretation, with counsel.
59. The policy names the role responsible for each section so handoffs do not get lost.

### Stage 15 — Compose the deliverable

60. Render the policy as a single document organised by stage. Each section ends with the responsible role and the operational SLA.
61. Emit `lifecycle_policy_json` with the fields enumerated under `outputs`. Each field carries a "owner" tag.
62. End with an "open questions" section that lists assumptions the policy makes that require legal or executive confirmation, and a "change log" placeholder for future revisions to the policy itself.

## Examples

**Input (placeholder):**

`corpus_profile`: "Customer support knowledge plus customer ticket archive. 30 million chunks across 5 million source documents. Ticket archive mutates as customers reply (eager-mode for hot tickets, lazy for closed). Knowledge base mutates weekly. Source content contains customer names, emails, account ids, occasionally health-adjacent disclosures."

`model_inventory`: "Hosted API embedding model at 1536 dimensions in production; planning to evaluate a self-hosted alternative at 1024 dimensions later this year. No second model currently in use."

`regulatory_constraints`: "GDPR applies to EU customers. CCPA applies to California customers. Right-to-erasure window 30 days. Data residency: EU customer data must not leave the EU region."

`consumer_systems`: "Internal support-agent search UI (latency tolerant up to 500 ms p95). Customer-facing self-service chat (latency budget 200 ms p95). Both can fail over to keyword search if vector lookup is unavailable."

**Policy (abbreviated):**

- Data classes: knowledge content is internal; ticket bodies are personal; flagged health disclosures are sensitive personal. Per-class TTL: knowledge has no system TTL; closed-ticket vectors expire 24 months after closure; sensitive content expires sooner per Stage 6 defaults.
- PII handling: NER redaction at ingestion for tickets; original ticket bodies stored in an access-controlled support-ticket store and joined at display time; vector payload contains only chunk-level non-identifying metadata.
- Source-to-vector ledger maintained in a relational store; content hash on normalised text drives change detection.
- Re-embedding: eager for open tickets, batched daily for the knowledge base, lazy on query miss for closed tickets.
- Model pinning: monthly equivalence check against a 500-pair evaluation set; tolerance 1.5 percent overlap drift; breach triggers re-pin and full re-embed of affected partitions.
- Dual-index cutover plan for the planned upgrade: backfill from source, dual-write for 6 weeks, shadow read for 1 week, graduated ramp over 2 weeks, cooldown 2 weeks, tear-down with sign-off.
- Erasure: 14-day internal SLA. Cascade covers live index, shadow indexes (if any), most recent 7 daily snapshots; older snapshots covered by delete-on-restore documented procedure.
- Index tenancy: separate indexes per residency region. Cross-region queries forbidden by infrastructure policy.

## Limitations

- The skill produces policy, not implementation. Translating policy into pipeline code, ledger schema, and runbooks is downstream work.
- Regulatory interpretation in the policy reflects general patterns; binding legal interpretation in any jurisdiction must come from qualified counsel.
- The skill assumes the corpus and the embedding pipeline are under the organisation's control. Embeddings produced by uncontrolled third parties (federated embedding markets, public model APIs that retain inputs) require additional contractual controls outside this skill's scope.
- The "embedding is personal data" question is unsettled in some regimes. The skill assumes the conservative interpretation; relaxing it must be a documented legal decision.
- The skill cannot estimate the absolute cost of re-embedding without specifics; cost estimates are relative to the policy's cadence choices.
- Very small deployments (under a million vectors, single tenant, no personal data) are over-served by this policy; the skill should detect this and recommend a stripped-down version.
- The dual-index cutover playbook assumes a source-of-truth for content exists outside the index. Deployments where the vector store is the only copy of the source content cannot perform a safe cutover and must reshape the architecture first.
