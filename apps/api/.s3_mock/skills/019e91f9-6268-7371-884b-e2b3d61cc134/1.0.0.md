# AI Video Shot Architect

## When to use

Use this skill when a creative team is about to generate a single AI video shot — or a tightly bounded handful of shots that will be cut together — and wants a deliberate plan rather than a series of rerolls. The skill produces the shot design, the first-pass prompt, two variant probes for the second pass, and the narrowing loop that turns a rough intent into a usable plate inside a tractable generation budget. It is the right skill for product teasers, social spots, music-video inserts, narrative B-roll, concept proofs, and the cutaway shots in a longer edit. It is not the right skill for full multi-shot scenes with continuous identity across cuts (use the consistency architect for that) or for the post pipeline that turns these plates into a finished deliverable (use the post-integration skill for that).

This skill produces methodology guidance for AI video generation. The skill does not address copyright status of training data or generated outputs in any jurisdiction; consult counsel for commercial use. The skill explicitly does not produce or recommend non-consensual likeness use (deepfakes), CSAM, defamatory content, or content that violates platform policy or applicable law.

The skill names a frontier hosted video-diffusion model generically and gives the open-source families by repository names where the methodology differs in important ways. The reasoning in the body applies to any modern video-diffusion model whose conditioning surface is some combination of text, reference image, reference video, and a small set of sampling knobs.

## How to apply

The skill walks the shot design in seven decisions. Each decision names the chosen position with rationale, names the alternatives with the conditions under which they are preferred, and contributes to the final prompt draft and the narrowing plan.

### Decision 1 — The subject sentence

1. The subject sentence is the irreducible nucleus of the prompt. It names the subject, the subject's state, and the subject's relationship to the camera. Everything else in the prompt is a modifier on this sentence.
2. Three useful templates for the subject sentence are: "A [subject] [action] in [environment]"; "[Subject], [pose or state], [subject's relationship to the camera]"; and the image-to-video case "The subject in the input frame [does the action]". The first is the workhorse, the second improves the chance of correct framing on text-only generation, and the third hands the model the identity and asks only for motion.
3. The subject sentence is written to be visible. Avoid abstractions ("contemplative", "yearning", "majestic") that the model has to translate. Replace abstractions with the visible behaviour that produces them: "looking down", "turning slowly toward the camera", "shoulders dropping".
4. Two subjects in the same shot are tractable when one is clearly primary and one is contextual ("a man walks past a parked motorcycle"). Two co-equal subjects with their own actions are a common failure mode — the model tends to merge them or drop one. Break a co-equal two-subject shot into a sequence of two shots and cut between them.
5. The subject sentence is locked before the camera, the lighting, or the style are written. Edits to the subject sentence cascade through every other layer of the prompt; edits to the other layers do not cascade back.

### Decision 2 — The action verb and its motion intensity

1. The action verb determines how much temporal change the model has to invent across the shot's frames. Frontier hosted models have wider tolerance here than smaller open-source models, but the same rule applies everywhere: name one primary action.
2. Three motion-intensity tiers are useful. **Subtle** — breathing, blinking, hair moving in wind, water rippling, smoke drifting. The model holds composition well; identity drift is the dominant risk. **Moderate** — walking, turning, reaching, a head turn, a glance. The model has to track the subject through translation; expect occasional limb morphs at the edges. **Aggressive** — running, jumping, dancing, fighting, a hand catching something, complex object interaction. The model frequently fails on aggressive motion in short shots; treat the result as a lottery and budget more generations.
3. The action verb is written in the present tense and is unambiguous. "Walks toward the camera" beats "approaches"; "raises a glass" beats "celebrates". Multiple sequential actions in a single shot ("walks in, looks around, sits down") are almost always a mistake — the model picks one and ignores the others, or interpolates badly between them. Decompose into separate shots.
4. For image-to-video, the action verb is the only motion signal. The reference frame carries the subject, the environment, and the style; the verb is what the model adds. Keep the verb modest relative to the model's coherence horizon — if the horizon is four seconds, a four-second sprint is a stretch; a four-second sit-down is comfortable.

### Decision 3 — The camera move and lens feel

1. Camera vocabulary that modern video-diffusion models respond to reliably: "static shot", "slow push-in", "dolly in", "dolly out", "tracking shot", "pan left", "pan right", "tilt up", "tilt down", "orbit around the subject", "handheld", "locked off". Naming the move plainly outperforms naming the equipment ("dolly" beats "Steadicam-style move on a sliding rig").
2. Lens-feel vocabulary that lands: "wide angle", "medium shot", "close-up", "extreme close-up", "shallow depth of field", "deep depth of field", "anamorphic", "telephoto compression", "fisheye". These are framing cues, not literal optics — the model reproduces the visual character associated with them in its training set.
3. Combine one camera move with one lens-feel cue. Two camera moves in one shot ("dolly in then pan right") almost always fail; the model picks one or smears between them. If the brief calls for two moves, that is two shots with a cut between them — that is a storyboard concern, addressed in Decision 6.
4. Frontier hosted models track camera direction better than open-source DiT and AnimateDiff-style models. For the open-source families, prefer "tracking shot, the subject in motion" over "tracking shot of the subject" — the former lets the model resolve the motion as subject motion if the camera-tracking interpretation fails.
5. Camera intensity should match motion intensity. A static camera over aggressive subject motion is forgiving; aggressive camera motion over aggressive subject motion is the hardest case for the model and the most expensive to land.

### Decision 4 — Lighting, environment, and time

1. Lighting cues that translate well: "golden hour", "blue hour", "overcast", "harsh midday sun", "soft north light", "candlelight", "neon", "moonlight", "rim light from behind", "key light from the left", "high contrast", "low contrast", "soft shadows", "hard shadows". Name the kind of light and one quality of it.
2. Environment cues are concrete nouns and qualities: "a wet asphalt street", "a kitchen with morning light", "a forest path with mist". The environment also implies the lighting; when the two conflict ("a kitchen with morning light, at night"), the model picks one and the result is unpredictable. Resolve conflicts in the brief before writing the prompt.
3. Time-of-day cues should be at the level of "morning", "midday", "dusk", "night" rather than clock times. The model has no clock; it has training-set associations.
4. Weather and atmosphere are powerful cheap motion: "fog drifting", "rain falling", "snow drifting through frame", "steam rising", "dust in the light beam" all give the shot kinetic texture without putting load on the subject motion. Use them when the subject motion is subtle and the shot would otherwise feel static.

### Decision 5 — Style anchors and rendered look

1. Style anchors place the shot in a visual tradition. The cheapest reliable anchors are medium and era cues: "shot on 35mm film", "shot on 16mm film", "shot on a Bolex", "VHS-era footage", "shot on a modern digital cinema camera", "shot on a smartphone". These are stronger than reaching for named artists or named films and avoid the trademark exposure of style-by-name.
2. Grade cues complement medium cues: "warm, low-contrast grade", "cool, high-contrast grade", "desaturated", "saturated", "teal-and-orange grade", "natural skin tones", "muted earth tones". One medium cue plus one grade cue is the right density.
3. For animation, name the technique generically: "2D cel animation", "stop-motion", "claymation", "rotoscoped", "3D rendered, stylised", "photoreal CGI". The model's stylistic priors here are strong; one cue is usually enough.
4. Style anchors interact with the conditioning mode. In image-to-video, the reference image already carries the style; adding style cues to the prompt often makes the result drift away from the reference. Either trust the reference or write a prompt whose style cues match the reference and accept a small drift toward those cues.

### Decision 6 — Length budget and the cut plan

1. Each video-diffusion model has a coherence horizon — the duration past which subject identity, background structure, and motion all start to degrade in characteristic ways. The horizon is shorter for older or smaller models (one to two seconds for AnimateDiff-class) and longer for current frontier models (six to ten seconds).
2. The skill's rule is: never ask the model for a shot longer than two-thirds of its coherence horizon if the shot has to look clean. The remaining third is the safety margin against drift.
3. When the brief calls for a duration longer than the horizon allows, the deliverable is not one shot — it is a cut. Decompose into shots whose individual durations sit inside the budget, plan the cut points where the action visually punctuates (a footstep landing, a head finishing its turn, a door closing), and write the prompts as separate shots.
4. The cut points are designed to hide the discontinuities the model cannot avoid across generations. Cut on motion, cut on a re-establishing change of angle, or cut on a graphic match (a circular shape in the outgoing frame matched by a circular shape in the incoming frame). Do not attempt to chain consecutive generations of the same shot from the last frame of the previous generation unless the model explicitly supports last-frame conditioning; the drift compounds fast.
5. The cut plan is recorded in `shot_summary.narrowing_plan` as an ordered list of shots with their durations, the action that punctuates each cut, and the carry-over identity or environment notes that the consistency architect will use.

### Decision 7 — Conditioning recipe and the narrowing loop

1. The conditioning recipe names the input the model sees on generation one. Three common recipes: **text-only** (prompt + seed + cfg + sampler choice); **image-to-video** (reference frame + prompt + motion strength + cfg); **video-reference** (driving video for motion + prompt + structure-conditioning weight). A fourth — **structure-reference** — feeds a depth map, an edge map, or a pose track that constrains the composition without dictating the surface look.
2. Seed strategy is not "pick the lucky seed" — it is a search. For the first generation, pick three seeds and run all three at low cost (small step count, low resolution if the model supports preview mode). Look at which seed lands the composition; that is the seed for the narrowing pass at full step count and resolution. Seeds that produce visually different but equally on-brief outputs all stay in the bracket; seeds that produce broken composition (missing subject, wrong framing, anatomy errors) are eliminated immediately and not narrowed.
3. CFG / guidance scale controls how strictly the model obeys the prompt versus how natural the motion looks. Higher CFG fits the prompt more closely but produces over-saturated, brittle motion; lower CFG looks natural but drifts from the prompt. Useful starting ranges: 5.0 to 7.5 for frontier hosted models, 3.5 to 5.5 for open-source DiT, image-conditioning models often want lower CFG than text-only models. Where the model exposes per-frame CFG (linear CFG schedules), the convention is to start lower on the first frame and ramp toward the prompt as frames progress; this lets the model match the conditioning frame on frame one and re-assert the prompt as the shot unfolds.
4. The narrowing loop is the explicit search procedure. Pass one: three seeds at low cost, fixed prompt, fixed CFG midpoint. Pass two: the surviving seed at full cost, plus one prompt variant that tightens whichever axis was weakest on pass one (composition, motion, style), plus one CFG variant one step lower or higher than midpoint. Pass three: the surviving generation goes into post or back into the loop with a focused edit to the prompt — usually a swap of one word in the action verb or one lighting cue. Past three passes, the shot's brief is usually the problem, not the seed; revisit Decisions 1–5.
5. The narrowing loop is bounded by `budget_for_generations`. When the budget is small (three to five), pass one is the whole loop and pass two is a single confirmation. When the budget is larger (ten to twenty), passes two and three can each branch into multiple variants and a winner is picked from the bracket.
6. Step count and sampler choice are secondary knobs. More steps trade compute for image quality at diminishing returns past a model-specific knee point (often 30–50 steps); samplers vary in how aggressively they push the noise schedule. The narrowing loop holds these constant unless the team has reason to vary them. Vary one knob per pass; multi-variable variation makes the result attributable to nothing.
7. Common failure modes the narrowing loop catches: subject is present but the action is wrong (rewrite Decision 2's action verb); subject is right but the camera move is wrong (rewrite Decision 3's camera vocabulary or accept that two-move shots are not landing); style drifts toward a default look (strengthen the style anchor or accept the model's default); motion is correct but the composition is wrong (revise Decision 1's subject sentence to put the framing cue earlier in the prompt). Each failure mode has a specific revision; the loop is not "try harder", it is "revise the right line".

### Decision 8 — Iterative narrowing on a target shot

1. Once the narrowing loop has produced a survivor, the final pass is a fidelity check. The survivor is reviewed at delivery resolution and at delivery frame rate (the post-integration skill handles the uprez and conform). Common artefacts surface only at full resolution: hands with extra fingers, eyes that don't converge, hair that intersects with a hat, text in the background that the model invented.
2. Artefacts have three responses. **Accept** when the artefact is invisible at the playback context the piece will be seen in (a sixth finger in a quarter-frame B-roll cut is invisible at a phone-sized viewport). **Repair** when the artefact is localised and the post pipeline can mask, patch, or paint it out. **Regenerate** when the artefact is global or central and no repair will hide it.
3. The skill writes the regeneration decision back into the narrowing plan so the team does not regenerate the same shot with the same conditioning twice. Changing one variable per regeneration keeps the loop converging; changing several variables turns it into a reroll.

## Outputs

The skill emits three artifacts.

**Shot design.** A markdown document with one section per decision (1–7), recording the chosen position and the reasoning grounded in the brief.

**Prompt draft.** A model-agnostic first-pass prompt in the grammar Decisions 1–5 produced, plus two variants that probe motion-strength and lighting respectively for the second pass.

**Shot summary.** A JSON object with `subject`, `action`, `camera`, `lighting`, `style`, `duration_seconds`, `conditioning_mode`, `seed_strategy`, `cfg_guidance`, and `narrowing_plan` keys, machine-readable for handoff to a generation pipeline.

## Examples

A team is making a 6-second product teaser for a watch. The brief is "the watch sits on a moss-covered rock by a stream; the morning light catches the dial as the camera pushes in". The skill names the subject sentence ("The watch rests on a moss-covered rock beside a stream"), the action ("the second hand sweeps; the water moves"), the camera ("slow push-in, medium shot, shallow depth of field"), the lighting ("morning light, soft, warm, dappled through trees"), the style ("shot on 35mm film, natural grade, warm tones"), the length budget (one shot — 6 seconds fits inside the frontier-hosted horizon with margin), and the conditioning recipe (image-to-video off a hero still, low motion strength, CFG 6.0, three seeds). The prompt draft and the two variants are written out. The narrowing plan lists pass one as three seeds, pass two as the survivor plus a "lighting variant" with the lighting cue changed from "dappled" to "side-lit", and pass three as a final at full step count.

A second team wants 12 seconds of a character walking down a rainy street. The skill flags that 12 seconds exceeds the coherence horizon for a clean tracking shot. It decomposes into three shots: a wide establishing dolly-in (4 seconds), a medium tracking shot of the character (4 seconds), and a close-up of the character's face under a streetlight (4 seconds). Each shot gets its own prompt, the cut points are described, and the consistency-architect skill is named as the next step for carrying the character identity across the three shots.

A third team has a high-aggression brief: 3 seconds of a martial-arts kick from a low angle with rain spray. The skill names this as the worst case — aggressive subject motion, aggressive camera, weather, and a low angle that puts demand on the model's anatomical priors. Decision 1 locks the subject sentence with explicit body position and dominant leg. Decision 2 picks "moderate" rather than "aggressive" for the action ("the leg sweeps up and across the frame") to give the model a tractable verb. Decision 3 picks a low-angle static shot rather than a tracking shot. Decision 6 confirms 3 seconds fits the horizon. Decision 7 budgets 10 generations across two passes because the brief is at the edge of what current models do well. The team accepts that one in three generations may still need repair in post.

## Inputs and outputs in detail

The skill takes the shot brief and emits the design, the prompt draft, and the structured summary. Each output has a downstream consumer in the team's workflow.

**Inputs in detail.** The shot brief is the primary input and may be a single sentence or a several-paragraph description. Briefer briefs produce broader recommendations; richer briefs let the skill make sharper specific calls. The model class lets the skill calibrate length budgets and prompt grammar — frontier hosted models tolerate elaborate prompts and have longer horizons; open-source U-Net families (AnimateDiff and friends) want simpler prompts and shorter shots. Conditioning mode lets the skill skip recommendations for the modes the team is not using. Target duration drives Decision 6's length budget. Aspect and resolution constrain Decision 1's framing recommendation. Budget for generations caps the narrowing loop.

**Shot design output.** A markdown document organised by the seven decisions, with each decision's chosen position and the rationale grounded in the brief. The document is the design contract; subsequent generations refer back to it rather than re-deciding each variable. The downstream consumers are the team running the generations, the consistency architect (which inherits identity and environment commitments), and the post-integration pipeline (which inherits length and resolution commitments).

**Prompt draft output.** Three prompts: the first-pass prompt and two variants. The first-pass prompt embodies Decisions 1–5 in a model-agnostic grammar (subject sentence, action, camera, lighting, environment, style, all in plain prose). The two variants probe specific axes — usually motion strength and lighting — so the second pass gathers signal on which variable is the weakest link. The prompts are written to be pasted directly into a frontier hosted model's interface or into an open-source pipeline's text input; the team's only adaptation is to add the model-specific quirks the prompt format may require.

**Shot summary output.** A JSON object that machine-reads as the canonical record. The object lives in the project's design archive and feeds dashboards, pipelines, and the consistency architect's planning. Fields are stable across versions of the skill so a project archived in version 1.0 reads correctly under future versions.

## Working with the narrowing loop in practice

The narrowing loop in Decision 7 is the part of the methodology teams most often skip and most often regret skipping. A worked walk-through.

A team is generating an establishing shot for a piece — a city skyline at golden hour, slow push-in, on 35mm film. The first-pass prompt is written from Decisions 1–5. The team picks three seeds (47, 213, 8451) and runs all three at preview quality and 4-second duration. The previews come back: seed 47 has the right composition but the skyline is generic; seed 213 has a distinctive skyline but the camera move is wrong (a pan rather than a push-in); seed 8451 has the right camera move but the colour is wrong (blue-hour rather than golden-hour).

The team picks seed 47 as the survivor because the camera move and the composition are both correct, and the generic skyline is repairable in the prompt. The second pass: seed 47 at full quality and full duration, with the prompt variant "a recognisable city skyline with distinctive towers" replacing the generic skyline phrase, plus a CFG variant one step lower (5.5 instead of 6.5) to soften the over-saturation that crept into the preview.

The full-quality result lands. The team moves to Decision 8, reviews at delivery resolution, accepts the shot, and notes a minor flicker in the foreground building that the post-integration skill will address in Stage 4.

The total budget was four generations — three at preview cost, one at full cost. Without the narrowing loop, the team would have spent ten or fifteen generations rerolling the same prompt and seed combination, none of which would have systematically improved the result. The narrowing loop is cheap when used, expensive when skipped.

## Handing off to the next skill

The shot architect produces design artifacts; subsequent skills consume them. The handoff conventions matter because they determine whether the design survives into the finished piece.

**Handoff to the consistency architect.** When a sequence has multiple shots that share a subject or an environment, the shot architect's outputs are inputs to the consistency architect's planning. The identity-relevant fields (subject sentence, environment phrases, style anchor) carry over verbatim. The consistency architect adds the lock-in pattern, the hero stills, and the cut plan that hides residual drift. Teams that try to design shots in isolation and bolt consistency on afterwards discover the bolt does not hold — the consistency strategy belongs in the design from shot one.

**Handoff to the generation pipeline.** The prompt draft and the shot summary are the inputs to whichever generation system the team is using. For hosted-frontier products, the prompt draft is pasted into the interface and the seeds and CFG settings are configured through the product's controls. For open-source pipelines, the prompt draft and the conditioning inputs flow through a workflow file. Either way, the shot summary lives in the project archive and the generation logs are written back into the summary as the generations land.

**Handoff to the post pipeline.** The duration, the resolution, and the aspect ratio in the shot summary tell the post-integration skill what the deliverable looks like. The accept-or-repair calls from Decision 8 tell the post pipeline what to repair. The narrowing plan tells the post pipeline what was tried so that the post team does not waste effort on issues the generation team chose to accept.

## Shot architecture for animation and stylised work

The skill's defaults assume photoreal live-action style. Animation and stylised work follow the same seven decisions but with different anchors.

**2D animation.** The medium cue is the technique ("2D cel animation", "limited-frame animation", "Saturday-morning cartoon style"). The grade cue is reduced — animation's colour is built in, not graded over. Subject motion budgets stretch a little because animation models tolerate aggressive motion better than photoreal models in some cases; in other cases, the limited-frame look the team wants is the hard target and the model's tendency toward smooth tweening fights it.

**3D rendered, stylised.** The medium cue names the look ("stylised 3D", "low-poly", "cel-shaded 3D"). The lighting and camera cues translate; the action budget often shrinks because stylised 3D models are sometimes less robust than photoreal models on aggressive motion.

**Stop-motion.** The medium cue ("stop-motion", "claymation", "puppet animation"). The frame rate cue matters — stop-motion has a characteristic stuttery look that the model reproduces if asked ("twos animation, 12fps look"). Subject motion budgets are tight.

**Mixed media.** Some pieces deliberately combine photoreal and stylised within the same sequence. Each shot picks its own anchors; the consistency architect handles whether the mix reads as intentional or accidental.

## Reading the model's failure modes

The narrowing loop is faster when the team reads the failure correctly. A taxonomy.

**Composition failure.** Subject is mis-framed, partially out of frame, or absent. Rewrite Decision 1's subject sentence with explicit framing cues.

**Action failure.** Subject is on screen and framed correctly but is not doing the intended action. Rewrite Decision 2's verb to be more visual and less abstract.

**Camera failure.** The camera move is wrong or absent. Try Decision 3's alternative camera vocabulary or accept a static shot.

**Identity failure.** Subject appears but is the wrong person or wrong version of the right person. Hand off to the consistency architect for identity conditioning.

**Anatomy failure.** Hands, faces, eyes, or other anatomical detail is wrong. Accept (if invisible at playback context), repair in post, or regenerate with a different seed.

**Style failure.** Look is wrong — colour, contrast, grain, era. Strengthen Decision 5's style anchors or accept and fix in grade.

**Temporal failure.** First frame is right but the shot falls apart over its duration. Shorten the shot below the model's coherence horizon or restart from a different seed.

**Hallucination failure.** Text on signs, brand logos, license plates, background details have come back nonsensical. Composite over in post or reframe to crop out.

Each failure mode has a specific cause and a specific corrective action. The narrowing loop is a sequence of corrective actions, not a sequence of rerolls.

## Common pitfalls

The skill catches a recurring set of mistakes that teams new to video diffusion make. Each pitfall is named and the corrective decision is named so the team can self-diagnose during the narrowing loop.

**Pitfall 1 — Over-stuffed prompts.** A prompt that names six things the model has to put on screen produces a smear of all six. The model has limited capacity for compositional complexity inside a single shot, and the failure mode is graceless: subjects merge, objects disappear, the action becomes incoherent. Corrective: revise Decision 1 to one primary subject and one contextual subject. Move the rest into separate shots.

**Pitfall 2 — Two sequential actions.** "Walks in, looks around, sits down" produces a single chimeric motion the model is trying to satisfy all three of, and usually one of them dominates while the others smear. Corrective: pick the most important action and let the cut handle the rest. The shot architect plans the cut at Decision 6.

**Pitfall 3 — Two camera moves.** "Dolly in then pan right" produces a smeared composite. Corrective: pick one camera move; the other move becomes a separate shot.

**Pitfall 4 — Style cue conflict.** "Shot on 35mm film" + "VHS-era footage" + "neon-lit cyberpunk grade" gives the model three incompatible style anchors and the result is whichever one the training set associates most strongly with the rest of the prompt. Corrective: one medium cue and one grade cue. Strip the rest.

**Pitfall 5 — Abstract subject descriptors.** "A contemplative figure" or "a yearning silhouette" forces the model to translate the abstraction into visible behaviour, and the translation is unreliable. Corrective: write the visible behaviour directly.

**Pitfall 6 — Asking for text on screen.** Most current video-diffusion models hallucinate text. Names of brands, signs, license plates, t-shirts with writing will come back wrong or unreadable. Corrective: composite the text in post, or accept that the text will be unreadable and use it only as a graphic shape.

**Pitfall 7 — Asking for hands doing detailed things.** Hands holding objects, hands counting fingers, hands writing — these are at the edge of current models. Corrective: frame to crop hands out, frame to show hands in low detail, or budget extra generations and accept a higher reject rate.

**Pitfall 8 — Reroll loop without revision.** The team generates twenty times with the same prompt and the same seed strategy hoping for a different result. The model is not getting better between generations; the prompt is the problem. Corrective: the narrowing loop in Decision 7 revises one variable per pass.

**Pitfall 9 — Treating the model's first frame as the shot's identity.** In text-only generation, the first frame is often the strongest frame and the team writes a brief around it; subsequent frames degrade. The shot is not the first frame, it is the duration. Corrective: review the full shot at full duration before committing to a generation, and reject generations whose first frame is strong but whose final second has fallen apart.

**Pitfall 10 — Burning the generation budget on the wrong shot.** Some shots in a sequence are easy (a static establishing shot of a landscape) and some are hard (a hero close-up with motion). Teams that spread their generation budget evenly across shots over-invest in the easy ones and under-invest in the hard ones. Corrective: triage shots by difficulty at storyboard time and weight the budget toward the hard shots.

## Decision summary

| Decision | Question | Default | When to override |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Subject | What is the one primary thing on screen? | One primary, one contextual. | A two-character interaction shot may justify two equal subjects with the failure mode accepted. |
| 2. Action | What is the one visible verb? | One unambiguous present-tense verb. | A short shot with no subject motion (subtle environmental motion only) skips the verb. |
| 3. Camera | One move plus one lens-feel cue. | "Slow push-in, medium shot, shallow depth of field" is a strong default. | Static shots are easier; pick static when the subject motion is the focus. |
| 4. Lighting and environment | One light kind + one quality + one environment phrase. | Match the time of day to the light kind. | Stylised pieces may break the natural pairing; do so deliberately. |
| 5. Style | One medium cue + one grade cue. | "Shot on 35mm film, natural grade, warm tones" is a workable default for live-action realism. | Animation, archival look, or stylised pieces pick different anchors. |
| 6. Length | Two-thirds of the model's coherence horizon. | If the brief exceeds the horizon, decompose into shots. | A 1-second shot can run at the horizon comfortably; the rule is conservative. |
| 7. Conditioning | Three seeds, full quality on the survivor, one variant per pass. | Image-to-video for identity-anchored shots; text-only for atmospheric shots. | Tight identity demands push to LoRA or adapter conditioning. |
| 8. Iteration | Three passes maximum on the narrowing loop. | Past three passes, the brief is the problem. | Hero shots may justify more generations across multiple seed brackets. |

## Limitations

The skill plans one shot or a tightly bounded sequence and does not produce continuity guidance across a longer sequence — that is the consistency architect's job. The skill does not name specific frontier products by trademarked name in the body; teams who want a recipe for one specific product should consult that product's documentation alongside this skill's methodology. The skill assumes the team has access to whichever model they target and does not address procurement, pricing, or platform terms of service. The skill assumes good-faith creative use and refuses to design shots that depict identifiable real people without consent, that depict minors in sexual contexts, that defame, or that violate the platform's content policy.

## Iterating on briefs that resist landing

Some briefs are easy and land in one or two passes; some resist regardless of effort. The skill names patterns of resistant briefs and what to do.

**Resistance pattern 1 — Conceptual abstraction.** The brief describes a feeling or a concept ("the loneliness of a city at dawn", "the moment before he decides") rather than a visible scene. The model has no handle. Corrective: with the team, translate the abstraction into a visible scene before invoking the skill. "A man stands at a window, looks at the empty street below, no other people in frame, soft dawn light" is a workable scene for the original abstraction.

**Resistance pattern 2 — Many specific visual references.** The brief asks for the exact composition of a famous painting, the exact lighting of a specific film, or a recreation of a real event. The model averages over its training set; exactness is out of reach. Corrective: pick the two or three strongest visual cues from the reference and let the model interpret them. Exact recreation needs traditional production or photo-real CGI.

**Resistance pattern 3 — Multi-agent dynamic interaction.** The brief asks for two characters reacting to each other with synchronised behaviour. Current models do not model multi-agent dynamics well; the result is two characters who happen to be in the same frame without responding to each other. Corrective: decompose into two shots cut against each other, or shoot the interaction practically.

**Resistance pattern 4 — Specific named objects or text.** The brief asks for a Coca-Cola can, an Apple logo, or a specific street sign. The model hallucinates the asset. Corrective: composite the asset in post over an AI-generated plate that leaves room for the composite.

**Resistance pattern 5 — Subtle facial expression on a hero character.** The brief asks for a specific emotion to read on the character's face. The model produces approximate emotions; subtleties are unreliable. Corrective: write the visible behaviour ("eyebrows raise slightly, eyes widen") rather than the emotion, and accept that hero emotional moments may need a live-action performance under an AI environment composite.

The skill flags resistant patterns in the design and recommends decomposition, composite, or practical production rather than burning generation budget on a brief the model cannot meet.

## Open-source vs hosted: what the methodology looks like in each

The seven decisions are the same in both contexts but the practical knobs differ.

**Hosted frontier products** typically expose: prompt, optional image reference, seed, a guidance scale or its equivalent, motion strength on image-to-video, duration, aspect ratio, optional structure reference, and sometimes a per-frame conditioning surface. Sampler choice and step count are usually opaque. The prompt grammar is generous; complex multi-sentence prompts work, and the model's prompt-adherence is usually high. The trade-off is per-generation cost and rate limiting.

**Open-source DiT pipelines** typically expose all of the above plus the underlying sampler, the step count, the scheduler, the LoRA loaders, the adapter loaders, and the workflow's compute settings. The prompt grammar is more sensitive — open-source models often want simpler prompts and respond to specific phrasings more idiosyncratically. The advantage is full pipeline control and per-generation cost dominated by the team's compute, not by per-call pricing.

**Open-source U-Net AnimateDiff-class pipelines** are the most constrained. Coherence horizons are shorter; aggressive motion is harder; image-to-video conditioning is the workhorse. The advantage is the flexibility of the underlying image model — the team can swap base image models, load image-model LoRAs, and use the broader image-generation ecosystem.

The skill's recommendations adapt to the chosen class through the `model_class` input. Where the input is omitted, the skill defaults to frontier hosted conventions and flags the assumption.
