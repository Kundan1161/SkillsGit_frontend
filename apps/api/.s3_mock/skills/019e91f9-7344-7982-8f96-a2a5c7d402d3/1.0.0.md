## When to use

Invoke this skill when a user needs to generate an outbound email sequence aimed at a specific prospect or persona, not a generic broadcast. The skill is designed for one-to-few or one-to-one outreach — campaigns where the sender is willing to read each draft, edit lightly, and personalize the opener line. It is not designed to be wired into a high-volume sender that fires thousands of messages a day; that pattern reliably degrades deliverability and the methodology here is calibrated against per-recipient quality.

Use this skill when the user can supply, at minimum, a persona, a company context, an offer, and a claimed business outcome. If any of the four are missing, the skill should ask before drafting — vague inputs produce vague emails, and vague emails are deleted.

Do not use this skill for cold calling scripts, for nurture sequences targeting opted-in subscribers (those are warmed lists with a different cadence), for transactional notifications, or for renewal outreach to existing customers. Those situations need different question framing and different tone calibration.

## How to apply

Outbound has matured into a craft with predictable failure modes. The vast majority of cold emails fail for one of five reasons: the subject line reads like an ad, the opener is about the sender, the body has no specific reason this recipient should care, the ask is too big for a first touch, or there is no follow-up. The methodology below addresses each in turn.

### Step 1 — Parse and validate the inputs

Read every input carefully before drafting. Confirm that the persona is a specific title (not "decision-maker"), that the company context contains something you can actually reference (size, stage, vertical, a trigger event), that the offer is described in buyer language rather than feature jargon, and that the business outcome is a measurable result the prospect would want.

If any input is too generic to write a credible email from, surface the gap and ask one focused question rather than guessing. A common failure mode is to invent a trigger event ("I saw you recently hired…") that turns out to be false — that lie is the message the prospect remembers.

### Step 2 — Estimate persona buying context

Before writing anything, build a quick mental model of the persona's day. For a VP of Engineering, what landed in their inbox this morning? A leadership 1:1, a fire from production, a vendor pitch they did not ask for. The cold email is competing against all three. For a CFO, the calendar is mostly meetings about money and the inbox is mostly people asking for money. For a Director of Customer Support, the inbox is angry customers escalated up.

Use this mental model to pick a tone. Engineering leaders read crisply-written technical-flavored prose and reject corporate-speak. Finance leaders read short messages with numbers and reject prose with adjectives. Support leaders read messages that demonstrate familiarity with their actual workflow.

### Step 3 — Decide the framework for the opener email

Pick one of three framings based on whether the buyer is aware of the problem you solve:

- **Problem-first** when the buyer feels the pain daily and the offer addresses a well-known category. Open by naming the pain in their words; transition to a one-sentence claim of the outcome.
- **Insight-first** when the buyer does not yet know there is a better way. Open with a credible counter-intuitive observation from the field; transition to what that implies for their stack.
- **Trigger-first** when there is a specific recent event in the company context (hire, funding, product launch, public statement). Open by acknowledging the event with a single specific detail; transition to why that event makes the offer relevant now.

Resist the urge to use multiple framings in one email. Each framing has a different pace and a different transition — mixing them produces emails that read as if a committee wrote them.

### Step 4 — Draft the opener email

Hold yourself to these constraints:

- Subject line under 50 characters, lowercase preferred for replies-look (not always — some personas, like CFOs, expect title case). No emojis unless the company culture obviously supports them. No "Re:" or fake personalization tokens.
- First line is about the recipient, not the sender. Either a specific reference to their company, or a credible observation about their role, or a one-line insight from their domain. Never "I hope this finds you well." Never "I noticed you are the VP of X at Y."
- Body is three to five sentences. Sentence one anchors the relevance. Sentence two states the problem you solve in their language. Sentence three states the outcome with a number if available. Sentence four (optional) names a proof point — a similar-stage customer, a benchmark, a published metric.
- The ask is small: a 15-minute call, a single specific question answered by reply, a link to a 90-second video. Never "Are you the right person?" — that question concedes you did not do the research. Never "Do you have time for a demo next week?" — too big a step. The job of touch one is to earn touch two.
- Signature is one line. Title, company, optional one-line credibility marker. No giant logo banners; image-heavy signatures hurt deliverability and trip spam filters.

Write three candidate subject lines that match the body's framing. Then write two more that take the opposite tone (one playful, one literal) to give the user genuine choice. Label each by the hypothesis it tests.

### Step 5 — Draft the nudge email (touch 2)

The nudge runs three to five business days after the opener. Its purpose is to give the recipient a second reason to reply, in case the first one missed for situational reasons (inbox volume, sick day, the topic was not top of mind).

The nudge is shorter than the opener. Two to four sentences. It either:

- **Adds a new angle.** A different value frame, a different proof point, or a more specific outcome example. Do not re-state the opener; the recipient already saw it.
- **Asks a sharper question.** A single concrete question they can answer in one line. ("Are you measuring deflection rate, or first-response time?") Sharper questions get replies even when broad asks do not.
- **Reduces the ask.** If the opener asked for 15 minutes, the nudge can ask for a yes/no on whether the topic is on their roadmap this quarter.

Quote-and-reply to the original email (i.e., send in-thread). This works better than a fresh thread because the recipient sees the prior context inline.

### Step 6 — Draft the break-up email (touch 3)

The break-up runs seven to ten business days after the nudge. It serves two purposes: (1) it signals you respect the recipient's time and will stop contacting them, which paradoxically lifts reply rates by 10–20%; (2) it offers a clean off-ramp that the recipient can use to close the loop.

Constraints:

- Two sentences. State that you are closing the loop and assume the timing or fit is wrong. Offer to reach back in a quarter or six months if that's more useful.
- No guilt trips. No "I've tried reaching you three times." No "If you're not interested, just let me know" — that is what the email itself is doing.
- Optional one-line P.S. that asks if there is a more appropriate contact at the company. This is the highest-conversion line in the entire sequence when used at touch three (it stops feeling pushy because you have visibly stopped pursuing them).

### Step 7 — Calibrate to seniority

A VP- or C-level recipient reads emails optimized for executive language: short sentences, numbers in the body, no jargon, a clear stated outcome. A director-level recipient is more likely to forward emails they like to their boss — so include a forwardable artifact (a one-page link, a benchmark) that survives the forward. An IC-level recipient (Engineer, Analyst, Specialist) is more likely to engage with substance — a deeper technical observation, a link to documentation, an example from a peer.

Adjust word count and proof-point selection accordingly. Do not just change the salutation.

### Step 8 — Calibrate to vertical

Match vocabulary to the buyer's industry. A fintech CFO reads "treasury", "runway", "burn", "AR aging". An e-commerce ops leader reads "AOV", "GMV", "fulfillment SLA", "RMA rate". A healthcare leader reads "HIPAA", "EMR", "throughput". If the user has not supplied vocabulary cues, infer from the company description and confirm before drafting.

Avoid sounding like a foreign-language tourist who learned three vocabulary words. Two well-placed industry terms earn credibility; five make you sound like a marketing intern.

### Step 9 — Verify deliverability hygiene

Before producing the final draft, check for known deliverability traps:

- No spam-trigger phrases ("guaranteed", "act now", "free trial", "100% effective", "limited time").
- No more than one link in the opener, no more than two in the whole sequence. Trackable links from new domains burn inboxes.
- No image-only content. Body should be plain text with at most a small inline link.
- No "unsubscribe" footer on a 1:1 cold email — that signals it is bulk and reduces reply rate. (For broader campaigns the law may require an unsubscribe; in that case, comply, but be aware of the cost.)
- No attachments. PDFs trigger filters. Link to a hosted page instead.

### Step 10 — Output the sequence in a reviewable format

Produce a single markdown deliverable with this structure:

- **Touch 1 (Day 0)** — subject (primary), three alternates, body, rationale (which framing, why it fits the persona).
- **Touch 2 (Day 3–5)** — quote-reply subject, body, rationale.
- **Touch 3 (Day 10–14)** — subject, body, rationale, optional P.S.
- **Pre-send checklist** — six to ten line items the sender should verify before sending each touch.

Also produce a short note on **what to A/B test next quarter**: usually the framing decision (problem-first vs. insight-first vs. trigger-first) is the highest-leverage test, not the subject line.

### Step 11 — Refuse to invent facts

Do not invent customer names, do not invent a number you do not have, do not invent a quote from a person. If the sender did not give you a proof point, leave a clearly-marked placeholder: `[INSERT PROOF POINT — see Step 4]`. If the company context is thin, do not fabricate a trigger event. Made-up specifics destroy credibility faster than no specifics.

### Step 12 — Handle objections preemptively in the body

If the offer commonly meets a predictable objection from this persona (CFOs object on price; VPs of Engineering object on build-vs-buy; CIOs object on security), address it in one sentence somewhere in the sequence. Touch one is too early for a full rebuttal; touch two is the natural place. Phrasing should be a single-sentence acknowledgment plus a one-sentence answer.

### Step 13 — Calibrate cadence to the vertical

Default cadence is Day 0 / Day 4 / Day 11. Adjust:

- Enterprise (CIO, CFO at 1000+ headcount): stretch to Day 0 / Day 7 / Day 21. Their calendars are slower; pushy timing reads as desperate.
- SMB (founder, head of ops at <50 headcount): tighten to Day 0 / Day 3 / Day 8. Their inboxes turn over faster.
- Mid-market: the default works.

Never send more than three touches in a single quarter. If the sequence completes without reply, wait a full quarter before re-engagement and start the new sequence with a different framing.

### Step 14 — Output a debrief block

After the sequence, output a short debrief: which assumptions you made, what would change the answer (e.g., "if this persona is actually a VP of Eng rather than Director, I would soften the technical depth in touch two"), and which inputs would most improve the next iteration. This makes the skill iterative rather than one-shot.

- **target_persona** (required) — specific title plus seniority level.

- **sender_context** (optional) — the sender's title and credibility markers.

- A three-touch sequence in markdown, with subject variants and per-touch rationale.
- A pre-send deliverability checklist.
- A short debrief on assumptions and the highest-leverage next A/B test.

## Examples

### Worked example — VP of Engineering at a Series C SaaS, developer productivity offer

**Inputs given to the skill:**

- target_persona: "VP of Engineering"
- target_company_context: "Series C SaaS, 350 engineers, recently posted publicly about scaling pains and a hiring spree for senior ICs."
- our_offer: "An async code review and PR coordination layer that sits on top of GitHub."
- business_outcome: "Cut median PR cycle time from 36 hours to under 8."
- proof_point: "Used by 4 of the YC W23 unicorns."
- sender_context: "Founder, ex-Stripe infra eng."

**Output produced by the skill (abridged for the example):**

**Touch 1 — Day 0**

Subject (primary): "350 engineers, 36-hour PR cycles"
Subject (alternates):
- "scaling reviews past 200 eng"
- "PRs sitting waiting for review"
- "from 36h to 8h on PR cycle"
- "the review bottleneck at 350 eng"

> Saw the public note about scaling pains alongside the senior IC hiring push — that combo usually means PR review latency is creeping into 30+ hour territory.
>
> We built an async review-and-PR-coordination layer that sits on top of GitHub. Four of the YC W23 unicorns are using it; their median PR cycle dropped from 36h to under 8h within a quarter.
>
> Worth a 15-minute look at how it would fit on top of your existing repo conventions?
>
> — [Name], founder, [Company]. Built infra at Stripe before this.

Rationale: trigger-first framing because the public statement is the strongest hook. Body is engineering-leader-paced (short, specific numbers, no marketing adjectives). Ask is small.

**Touch 2 — Day 4**

Subject: in-thread (Re: prior)

> Quick follow-up: the question I would want answered if I were sitting in your chair is whether the layer touches your existing branch protection rules. It does not — it sits next to GitHub's native checks, not on top of them. Build-vs-buy reads here are usually about that interaction.
>
> If "scaling PR review" is not a Q2 priority, say so and I will close the loop.

Rationale: anticipates the build-vs-buy objection that any infra-leaning VP of Eng has when reading a new tool. Reduces the ask to a one-word yes/no.

**Touch 3 — Day 11**

Subject: "closing the loop"

> Last note — assuming the timing is wrong on this; will check back next quarter if useful.
>
> P.S. If there's a better contact on the platform team for this, happy to route there instead.

Rationale: standard break-up plus the routing P.S., which has consistently lifted reply rates at this persona.

**Pre-send checklist:**

- Confirm the public statement actually exists and is recent (< 60 days).
- Confirm the proof point (YC W23 unicorns) is something the sender can name on a follow-up call.
- Verify GitHub is actually their VCS (don't send if they are on GitLab — opener becomes a lie).
- Test sending from a warmed domain, not a brand-new sending domain.

**Debrief:**

The single highest-leverage input was the public statement; if it had not been there, the skill would have switched to insight-first framing built around a generic engineering benchmark. Next A/B test recommendation: same framework, but compare trigger-first against problem-first ("PRs sitting in review queues for >24 hours") on a matched-pair list.

## Limitations

This skill writes the message; it does not validate the recipient is a real person at the company, that the email address is deliverable, or that the company is currently in market. Combine with list hygiene tooling and intent-data signals; the skill is one component of an outbound stack, not the whole stack.

The skill assumes the sender will read and edit every draft. It is not safe to wire into a fully automated sender that mails thousands of drafts unreviewed; persona inference at scale is unreliable and inventing details on bulk lists damages domain reputation and brand.

Cold email regulations vary by jurisdiction (CAN-SPAM in the US, CASL in Canada, GDPR/PECR in the EU). This skill does not produce compliance language and does not check whether the recipient is in a regulated jurisdiction. The sender is responsible for compliance.

Deliverability rules shift quarterly. Subject-line heuristics that worked last year (e.g., lowercase, length, emoji) move as filtering models update. This skill is sold as a subscription specifically so the methodology can be refreshed; the version published at the date in the changelog reflects the patterns that were holding up at that time.

The skill will not invent proof points. If the user does not supply one, the output will mark a placeholder. This is by design — invented references are the single fastest way to lose credibility once the recipient checks.
