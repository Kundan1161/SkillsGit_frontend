## When to use

Use this skill when a marketing team needs an outside perspective on its measurement program — not just "what attribution model should we use," but the full stack of how the program decides where the next dollar goes. Typical triggers:

- The team uses platform-reported numbers to make budget decisions and finance has stopped believing them.
- An MMM (in-house or vendor) and an MTA tool disagree by a meaningful margin and nobody can articulate which to trust.
- Privacy regulation, browser changes, or platform attribution-window changes have eroded the previous setup and the team needs to redesign.
- A new channel (or large new spend) is about to launch and the team wants to know how it will be measured before it ships.
- An MMM vendor contract is up for renewal and leadership wants a defensible answer to "do we need this."
- A geo or lift test produced a result that conflicts with what platform attribution suggested and the team needs a framework for what to do.

Do NOT use this skill for:

- Building the MMM itself — the skill recommends methods and reviews plans; it does not fit the regression or specify priors.
- Tactical "what's wrong with this campaign" questions — see the Paid Channel Audit skill.
- General A/B test design — see broader experimentation skills.
- Data engineering for the measurement pipeline — that is a separate discipline.

## How to apply

A measurement program is a portfolio of methods, not a single chosen one. The job of this skill is to map the team's decisions to the methods best suited to inform them, name the gaps, and give the team a protocol for living with disagreement.

### Step 1 — List the decisions the measurement program must inform

Measurement only matters in service of decisions. Force the team to enumerate them:

- Total paid budget — set quarterly or annually.
- Channel-mix allocation — how spend is divided across Google, Meta, LinkedIn, TikTok, programmatic, OOH, etc.
- Campaign-level allocation — which campaigns within a channel get what share.
- In-flight bidding — what the platform optimizer is told to optimize against.
- Creative testing decisions — which concept wins.
- Strategic bets — new market launch, new product, new channel.
- Finance and forecasting — how marketing-driven revenue is reported externally.

Each decision lives at a different time horizon (annual to sub-daily) and a different level of granularity (channel to ad-creative). No single method serves all of them. The skill's job is to map each decision to the cheapest method that can defensibly inform it.

### Step 2 — Define the methods on the board

Be explicit about what each method does and does not do. Capture the team's current understanding so misconceptions surface:

- **Last-click attribution** assigns the full conversion to the last touched channel/campaign in a defined window. Cheap, easy, biased toward lower-funnel and especially toward branded search and direct.
- **Rules-based MTA** (first-touch, linear, time-decay, position-based) distributes credit across touches using a fixed rule. The rule encodes an assumption about how attention works; it does not measure incrementality.
- **Data-driven attribution (DDA)** uses a model — often inside the ad platform — to assign fractional credit across observed touches. Better than fixed rules at predicting what touchpoints correlate with conversion; still correlational, still inside one platform's data, still walled off from other platforms.
- **Multi-touch attribution (MTA) vendors** stitch identity across platforms and assign credit using more sophisticated models. Identity quality is the binding constraint; under restricted tracking, MTA's signal degrades.
- **Marketing mix models (MMM)** use time-series regression at the channel or campaign level to estimate the incremental contribution of spend, controlling for seasonality, macro effects, and saturation. Causal-ish, robust to identity loss, slow to update, blind to within-channel changes.
- **Geo experiments / lift studies** are the closest thing the industry has to a randomized trial in marketing. A treatment set of geos or audiences sees the program; a control set does not. Differences are incremental by construction. Requires enough volume, takes weeks, costs the foregone spend in the control.
- **Platform conversion-lift / brand-lift studies** are randomized inside one platform's inventory. Strong within that platform; cannot answer cross-platform questions.

### Step 3 — Map decisions to methods

The mapping the skill should propose, defaulting to the cheapest method that can defensibly answer the question:

| Decision | First-choice method | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Total paid budget | MMM | Annual or semi-annual cadence; sized against finance models. |
| Cross-channel mix | MMM, validated by geo experiments | MMM proposes, experiments validate the largest reallocations. |
| Within-channel campaign mix | Platform DDA + platform lift | DDA for routine, lift for big bets. |
| In-flight bidding | Platform DDA on deduped server-side conversions | The bidder is going to optimize against something — make sure it is something the team trusts. |
| Creative tests | Platform A/B + holdout | See the Creative Test Planner skill. |
| New channel evaluation | Geo experiment | An MMM cannot evaluate a channel that has no historical spend. |
| Finance reporting | MMM, blended with first-touch warehouse counts as a sanity check | Pick one official number and document it. |

This is a default mapping. Adjust based on the team's context.

### Step 4 — Stress-test the current setup against this map

Where is the team using a method to make a decision the method cannot defensibly inform? Common failure modes:

- Using platform last-click to set channel-mix allocation. Brand-search and retargeting will eat the credit; incremental cold-audience prospecting will look unprofitable.
- Using MMM alone to set in-flight bidding. MMM's update cadence (weekly at best, monthly typical) cannot drive a system that re-bids in seconds.
- Using MTA to evaluate a new channel that has been live for two weeks. The model has no history to weight from.
- Using a single geo test as the basis for a permanent channel-mix change. Geo tests measure the world as it was during the test; the underlying market shifts.

Name each mismatch as a finding.

### Step 5 — Review MMM design (if one exists)

If an MMM is in scope, run the following checks:

- **Data window.** Two to three years of weekly data is typical. Less than 18 months at typical seasonality is fragile.
- **Granularity.** Channel-level is the minimum; campaign or audience-segment level requires both the data and the regularization to support it.
- **Variables.** Are non-paid drivers in the model — seasonality, macro indicators, promotional events, weather where it matters, pricing changes, product launches, competitor pressure? An MMM without these will absorb their effects into paid coefficients.
- **Saturation and adstock.** Are saturation curves (Hill, sigmoid) and carryover (adstock with a tunable decay) parameterized rather than fixed? Fixed parameters destroy model honesty.
- **Validation.** Out-of-time backtest. Has the team held out the last N weeks and measured prediction error? "Looks reasonable on training" is not validation.
- **Calibration to experiments.** Has any MMM channel coefficient been calibrated to a geo or lift experiment? If yes, when, and how stale? If never, the MMM is one model class away from observational; treat it accordingly.
- **Saturation honesty.** Does the MMM say "we are far from saturation on X channel — keep spending"? If yes, has anyone tested that claim with a real spend ramp? MMMs frequently understate saturation in the extrapolation region.
- **Uncertainty.** Are channel ROI estimates reported with credible intervals? Point estimates without intervals mislead the budget conversation.

### Step 6 — Review MTA design (if one is in use)

If an MTA tool is in scope:

- **Identity quality.** What share of paid conversions are matched to a user identity that spans platforms? Under restricted tracking, this share is materially below historical baselines.
- **Lookback window.** What window does the MTA use, and does it match what finance reports on? Mismatched windows produce internal arguments.
- **Channel coverage.** Does the MTA cover every paid channel in the mix? A model that ignores TikTok or CTV will over-credit the channels it can see.
- **Validation against incrementality.** Has the MTA's credit assignment ever been validated against a geo or lift test? If not, it is a credit-assignment opinion, not a measurement.
- **Direct and organic handling.** How does the MTA treat direct traffic and organic search? "Last non-direct" rules silently re-route credit to whichever paid touch happened to be in window.

### Step 7 — Review experimentation cadence

For a program with non-trivial paid spend, the question is not "are we running experiments" but "are we running enough." Targets to recommend:

- For programs at $500k+/month paid spend, at least one geo or platform-lift test per quarter, ideally one per major channel per year.
- For programs running an MMM, one calibration experiment per year for each channel whose MMM coefficient drives a budget decision worth more than the experiment's foregone revenue.
- For new channels representing >5% of spend, an incrementality test within the first ninety days of meaningful scale.

If experimentation cadence is materially below these guides, name it as a finding.

### Step 8 — Audit attribution-window discipline

Pick the conversion event and walk through every system that uses an attribution window for it: ad platforms (with their click and view windows), web analytics, MMM input, finance reporting, MTA. Document each. The point is rarely "make them all the same"; it is "make sure the team knows which is which." Most measurement disputes inside teams are attribution-window mismatches in disguise.

### Step 9 — Identify the data plumbing

A measurement program is only as good as its inputs:

- Are server-side conversions deduped against browser-side?
- Are offline conversions (CRM stage progressions, returns, refunds) pushed back to the platforms and into the warehouse on a cadence the model needs?
- Is spend data joined to the same channel/campaign labels the conversion data uses?
- Is the warehouse the source of truth, and if not, where do disputes get resolved?

A missing or fragile pipe is a higher-priority finding than a sub-optimal model.

### Step 10 — Build the recommendation portfolio

Combine findings into a recommended portfolio. A typical recommendation for a mid-sized B2C program:

- MMM at quarterly cadence for annual budget and channel mix, calibrated against one geo experiment per year per major channel.
- Platform DDA on deduped server-side conversions to drive in-flight bidding.
- A standing experimentation calendar with one geo or lift test per quarter.
- A warehouse-of-record for finance reporting that uses first-touch or position-based credit and is documented as one specific operational definition.
- An explicit decision protocol when methods disagree (see Step 11).

Adjust for scale. At under $25k/month total paid spend, MMM is rarely defensible — geo tests and platform DDA carry the program. At over $2M/month, MMM is table stakes and the question is whether to build vs buy.

### Step 11 — Define the triangulation protocol

When MMM and platform numbers disagree, the team needs a written tie-breaker. Recommended hierarchy, in decreasing order of strength:

1. A clean, recent geo or lift experiment on the channel in question wins, full stop, for the scope it covers.
2. An MMM coefficient calibrated against an experiment in the last 12 months wins for channel-mix decisions.
3. Platform DDA (deduped server-side) wins for in-flight bidder decisions.
4. Platform last-click is for diagnostics only and should not break ties.

If no experiment exists and methods disagree, the right answer is "run the experiment," not "average the numbers."

### Step 12 — Name the common pitfalls

Before closing, name the pitfalls the team should watch for:

- **MMM saturation drift.** Models trained on historical spend extrapolate poorly when the team pushes a channel meaningfully past its trained range.
- **MTA identity drift.** A model that worked under one tracking regime degrades silently when the regime changes; revalidate at least annually.
- **Geo test contamination.** Holiday weeks, regional weather events, competitor moves, and product launches can swamp a treatment effect. Always document the test window's events.
- **"Brand search as paid" inflation.** Including brand search in non-brand ROAS is the most common single source of inflated paid performance.
- **Lookback-window cherry-pick.** Teams sometimes widen the lookback to recover "lost" conversions after a tracking change. This trades real precision for political comfort.
- **Two methods, two reports, two budgets.** The biggest organizational failure mode is having both an MMM and an MTA produce numbers that finance, marketing, and ops each prefer for political reasons. Pick one official source per decision class in writing.

### Step 13 — Open questions

Examples worth surfacing:

- "Is there enough geo volume on the smallest paid channel to power a four-week lift test?"
- "Is finance willing to switch from last-click to first-touch warehouse counts for monthly reporting?"
- "Does the team have an engineering owner for the conversion pipeline, or is this still a marketing-ops function on borrowed time?"
- "If MMM and an experiment disagree, is leadership willing to follow the experiment?"

### Step 14 — Write the review

The output should have four sections:

- **Findings**, lens by lens, with severity.
- **Recommended portfolio**, decision-by-decision, with phasing.
- **Triangulation protocol**, one page, explicit hierarchy.
- **Open questions**, ordered by impact.

Avoid the reflex to recommend "build a better MMM" when the actual binding constraint is "no experiments to calibrate it." The cheapest, most valuable measurement upgrade for most programs is a regular cadence of geo and lift tests, not a fancier model.

### Step 15 — Calibrate confidence

End by noting what depth of evidence the review was based on, what was assumed, and which findings would flip if a specific input changed. Reviews that admit their dependencies are easier to act on.

## Examples

**Example 1 — Mid-sized DTC, MMM vs platform conflict.**

Input: "$1.2M/month paid, mostly Meta and Google. We bought an MMM vendor 14 months ago. The MMM says Meta is 30% over-credited and we should shift to Google. Meta's platform attribution says we are still scaling efficiently. We have not run an experiment in 18 months."

Expected behavior:
- Finding: the MMM has not been calibrated against any experiment in its lifetime; its claim that Meta is over-credited is plausible but unverified.
- Recommendation: do not act on the MMM's full magnitude. Run a four-to-six-week Meta geo holdout sized to detect a ±20% incremental difference. Use the experiment to calibrate the MMM coefficient. Concurrently, run a Google geo holdout to estimate Google's own incrementality so the comparison is apples-to-apples.
- Triangulation protocol: the geo test wins for the budget question. The MMM regains authority once recalibrated.
- Open questions: is there geo volume to power both tests in parallel, or do they need to sequence? Is finance willing to hold budget flat during the test window?

**Example 2 — Early-stage B2B SaaS, attribution debate.**

Input: "$40k/month split LinkedIn and Google Ads. Sales says LinkedIn is producing pipeline; the platform last-click report says Google. We're being asked whether to invest in an MMM."

Expected behavior:
- Finding: at this scale an MMM is premature — there is not enough spend variation per channel per week to identify coefficients usefully.
- Recommendation: instead, ship offline conversion import to both platforms keyed to opportunity-creation, switch reporting to that event, and run a LinkedIn audience holdout for eight weeks. Defer the MMM question for at least 12–18 months or until total paid spend exceeds roughly $200k/month sustained.
- Triangulation protocol: until the experiment finishes, treat platform numbers as platform-internal optimization signals, not as business truth.
- Open questions: is the CRM stage definition stable enough to send back as a conversion event, or does it change too often?

## Limitations

- This skill recommends methods and reviews plans. It does not specify priors, choose adstock decay rates, or fit regressions.
- Causal claims require causal designs. Where no experimental evidence exists, the skill is honest that the recommendation is correlational.
- MMM, MTA, and identity-stitching technologies evolve quickly. The mapping in Step 3 is a default; specific vendor capabilities should be checked against current product documentation.
- The skill cannot resolve political conflicts. If finance, marketing, and analytics all want different official numbers, the skill recommends a protocol; adopting it is a leadership decision.
- Where the team's scale is below the threshold for any one method, the skill says so rather than recommending the method anyway. Right-sizing is an output.
