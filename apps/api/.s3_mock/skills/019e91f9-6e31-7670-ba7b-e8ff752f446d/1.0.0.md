# A/B Test Designer

## When to use

Use this skill when someone has an idea for a change they want to test on a website, in a product, or in a marketing funnel, and needs the experiment fully specified before engineering builds it or a platform like a feature-flag service starts assigning variants. Typical triggers include a landing-page redesign proposal, a new pricing layout, a copy variant, an onboarding-flow change, a checkout button color, a recommendation algorithm swap, a paywall threshold change, an email subject-line test, or any product change a team is willing to gate behind a flag.

The output is meant to be reviewed by a product manager, a designer, an engineer, and an analyst before launch. It does not run the test, write the assignment code, or analyze results — it produces the plan and the questions that need answering, so the test that does run is the test that was designed.

Skip the skill when the change is so small that a one-week ship-and-watch is cheaper than instrumenting an experiment, when the eligible population is too small to detect any plausible effect within a quarter, when the change cannot be cleanly randomized (because it affects everyone, like a backend rewrite), or when the team has not agreed on what success looks like — design rests on a goal, and if there is no goal the first deliverable is a different conversation.

## How to apply

1. **Restate the change as a single sentence.** Begin the plan with a one-line summary in the form "We will show {variant} instead of {control} to {eligible users} starting at {date or trigger}." If the requester used vague verbs like "improve" or "optimize," replace them with the concrete change. Vague verbs produce vague tests.
2. **Write the hypothesis in three parts.** Use a literal "Because {observation}, we believe that {change} will cause {outcome} for {users}, measured by {metric} moving from {baseline} to at least {target}." Each clause does work. Observation grounds the test in evidence, not in a stakeholder's hunch. Outcome forces a direction, not a vague "impact." Measured-by names the primary metric, which prevents post-hoc metric switching. If any clause is hard to fill, that is itself a finding — surface it in the open-questions output rather than papering over it.
3. **Identify the primary metric.** Choose exactly one metric the test is designed to move. It must be measurable on every assigned unit, attributable to the experiment surface, and directionally aligned with the business goal. Examples by goal — for "more signups" use signup-completion rate per visitor; for "more revenue" use revenue per visitor (not per buyer, which inflates when fewer people buy); for "less churn" use 30-day retained rate per cohort. Reject vanity metrics (clicks on the variant, scroll depth on the variant) that cannot be hurt by control behavior.
4. **Add two or three secondary metrics that explain why.** Secondary metrics are diagnostic, not decision-making. They include intermediate funnel steps, engagement signals, and quality indicators that help interpret a primary-metric move. Mark each clearly as "diagnostic, not for decision."
5. **Specify the guardrail metrics that can block a ship.** Guardrails are the things you refuse to harm. Standard guardrails include page load latency, error rate, customer support contact rate, refund or chargeback rate, downstream funnel drop-off for non-target steps, churn within 30 days, and high-value-segment revenue. Give each guardrail a numerical bar — a non-inferiority threshold expressed as "no worse than X% absolute or Y% relative degradation at 95% confidence." Guardrails without bars are wishes.
6. **Pick the randomization unit deliberately.** The default is the lowest unit that fully contains the change exposure — visitor cookie for marketing-site changes, logged-in user id for product changes, account id for B2B multi-seat products, session id for stateless changes that can switch within a visit. Avoid randomization at a finer grain than the unit of consumption (a per-request randomization on a UI element causes within-session flicker and contaminates the analysis). Avoid randomization at a coarser grain than necessary (account-level on a consumer product slashes statistical power for no reason). State the unit and one sentence on why the alternatives were rejected.
7. **Define eligibility before assignment.** Eligibility is the set of units that will be exposed to the experiment surface even in control — for example, "logged-in users on the pricing page on the web app, in any locale, on any device, who have not previously seen the v2 pricing." Assignment happens at first eligibility, not at first visit. Document the inclusion and exclusion filters as a small bullet list. Common exclusions: internal employees, bot user agents, accounts flagged as test fixtures, users in jurisdictions where the change is not permitted, users mid-flow in a mutually exclusive test.
8. **Calculate the minimum detectable effect (MDE).** Ask the requester or the stakeholder one concrete question: "What is the smallest change in the primary metric that would change what we do next?" If the answer is "a 0.5% absolute lift on a 4% base," size for that, not for the lift you wish for. A larger MDE is faster to detect but blinds you to small real wins; a smaller MDE costs traffic and time. Surface the tradeoff explicitly.
9. **Compute sample size and duration.** For a two-sided test of two proportions with significance 0.05 and power 0.80, the per-arm sample is approximately 16 × p × (1 − p) / MDE² where p is the baseline rate and MDE is expressed in absolute proportion. For continuous metrics with mean m and standard deviation s, the per-arm sample is approximately 16 × s² / (m × relative-MDE)². Multiply by the number of arms. Convert to weeks by dividing by weekly eligible traffic. Round up. Add a "minimum duration" floor of one full business cycle (typically 7 days) to capture day-of-week effects, even if the math says fewer days suffice — short tests miss weekly seasonality. State the resulting duration as a range, with the lower bound being the math and the upper bound being the practical "we will stop if no decision by" date.
10. **Reject under-powered tests at the design stage.** If duration exceeds the available window, present the options in order: increase traffic share by widening eligibility; raise the MDE the test is willing to accept and explain the implication; reduce the number of arms; combine with another test using a covariate-adjusted analysis; or abandon the test and recommend a different evaluation method (qualitative research, sequential rollout with rollback, or holdout). Do not propose running an under-powered test "to see what happens" — that is how false positives become product decisions.
11. **Specify exposure logging.** The system must emit an "exposure event" the first time each eligible unit sees the experiment surface, regardless of which variant. The exposure event includes unit id, experiment id, variant id, timestamp, and the eligibility context (page, route, server time, locale, device). This event is the spine of the analysis. Without exposure logging, the analysis falls back to comparing all-traffic conversion rates and bakes in selection bias. Make the engineering owner sign off that exposure logging is wired before the test starts.
12. **Specify the assignment function.** Assignment is deterministic on (unit id, experiment id) — typically a stable hash modulo the traffic split. Document the split percentages, the salt or seed, and the rollout ramp (see step 13). Deterministic assignment means a returning user always sees the same variant. Non-deterministic assignment is a bug, not a feature.
13. **Plan the ramp.** Start at a small share — 1% per arm for a 24-hour smoke phase, then 10% per arm for a 48-hour validation phase, then the target split (often 50/50) for the rest of the run. The early phases catch instrumentation bugs (missing exposure events, wrong assignment, broken variant) before they consume statistical budget. Each ramp step has explicit health checks: SRM is within tolerance, error rates are flat, latency is flat, primary-metric outliers are not extreme.
14. **Include an SRM (sample ratio mismatch) check.** SRM compares the observed allocation between arms against the designed split. A chi-square p-value below 0.001 indicates a problem — the test is not actually randomized as designed, and any apparent lift is suspect. Make SRM a hard gate: if SRM trips during the smoke or validation phase, pause and investigate; do not press on.
15. **Decide the analysis approach in advance.** State the test statistic (typically a two-sample z-test for proportions or Welch's t-test for continuous outcomes), the multiple-comparisons handling if there is more than one primary metric or more than two arms (Bonferroni or Benjamini-Hochberg), and the segmentation cuts that will be reported as exploratory rather than confirmatory. Lock in the analysis plan to prevent garden-of-forking-paths drift after results land.
16. **Set decision criteria, not just significance criteria.** A common failure is "p < 0.05 → ship." Replace it with a decision matrix: (a) primary metric moves favorably, no guardrail breach, business case clears → ship; (b) primary metric moves favorably, guardrail breach → do not ship, investigate the tradeoff; (c) primary metric flat with tight confidence interval → do not ship, but document the negative result; (d) primary metric flat with wide confidence interval → inconclusive, decide whether to extend the test or abandon; (e) primary metric moves unfavorably → do not ship. Write the matrix into the plan so the decision is pre-committed.
17. **Forbid peeking unless using a sequential method.** Fixed-horizon tests must not be stopped early on "looking good" interim results. If business pressure requires early stopping, design with a sequential method (alpha-spending, group sequential boundaries, or always-valid confidence intervals from a sequential testing framework) from the start, not retrofit at the moment of temptation.
18. **Document interaction risk with other live tests.** List the currently live experiments that touch the same surface, the same users, or the same downstream metric. For each, decide whether to make the new test mutually exclusive (no overlap) or independent (overlap accepted with a planned interaction analysis). Mutually exclusive tests cost traffic but produce clean reads; independent tests preserve traffic but require larger effects to detect reliably.
19. **Name an experiment owner and a stakeholder reviewer.** The owner answers process questions and pulls the plug if guardrails breach. The reviewer is a second pair of eyes who signs off on the design before launch. For high-stakes tests (pricing, paywalls, anything regulated), add a third reviewer from legal, finance, or trust and safety as the brief requires.
20. **Write a rollback plan.** If a guardrail trips, what is the unwind? "Flip the flag to 100% control" is the right answer for a flag-gated UI test. For tests that touched persistent state — coupons issued, accounts upgraded, emails sent — the rollback is partial and the plan must say so explicitly, including who is responsible for the compensating action.
21. **Estimate downside cost.** Multiply the worst plausible variant-arm degradation in the primary metric by the variant traffic and the period of exposure. Compare to the upside if the test wins. If downside materially exceeds upside, redesign — shrink the variant traffic share, shorten the ramp, raise the SRM gate, or add a tighter guardrail. The brief is not "no risk"; the brief is "risk that is sized and accepted."
22. **Produce the readout template.** A test plan that does not include the shape of the readout often produces a readout that nobody reads. Sketch the headline, the primary-metric chart, the guardrails table, the segment table, the funnel diagnostic, the open issues list, and the recommended decision. The readout author will fill it in; pre-committing the shape prevents post-hoc framing.
23. **Surface the open questions.** Anything ambiguous in the inputs becomes an explicit question in the second output document, ordered by impact on the design. Common questions: what exactly is the baseline rate and over what window; what is the eligible population size; are there in-flight tests on the same surface; what is the deadline for a decision; who has authority to ship if results are positive but guardrails are flat-but-noisy.
24. **Match the rigor to the stakes.** A homepage hero copy test on a 5% lift hypothesis does not need the same blast-radius analysis as a paywall threshold change. Default to the full plan, but allow a "lightweight" mode that retains hypothesis, metrics, sample size, SRM gate, and decision criteria while abbreviating ramp and rollback when the surface and the stakes are low. State which mode the plan is in.
25. **Return two artifacts.** The experiment plan reads top-to-bottom: hypothesis, variants, eligibility, randomization, primary and secondary and guardrail metrics with bars, sample size and duration, ramp schedule, monitoring checklist, decision matrix, rollback, owner. The open-questions document is short, numbered, and ranked. Designers and engineers consume the plan; the requester answers the questions.

## Inputs

- `change_description` — what is being changed and where it appears.
- `business_goal` — the outcome the change is meant to move.
- `baseline_metric` (optional) — current conversion rate and the population it covers.
- `traffic` (optional) — weekly eligible visitors or event count.
- `randomization_unit` (optional) — visitor, user, session, account, device, or request.
- `constraints` (optional) — windows, regulatory blockers, mutually exclusive tests, regions excluded.

## Outputs

- A complete experiment plan: hypothesis, variants, eligibility, randomization unit, primary and secondary and guardrail metrics with non-inferiority bars, sample size and duration, ramp schedule, SRM gate, monitoring checklist, decision matrix, rollback, owner, and a readout template.
- A numbered list of open questions the requester should answer before launch, ranked by their impact on the design.

## Examples

**Example 1: Pricing-page hero rewrite, web marketing site.**

*Input* — change: replace the current four-line value-prop paragraph with a three-step "how it works" component above the plans; goal: lift paid signups from the pricing page; baseline: 3.2% paid-signup rate per pricing-page visitor over the last 8 weeks; traffic: ~42,000 eligible pricing-page visitors per week; randomization: visitor cookie.

*Plan sketch* — Hypothesis: "Because clarity drives action and current pricing visitors report confusion in five-of-eight user interviews, we believe replacing the value-prop paragraph with a three-step component will increase the per-visitor paid-signup rate from 3.2% to at least 3.5%." Primary metric: paid-signup rate per pricing-page visitor. Secondary diagnostics: pricing-page bounce rate, click rate to plan-select, time on page. Guardrails: support contact rate non-inferior by 0.2% absolute; mobile error rate non-inferior; revenue per visitor non-inferior by 1% relative. MDE: 0.3% absolute (≈9.4% relative). Sample size: roughly 16 × 0.032 × 0.968 / 0.003² ≈ 55,000 per arm; with 50/50 split on 42k/week eligible, ≈2.6 weeks; round to 3 weeks; minimum 7 days. Ramp: 1% for 24h, 10% for 48h, 50% for the remainder. SRM gate at chi-square p<0.001. Decision: ship on positive primary with all guardrails non-inferior; do not ship if guardrails breach; otherwise document and revisit.

**Example 2: Onboarding step removal, B2B SaaS.**

*Input* — change: skip the "invite teammates" step for trial signups; goal: lift trial-to-paid; baseline: 9% trial-to-paid over the trailing 90 days; traffic: ~1,400 trial starts per week; randomization: account id.

*Plan sketch* — Hypothesis names the observed friction (drop-off of 38% on the invite step) and the proposed change. Primary: trial-to-paid conversion within 14 days of trial start. Secondary diagnostics: time-to-first-value, day-7 retention, day-14 retention. Guardrails: teammate adoption non-inferior at day 30 (since the missing step might lower invites); refund rate non-inferior. MDE: a 1.5% absolute lift (a meaningful business move at this base rate). Sample size: roughly 16 × 0.09 × 0.91 / 0.015² ≈ 5,800 per arm; with 50/50 split on 1.4k/week and a delayed outcome that requires waiting 14 days after assignment, total exposure window ≈ 8 weeks plus 2 weeks observation. Surface the long duration as a tradeoff: tightening MDE to 1% would push to ≈18 weeks of exposure, which exceeds the planning window — recommend MDE 1.5%. Ramp: 5%, 25%, 50%. SRM gate. Open question: is the teammate-invite step optimized for accounts in the SMB segment versus enterprise — if the change differentially affects segments, pre-register the SMB/enterprise split as a confirmatory cut, not exploratory.

**Example 3: Checkout button label, mobile app.**

*Input* — change: change "Continue" to "Place order — $29.99 today"; goal: reduce abandonment at the final checkout step; baseline: 64% complete-rate on the final step; traffic: ~9,000 reach-final-step events per week; randomization: session id.

*Plan sketch* — Hypothesis frames the friction as price-disclosure-at-commit reducing surprise-driven abandonment. Primary: final-step completion rate per reaching-final-step session. Secondary diagnostics: refund/cancel rate within 24 hours of order (in case the label increases regret rather than reduces hesitation), customer-support contact rate, total revenue per checkout-started session. Guardrails: refund rate non-inferior by 0.5% absolute; chargeback rate non-inferior by 0.1% absolute. MDE: 1.5% absolute completion-rate lift. Sample size: roughly 16 × 0.64 × 0.36 / 0.015² ≈ 16,400 per arm; ≈4 weeks at 9k/week and 50/50 split. Ramp: 1%, 10%, 50%. SRM gate. Note that session-level randomization is correct here because the surface is reached once per session, the variant choice does not need to persist across sessions, and a within-session switch is structurally impossible.

## Limitations

- Sample-size formulas are approximations adequate for the design stage; production analyses should re-derive with the actual variance and adjust for any covariate corrections used.
- The skill does not write the assignment code or instrument the events; engineering owns implementation, and a real platform integration (any major feature-flag service, or an in-house equivalent) is assumed.
- Tests with delayed primary outcomes (week-30 retention, lifetime value, renewal rate) need additional discussion of stopping rules and surrogate metrics that this skill flags but does not solve.
- Network and clustering effects (marketplace tests where buyer and seller see different sides, viral mechanics, social products where users influence each other) need cluster-randomized designs that this skill explicitly does not produce.
- Multi-armed tests with more than four arms benefit from sequential allocation methods that this skill does not design.
- The skill assumes the experiment platform supports deterministic assignment, exposure logging, and SRM checks; if it does not, the plan calls that out as the first open question.
- Pricing experiments may carry regulatory and contractual implications (existing customer fairness, jurisdictional rules) that need legal review beyond the scope of this skill.
