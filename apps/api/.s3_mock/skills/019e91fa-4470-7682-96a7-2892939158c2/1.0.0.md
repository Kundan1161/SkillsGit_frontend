# Medical Narrative-vs-Meta-Analysis Decider

## When to use

Use this skill at the point in a review where the included-studies set is stable, extraction is far enough along to see the shape of the evidence, and the team must choose how to synthesize each outcome. The default for many teams is "pool if you can"; the right default is "pool if you should". This skill makes the should explicit.

This skill produces methodology guidance for literature reviews. Outputs are not clinical decisions or care recommendations. Every output must be reviewed by qualified clinical/research staff. Conclusions about treatment efficacy or safety must be confirmed against current clinical practice guidelines and primary literature.

Typical triggers:

- Extraction is complete for an outcome and the team is about to decide between a forest plot and a structured narrative.
- The protocol pre-specified meta-analysis but the included studies turned out to be more heterogeneous than expected.
- A reviewer disagrees with a proposed pooled estimate on grounds of comparability rather than statistics.
- A guideline panel wants both a pooled estimate where defensible and a narrative summary where not.
- A peer reviewer has asked the team to justify why they did (or did not) pool.

Do not use this skill for:

- Running the meta-analysis itself — that is the statistical-synthesis stage. This skill decides whether and what to pool.
- Deciding to do a network meta-analysis vs. pairwise meta-analysis — that decision pre-dates this one and lives in the protocol.
- Choosing the effect metric (risk ratio vs. odds ratio vs. risk difference) — this skill flags when no common metric exists across studies; the metric choice when one does exist is a synthesis-stage decision.

## Inputs

- `included_studies_summary` (required) — Per-study summary for the outcome under consideration: design, population, intervention details, comparator, outcome definition and timepoint, effect-metric as reported, summary statistics, and risk-of-bias judgment. Even a five-row table is enough to drive the decision.
- `clinical_question` (required) — The structured question and the audience. A pooled estimate carries a different responsibility for a guideline panel than for a hypothesis-generating review.
- `team_constraints` — Statistical capacity and timeline. A team without a statistician should not commit to a method it cannot defend.
- `pre_specified_plan` — What the protocol said. The decision must either match the protocol or be documented as a justified deviation.

## How to apply

Apply the steps in order per outcome. The output is a decision with explicit reasoning, not a vote.

### 1. Confirm what the protocol pre-specified

1.1. Locate the synthesis plan in the protocol for this specific outcome. Pre-specification is the strongest defense against confirmation bias in the synthesis decision.

1.2. If the protocol pre-specified pooling and the assessment supports it, proceed to step 6. If the protocol pre-specified narrative and the assessment supports it, proceed to step 7.

1.3. If the protocol's pre-specification appears no longer appropriate, the change is a **protocol deviation**. Deviations are allowed but must be documented with: the reason, the date, who decided, and the impact assessment. The decision document this skill produces is the deviation log entry.

### 2. Examine clinical comparability

2.1. Walk through the **populations**: are they similar enough that a single pooled estimate is meaningful? Differences in age, sex, comorbidity, severity, and setting can each erode comparability.

2.2. Walk through the **interventions**: are agents, doses, durations, and co-interventions similar enough? An intervention class with very different mechanisms across studies should not be pooled without an explicit class-effect assumption.

2.3. Walk through the **comparators**: is the comparator the same across studies? Placebo vs. active comparators are not interchangeable; pooling across them is rarely defensible without a structured network analysis.

2.4. Walk through the **outcomes**: are the outcome definitions and timepoints comparable? Different definitions of the same named outcome can drive heterogeneity invisibly.

2.5. If clinical comparability fails on any of these axes substantively, pooling is hard to defend even with low statistical heterogeneity. Move toward narrative or structure by subgroup.

### 3. Examine the methodological comparability

3.1. Compare **study designs**. Pooling across designs is sometimes done but requires explicit justification and a sensitivity analysis. Pooling within a single design is the more defensible default.

3.2. Compare **measurement instruments** within the outcome. Different scales measuring the same construct may be poolable via a standardized metric; different scales measuring related-but-distinct constructs are not.

3.3. Compare **risk-of-bias profiles**. A body dominated by high-risk studies is a poor candidate for a confident pooled estimate; the pooling may be technically possible but the interpretation is constrained.

### 4. Examine the availability of a common effect metric

4.1. List the metric each study reports. Common categories:
   - **Binary outcomes** — risk ratio, odds ratio, hazard ratio, risk difference. Conversion across these is sometimes possible with assumptions but introduces error.
   - **Continuous outcomes** — mean difference (when the same scale), standardized mean difference (when different scales of the same construct).
   - **Time-to-event outcomes** — hazard ratio, ideally with the variance derivable from the reported CI.
   - **Count or rate outcomes** — rate ratio with person-time denominator.

4.2. Decide whether a **common effect metric** exists or can be derived for all (or substantially all) included studies. If not, pooling is structurally infeasible for the outcome; move toward narrative.

4.3. For partial coverage — e.g., 6 of 8 studies have the common metric — consider pooling the eligible studies and narratively integrating the others. Be explicit about what the pool does and does not represent.

### 5. Examine the heterogeneity in the planned pool

5.1. Pre-pool sanity check: with the available studies on the common metric, what is the spread? Visual inspection of the forest plot — including a draft random-effects estimate — is fast and informative.

5.2. Examine the **direction of effect**: do all studies favor one arm? Mixed direction is a stronger signal than mixed magnitude.

5.3. Examine the **heterogeneity statistics**: I-squared, tau-squared, Q-test. Each has known limitations; combine them with the visual and clinical assessment rather than threshold-mechanically.

5.4. Examine the **leverage**: does one large study dominate the pool? A pool driven by a single study is a forest plot disguised as a meta-analysis; report the single study and the others narratively or in a subgroup.

5.5. If heterogeneity is explained by a **pre-specified subgroup**, pool by subgroup and report the subgroup-specific estimates. If heterogeneity is unexplained and substantial, narrative synthesis is the safer route.

### 6. Decision: quantitative pool

6.1. Choose if all of the following are true: clinical and methodological comparability is acceptable, a common effect metric is available for substantially all studies, heterogeneity is low or fully explained by a pre-specified subgroup, the number of studies is sufficient to make the pooled estimate meaningful (typically ≥2 for very simple questions, more commonly ≥4 for meaningful precision), and the team has the statistical capacity to run and interpret the model.

6.2. Specify the **model family** — fixed-effect vs. random-effects, with the estimator. Document the rationale: small number of studies with a likely common effect favors fixed-effect; expected variability in true effects across populations or interventions favors random-effects.

6.3. Specify the **effect metric** and the **transformation** if any (e.g., log-transform for ratios, Hedges' correction for standardized mean differences).

6.4. Plan the **sensitivity analyses**: leave-one-out, by risk-of-bias category, by pre-specified subgroup, by alternative model family.

6.5. Plan the **diagnostic checks**: heterogeneity statistics, small-study-effects assessment if number of studies permits, influence plots, prediction interval.

6.6. Plan the **reporting**: forest plot, summary of heterogeneity, prediction interval, sensitivity-analysis summary.

### 7. Decision: structured narrative synthesis

7.1. Choose if any of the following are true: clinical or methodological comparability is poor, no common effect metric is available, heterogeneity is substantial and unexplained, the number of studies is too small for a meaningful pool, or the team lacks statistical capacity to defend a pool.

7.2. Use a structured narrative method — for example, grouping by intervention type, by population subgroup, or by outcome type. Avoid pure narrative storytelling; the structure is what makes narrative synthesis defensible.

7.3. Plan the **tabulation**: a structured table per outcome with one row per study, columns for design, population, intervention, comparator, effect estimate as reported, and risk of bias. The table is the synthesis; the text walks the reader through it.

7.4. **Vote counting** based on statistical significance is discouraged. If it is used, it must be explicit, accompanied by direction-of-effect counts independent of significance, and presented with clear limitations. Direction-of-effect vote counting is more defensible than significance-based.

7.5. Plan the **synthesis narrative structure**: lead with the most consistent finding across studies, then describe departures, then describe outcomes where evidence is too sparse to characterize.

7.6. Plan the **certainty assessment** for the narrative outcome. The same five considerations from quantitative grading apply, with adjustments for the absence of a pooled estimate.

### 8. Decision: pool a subset, narrate the rest

8.1. Choose when most studies share a common metric and comparability profile, but a minority diverge.

8.2. Pool the eligible subset, and **narratively integrate** the divergent studies. Be explicit about which studies are in the pool, which are not, and why.

8.3. Plan a **sensitivity analysis** including the divergent studies if the divergent metric can be roughly converted — present both and note the conversion assumption.

8.4. Address the question of generalizability: does the pool represent the question, or only the easy part of it?

### 9. Decision: no synthesis (rare)

9.1. Choose when the included studies are so heterogeneous, so few, or so high-risk that any synthesis would mislead.

9.2. Plan a **structured catalog** rather than a synthesis — a table of what was studied, where, and what was found, with a clear statement that the question cannot yet be answered from this body of evidence.

9.3. Identify the **evidence gap** explicitly. A clear gap statement is often the most useful product of a review of this kind.

### 10. Validate the decision against an internal checklist

Before emitting, verify:

- Pre-specification was located and either matched or the deviation is documented.
- Clinical comparability across population, intervention, comparator, outcome was assessed.
- Methodological comparability across design, instrument, risk of bias was assessed.
- A common effect metric was either confirmed available, derived, or absent.
- Heterogeneity was inspected visually, statistically, and by leverage.
- Subgroup analyses pre-specified in the protocol were considered as the alternative to a single pool.
- The chosen path matches the criteria for that path; the reasoning is documented.
- The synthesis workplan is concrete enough to execute (model, metric, structure, table format).

### 11. Emit the decision and the workplan

The decision document is one short reasoning section per outcome plus the workplan. If the team produces this skill's output before the synthesis stage, the synthesis stage's output should match the workplan or document the further deviation.

### Decision rules and heuristics

- **Comparability before computability.** If studies are not clinically comparable, statistical pooling is a technical exercise without an interpretable result.
- **Pre-specification is a covenant.** Honor the protocol or document the change; do not silently shift the synthesis approach.
- **Narrative synthesis is not a fallback.** Done well, it is a synthesis method in its own right with its own structure and rigor.
- **Pooling a small number of studies is a precision issue, not an impossibility.** With two studies, a pool is possible but the prediction interval will be wide; lean toward narrative unless the two studies are very compatible.
- **A subgroup that explains heterogeneity is a real finding.** Do not bury it by averaging across subgroups.
- **A leverage-dominated pool is a single study in disguise.** Read the forest plot, do not just read the diamond.
- **Mixed direction is the strongest no-pool signal.** Heterogeneity in magnitude can sometimes be modeled; heterogeneity in direction usually cannot.
- **Vote counting by significance is rarely defensible.** If used, pair with direction-of-effect counts and explicit limitations.

### Edge cases

- **Two studies, very compatible.** A two-study pool is technically possible but provides little precision gain over a structured side-by-side comparison; choose narrative if the two studies report adequately, pool only if the synthesis question demands a single estimate.
- **Studies with different timepoints for the same outcome.** Consider whether the timepoints are interpretable as the same construct (e.g., 12 weeks vs. 14 weeks for chronic disease); pool only if the differences are small relative to the disease course, otherwise pool within timepoint windows or narrate.
- **Studies with different scales for a continuous outcome.** A standardized metric is sometimes feasible but the interpretation is the standardized units, not the clinically meaningful units; consider back-transforming to a reference scale for reporting.
- **One outlier study with a very large effect.** Do not exclude on the basis of effect size; investigate clinical or methodological reasons (different population, different intervention nuance, risk of bias). Include with sensitivity exclusion.
- **All studies industry-funded.** Pooling is still possible; the certainty assessment carries the bias signal, not the synthesis decision.
- **Mix of randomized and non-randomized evidence.** Generally analyse separately and integrate at the interpretation stage rather than pool across designs.
- **Diagnostic-accuracy studies.** Pooling uses bivariate or hierarchical models with sensitivity and specificity as paired outcomes; the decision to pool follows the same clinical-comparability logic but the statistical method is different from therapy meta-analysis.
- **Prognostic-model performance studies.** Pooling discrimination metrics (e.g., c-statistic) requires homogeneity in population, predictors, and validation method; often narrative summary is more defensible.

## Outputs

- `synthesis_decision_markdown` — Per-outcome decision (pool / narrative / both / no synthesis) with reasoning across comparability, metric availability, heterogeneity, and leverage.
- `synthesis_workplan` — Concrete next steps for the chosen path, including the model and metric for pooling or the framework and table structure for narrative.
- `open_questions` — Items requiring methodology-lead confirmation before the synthesis stage proceeds.

## Examples

### Worked example (abbreviated)

Input:

> Outcome: depressive symptoms at 12 weeks post-randomization.
> 8 RCTs of digital CBT vs. waitlist or usual care, ages 13-18, total N ~1,800.
> Outcome instruments: 5 trials used a 9-item self-report depression scale; 2 trials used
>   a 13-item adolescent depression scale; 1 trial used a clinician-rated 17-item scale.
> Comparator: 5 trials waitlist, 3 trials usual care.
> Effect direction: 7 trials favor intervention, 1 favors comparator (small effect, wide CI).
> Heterogeneity: I-squared 62% on the standardized mean difference across all 8.

Expected synthesis decision (abbreviated):

```
Outcome: depressive symptoms at 12 weeks

Pre-specified plan: random-effects meta-analysis on standardized mean difference.

Clinical comparability:
  - Population: comparable (adolescents 13-18).
  - Intervention: digital CBT but with different module counts (range 4-12). Moderate variation.
  - Comparator: waitlist vs. usual care differ in expected attention effects. Substantive difference.
  - Outcome: three different scales of the same construct; SMD makes them comparable but
    not identical.

Methodological comparability: all RCTs; risk-of-bias mostly low with two trials at some concerns.

Common metric: SMD is derivable for all 8 trials.

Heterogeneity: I-squared 62%, mostly explained on visual inspection by comparator type
  (waitlist trials show larger SMD than usual-care trials).

Decision: pool by subgroup. Two pooled estimates:
  - Subgroup A: vs. waitlist (5 trials, SMD calculation, random-effects).
  - Subgroup B: vs. usual care (3 trials, SMD calculation, random-effects).
  Overall pooled SMD across all 8 is reported as a secondary analysis with the
  heterogeneity statistic and the subgroup explanation.

Sensitivity: leave-one-out within each subgroup; sensitivity excluding the clinician-rated
  outcome to test instrument effect.

Reporting: forest plot with subgroup panels; narrative emphasizes the waitlist-vs-usual-care
  distinction; the overall estimate is presented with the caveat that the comparator difference
  drives most of the heterogeneity.
```

`synthesis_workplan` example:

```
Pool subgroup A (vs. waitlist):
  Model: random-effects, restricted maximum likelihood estimator
  Metric: SMD (Hedges' g) with 95% CI
  Heterogeneity: I-squared, tau-squared, prediction interval
  Sensitivity: leave-one-out

Pool subgroup B (vs. usual care):
  Same model as subgroup A

Overall pool:
  Reported as secondary, with subgroup-by-treatment-effect test

Narrative for the one trial with reversed direction:
  Describe in the text and in the forest plot; do not exclude

Table for the report:
  Per-study row: trial, design, n, intervention, comparator, instrument, SMD (CI), risk of bias
```

`open_questions` example output:

```
- Confirm the comparator-subgroup decision; alternative is to pool overall and downgrade
  for inconsistency in the certainty assessment.
- Confirm the SMD transformation rule when the clinician-rated scale is included.
- Approve the prediction-interval reporting; some readers find it more useful than the CI
  of the pooled estimate.
- Decide whether to back-transform SMD to one reference scale for the summary-of-findings table.
```

## Limitations

- The decision is a judgment, not a calculation. Two qualified reviewers can defensibly disagree; the value is in the reasoning being explicit.
- The skill assumes the included-studies set is stable. New inclusions can shift the decision; re-run when the set changes materially.
- The skill does not run the meta-analysis or write the narrative. It chooses the path and lays out the workplan.
- The skill does not adjudicate between specific statistical models within a chosen path; the team's statistician makes those calls.
- The skill assumes a pairwise comparison frame; network meta-analysis decisions are pre-specified upstream and follow different criteria.
