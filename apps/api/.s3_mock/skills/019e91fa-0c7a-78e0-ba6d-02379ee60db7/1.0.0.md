# 510(k) Predicate Comparison Architect

## When to use

**Mandatory disclaimer.** This skill produces methodology guidance only. Outputs are not regulatory submissions. Real submissions must be authored, reviewed, and signed by qualified regulatory affairs staff and may require external counsel. No output may be filed with any regulator without sponsor authorization.

Invoke this skill when a sponsor has converged on a 510(k) pathway (see the strategy planner skill if not yet) and needs to construct the substantial-equivalence argument: the structured comparison between the subject (new) device and one or more predicate devices that anchors the entire submission. Typical entry points: a regulatory writer drafting the substantial-equivalence section of the 510(k) summary; a regulatory affairs lead pressure-testing two candidate predicates before committing to one; a product manager whose engineering team has changed a component and needs to confirm the substantial-equivalence argument still holds; a consultant preparing a pre-submission package for FDA feedback on predicate strategy.

Do not use this skill for:

- Devices with no plausible predicate. If the technological characteristics are genuinely novel and no cleared device performs the same intended use with comparable methodology, the right pathway is De Novo classification, not a forced substantial-equivalence argument. The skill will detect this and recommend a pathway change rather than fabricate a comparison.
- PMA-class devices. Substantial-equivalence is a 510(k) concept; PMA approval rests on independent demonstration of safety and effectiveness, not equivalence.
- Combination products where the device constituent is secondary; the predicate-comparison framework applies only to the device-led pathway.
- Pre-product-definition exploration. The skill assumes the subject device is well-enough defined that intended use, indications, and key technological characteristics are stable. Producing a comparison against a moving target wastes the comparison.

The methodology is conservative on the "not substantially equivalent" boundary. When the differences accumulate to a point where the safety and effectiveness questions diverge from the predicate's, the skill will flag the risk of an NSE determination and recommend either a pre-submission to validate the predicate strategy, additional bridging evidence, or a pathway change to De Novo. A 510(k) that gets converted to a De Novo mid-review is more expensive and slower than one that started as a De Novo intentionally.

## How to apply

Substantial equivalence is not a similarity score. It is a structured argument that the subject device has (a) the same intended use as the predicate and (b) the same technological characteristics — or, if different, that the differences do not raise different questions of safety and effectiveness *and* are supported by performance data. The methodology below walks the argument the way FDA reviewers walk it.

1. **Lock the subject device description before comparing anything.** The substantial-equivalence argument is fragile to subject-device drift. Confirm — or surface as a missing input — each of: intended use; indications for use (which is a subset of intended use specifying the disease/condition, patient population, and clinical setting); principles of operation; energy source; materials of patient contact; key performance specifications. If any of these is still being negotiated by the engineering or clinical team, flag that the comparison output is provisional until they freeze.

2. **Evaluate each candidate predicate against the four-corner test.** A device is eligible as a 510(k) predicate if it is legally marketed in the US and has not been removed from the market for safety or effectiveness reasons. For each candidate:
   - Is it legally marketed? (Was it cleared via 510(k), grandfathered as preamendments, or classified into Class I/II without a 510(k) requirement?)
   - Has it been recalled or withdrawn for safety or effectiveness? (A Class I recall is usually disqualifying; a Class II recall may be navigable but flag it.)
   - Is the predicate's intended use compatible with the subject device's intended use?
   - Are the technological characteristics close enough that a comparison is plausible (i.e., not a "stretch predicate")?
   Reject any candidate that fails the first two. Note any that fail the third or fourth with the specific reason.

3. **Build the intended-use comparison first.** Intended use is the gating question. If the intended uses differ — e.g., the predicate is indicated for "monitoring" but the subject device claims "diagnosis," or the predicate is indicated for "adults" but the subject device claims "pediatric" — the substantial-equivalence argument is in trouble from sentence one. Construct a side-by-side comparison with three columns: subject, predicate, same-or-different. Where wording differs but meaning is the same, note "same in substance, different in wording" — but be conservative; reviewers read literally.

4. **Build the indications-for-use comparison next.** Indications for use are more specific than intended use — they name the disease/condition, the patient population, the user (clinician vs. patient vs. layperson), and the use environment (hospital vs. home vs. emergency setting). A subject-device indication that expands any of these dimensions vs. the predicate creates a "new indication" question that may push the submission toward De Novo. Common expansion patterns to flag aggressively:
   - **Population expansion** (adults → pediatric, neonatal, geriatric).
   - **Setting expansion** (hospital → home use; ICU → general ward).
   - **User expansion** (trained clinician → consumer / OTC).
   - **Disease scope expansion** (specific disease → "general monitoring").
   Each expansion is a target for bridging data; some — particularly OTC and pediatric — may require entire new categories of evidence (human factors / use error studies, pediatric subgroup analyses).

5. **Build the technological-characteristics comparison.** This is the core of the SE argument. The table should include (at minimum): principles of operation, energy source, materials of patient contact, software/algorithm description, sensor specifications and modalities, output/display characteristics, sterility/biocompatibility status, electrical-safety classification, EMC classification, cybersecurity posture for connected devices. For each row:
   - **Same** — note as same. No bridging required.
   - **Different but does not raise different questions of safety and effectiveness** — explicit rationale plus bridging-evidence reference (bench test, biocompatibility report, software documentation). This is the heart of substantial-equivalence work.
   - **Different and raises different questions of safety and effectiveness** — this is the SE-killer. Either generate the evidence to demonstrate the safety question is addressed (often via clinical data), or accept that the predicate strategy is broken.

6. **Use a split-predicate strategy only when necessary, and document it cleanly.** A split-predicate (technically "primary predicate + reference predicate") strategy claims one predicate for intended use and a separate cleared device as a reference for a specific technological characteristic. This is acceptable under current FDA practice when documented carefully:
   - The primary predicate must match intended use and indications.
   - The reference device must be cleared for the specific technological characteristic being borrowed (e.g., a particular sensor modality, communication protocol, or material).
   - The argument must connect explicitly: "We rely on Predicate A for intended use and indications; we reference Cleared Device B as the basis for our Bluetooth Low Energy wireless interface, which has been demonstrated safe in B's clearance."
   Avoid the appearance of a "Frankenstein predicate" built by cherry-picking convenient features from multiple devices; reviewers consistently push back on this.

7. **Build the performance-data comparison.** Where performance specifications differ, the sponsor must show that the subject-device performance is at least as good as the predicate's *or* that the differences do not raise safety/effectiveness questions. Performance comparison rows: accuracy (point estimates, confidence intervals, bias, limits of agreement for measurement devices); sensitivity/specificity for diagnostic devices; battery life; signal-to-noise; alarm thresholds; durability; sterility-assurance level; shelf life; biocompatibility test results; EMC immunity/emissions levels; cybersecurity controls coverage.

8. **Map every difference to bridging evidence — and flag the gaps.** For each "different" row in any comparison table, ask: what evidence in the submission supports that the difference is acceptable? Acceptable bridging evidence includes:
   - **Bench testing** — most common; appropriate for hardware specifications, software output verification, biocompatibility, sterility.
   - **Animal testing** — appropriate when the difference is in tissue-contacting characteristics that bench testing cannot adequately simulate.
   - **Clinical testing** — required when bench/animal data cannot adequately address the safety/effectiveness question. Common triggers: novel signal-processing algorithm with clinical impact, new indication, new use environment.
   - **Human factors / usability testing** — required when the user, use environment, or interface has materially changed, especially for OTC and home-use devices.
   - **Cybersecurity testing** — required for any device with network or wireless connectivity; FDA premarket cybersecurity expectations now include SBOM, threat modeling, vulnerability assessment, and penetration testing.
   For each row, list the planned evidence and its status (planned / in progress / complete). Rows with status "planned" or "in progress" are gaps; rows with no evidence at all are red flags.

9. **Draft the SE conclusion explicitly.** The SE conclusion is a short paragraph that states: (a) the subject device has the same intended use as the predicate; (b) the technological characteristics are the same or, where different, the differences do not raise different questions of safety and effectiveness; (c) the performance data demonstrate that the device is as safe and effective as the predicate. Each clause should be grounded in a specific row of the comparison tables. Avoid vague language; reviewers look for the specific syllogism.

10. **Surface the NSE risk explicitly.** If the comparison reveals (a) intended-use divergence, (b) indications-for-use expansion into populations or settings the predicate does not address, (c) technological characteristics that raise safety questions the predicate does not raise, or (d) performance gaps that are not bridged by adequate data — the risk of a Not Substantially Equivalent determination is elevated. Output a `risk_of_not_se` field (low/medium/high) with a one-line rationale, and recommend either a pre-submission to validate, additional bridging data, or a pathway change.

11. **Recommend a pre-submission when predicate strategy is non-obvious.** A Q-Sub (pre-submission) costs the sponsor a briefing package and four to eight weeks; an NSE determination costs months and the morale of the program. When the predicate strategy involves split predicates, OTC/home expansion, AI/ML components, or any indication-language expansion, recommend a Q-Sub before the predicate strategy locks.

12. **Self-check before returning.** Confirm: (a) each candidate predicate has been evaluated against the four-corner test; (b) the intended-use comparison is present and complete; (c) the indications-for-use comparison flags any expansion explicitly; (d) every "different" row in the technological-characteristics table has a bridging-evidence reference or is flagged as a gap; (e) the SE conclusion draft is grounded in specific table rows; (f) the disclaimer is present; (g) the NSE-risk rating is justified.

13. **When predicate strategy is genuinely broken, say so and recommend De Novo.** Do not produce a comparison that strains the substantial-equivalence concept past the breaking point. Reviewers see through it, and the sponsor pays for the wasted submission cycle. A clear "this device should pursue De Novo; here is why" recommendation is more valuable than a forced SE argument.

## Inputs

- `subject_device_description` (required, text) — must include intended use, indications, principles of operation, materials, energy source, performance specs. Sparse inputs produce sparse outputs.
- `candidate_predicates` (required, text) — one or more candidates with 510(k) numbers and key descriptors. The agent does not access FDA's database in real time; it works from what the sponsor provides.
- `reference_devices` (optional, text) — for split-predicate strategies.
- `differences_acknowledged` (optional, text) — pre-flagged differences. Helps the agent focus.
- `performance_data_summary` (optional, text) — what data exists or is planned. The agent will map data to differences and flag uncovered ones.

## Outputs

- `comparison_report` (markdown) — predicate selection rationale; intended-use comparison table; indications-for-use comparison table; technological-characteristics comparison table; performance-data comparison table; bridging-evidence map with gap flags; SE conclusion draft; NSE-risk rating with rationale; pre-submission recommendation if warranted.
- `comparison_json` (JSON) — `predicate_selection`, `comparison_tables` (intended_use, indications, tech_characteristics, performance — each as array of rows), `gaps` (array of {difference, current_evidence, recommended_action}), `se_conclusion_draft`, `risk_of_not_se` (low/medium/high), `risk_rationale`, `pre_submission_recommended` (bool), `pathway_change_recommended` (bool, true when De Novo is the cleaner path), `confidence` (float), `confidence_reason`.

## Examples

### Example 1 — clean single-predicate comparison

**Input subject_device_description (excerpt):** A wrist-worn pulse oximeter for adult, hospital-setting use, with red/IR LED sensors, OLED display, internal battery, no wireless connectivity. Accuracy claim: ±2% Arms across 70-100% SpO2.

**Input candidate_predicates:** K20XXXX, a cleared wrist-worn pulse oximeter with the same intended use, indications, sensor modality, and accuracy claim of ±2.5% Arms.

**Output comparison_report (excerpt):**

> **Predicate selection.** K20XXXX is selected as the sole predicate. It is legally marketed, cleared via 510(k), and not subject to a current safety recall.
>
> **Intended use:** Same. Both devices are intended for spot-check and continuous measurement of functional oxygen saturation of arterial hemoglobin (SpO2) in adult patients in hospital settings.
>
> **Indications for use:** Same in substance. Wording differs ("hospital and clinical settings" vs. "hospital settings") but the population, condition, and setting are equivalent.
>
> **Technological characteristics:** Same on principles of operation (red/IR transmissive pulse oximetry), energy source (lithium-ion battery), materials of patient contact (medical-grade silicone strap), and display modality. **Different on accuracy**: subject device ±2% Arms vs. predicate ±2.5% Arms. Bridging evidence: bench-tested accuracy study against arterial blood gas controlled-desaturation per ISO 80601-2-61, demonstrating subject-device accuracy is at least as good as predicate.
>
> **SE conclusion draft.** The subject device has the same intended use and indications for use as the predicate. The technological characteristics are the same in principles of operation and materials; the accuracy specification is tighter than the predicate, and the bench-test data demonstrate that the subject device is at least as safe and effective as the predicate. The subject device is substantially equivalent to K20XXXX.
>
> **NSE risk: low.** Confidence: 0.85.

### Example 2 — indication-expansion trap

**Input subject_device_description (excerpt):** A wrist-worn pulse oximeter intended for adult and pediatric use in home and clinical settings. OTC sold.

**Input candidate_predicates:** K20XXXX, cleared for adult hospital use only.

**Output comparison_report (excerpt):**

> **NSE risk: high.**
>
> The intended-use comparison shows three expansion vectors: (a) pediatric population not in the predicate's indications; (b) home-use setting not in the predicate's indications; (c) OTC consumer-user not in the predicate's indications. Each expansion individually creates a different question of safety/effectiveness; in combination they likely place the subject device outside the substantial-equivalence band.
>
> **Recommendation:** either (i) restrict the subject device's indications to match the predicate and pursue 510(k) on the narrower indications, then expand via post-clearance change submissions backed by appropriate data; or (ii) pursue a De Novo classification request with the expanded indications, recognizing the higher evidence bar including human-factors and pediatric data.
>
> Pre-submission strongly recommended before any predicate commitment.

### Example 3 — split-predicate done cleanly

**Input subject_device_description (excerpt):** A wrist-worn pulse oximeter for adult hospital use with Bluetooth Low Energy wireless data export to a paired tablet display. ±2% Arms accuracy.

**Input candidate_predicates:** Primary: K20XXXX (cleared adult hospital pulse oximeter, no wireless). Reference: K19YYYY (cleared adult patient monitor with BLE wireless data export).

**Output comparison_report (excerpt):**

> **Predicate strategy:** Primary predicate K20XXXX for intended use, indications, and core SpO2 measurement. Reference device K19YYYY for the BLE wireless interface, which is cleared under K19YYYY with the same BLE 5.0 stack and the same cybersecurity controls (SBOM, threat model, controlled provisioning).
>
> **Technological characteristics — BLE wireless:** Different from primary predicate (no wireless); same in substance as reference device K19YYYY. Bridging evidence: cybersecurity test report including threat model, SBOM, vulnerability assessment, and penetration test report; BLE interoperability test against named tablet receiver.
>
> **SE conclusion draft.** The subject device has the same intended use and indications as the primary predicate K20XXXX. The wireless interface, which is the principal technological difference from the primary predicate, is supported by reference to K19YYYY and by cybersecurity test data demonstrating that the difference does not raise different questions of safety and effectiveness.
>
> **NSE risk: medium.** Recommend Q-Sub to confirm the split-predicate strategy and the cybersecurity test plan before submission. Confidence: 0.70.

## Limitations

- **Not regulatory advice.** Outputs are methodology drafts. A qualified regulatory affairs professional must verify the predicate strategy, the SE argument, and the submission.
- **No real-time FDA database access.** The skill cannot verify a candidate predicate's clearance status, indications language, or recall history in real time. Sponsors must verify against the live 510(k) database.
- **Predicate-strategy nuance is moving.** FDA periodically updates its expectations on split predicates, AI/ML predicates, and indication-language expansion. Treat the methodology as durable principles, not current-state policy.
- **Cybersecurity, AI/ML, and human factors are deeper than this skill goes.** The skill flags when these are in scope and recommends the relevant submission appendices, but does not attempt to draft them. Pair with the appropriate specialist skill or external expertise.
- **Performance-data adequacy is a clinical/statistical judgment.** Whether a particular bench-test design, animal study, or clinical study is adequate for a particular difference is a question for biostatisticians and clinical experts; this skill flags gaps but does not validate study design.
- **Reference devices are limited to specific characteristics.** A reference device cannot be used to extend the intended use or indications of the subject device. The skill enforces this in the comparison structure.
- **Bias toward conservatism.** When uncertain about substantial equivalence, the methodology defaults to recommending Q-Sub or De Novo rather than asserting equivalence. False positives on equivalence are far more expensive than false negatives.
