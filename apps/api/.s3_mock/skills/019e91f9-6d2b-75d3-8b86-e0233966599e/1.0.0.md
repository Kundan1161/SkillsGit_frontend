# Records of Processing Activities (RoPA) Author

> **Important — not legal advice and not a compliance determination.** This skill produces methodology guidance only. Compliance determinations require qualified counsel and, for regulated entities, formal program ownership (Privacy Officer, Security Officer, Compliance Officer, or equivalent). No skill output constitutes legal advice, satisfies a regulator's evidence requirement on its own, or substitutes for jurisdiction-specific analysis. Real submissions and notices must be authored, reviewed, and signed by qualified staff before any external action. The RoPA is the regulator's primary accountability artifact under Article 30 and is among the first documents a supervisory authority requests when opening an investigation. Treat every output here as a draft to harden with the Data Protection Officer or qualified privacy counsel before any production to a regulator or partner.

## When to use

Invoke this skill when an organization that processes personal data in the EU or UK needs to author, refresh, or audit its Record of Processing Activities under Article 30 of the GDPR. The right moments are the initial build (commonly during the rush to GDPR readiness, after the appointment of a DPO, after a merger, or after a notice from a supervisory authority that put the organization on a remediation plan); the periodic refresh (most mature programs run quarterly or biannual refreshes plus event-triggered updates); the post-launch update following a new product, new marketing technology, new HR system, or any other change that introduces a processing activity or materially changes purposes, recipients, retention, or transfers; the response to a supervisory authority production request, where the team has fifteen to thirty days to produce a current record; and the diligence phase of an acquisition where the acquirer wants to inherit a defensible accountability artifact rather than a pile of unreconciled spreadsheets.

Do not use this skill as a substitute for the underlying processing inventory. The RoPA documents reality; it does not create it. If the organization does not know what it is doing with personal data, run discovery first using this skill's discovery questions, then synthesize the RoPA. Do not use this skill in place of an Article 35 Data Protection Impact Assessment for high-risk processing — the RoPA is the inventory; the DPIA is the risk assessment for a specific activity that the inventory flags. Do not use this skill to manage records of processing for non-personal data, training-dataset lineage, or model cards; the obligation under Article 30 is specifically about personal data processed by the controller or processor.

A subtle correct invocation is the case where the organization is unsure whether Article 30 applies at all. The fewer-than-250-employees carve-out is narrow: the obligation still applies when the processing is likely to result in a risk to the rights and freedoms of data subjects, is not occasional, or includes special categories of data or data relating to criminal convictions and offences. In practice almost no employer-of-record, SaaS, marketplace, or health-adjacent business below 250 employees actually qualifies for the carve-out. When in doubt, build the record; the record itself is also the artifact that justifies any narrower scope decision.

## How to apply

Treat the RoPA as a structured document with two record sets — one for processing carried out as a controller, the other for processing carried out as a processor on behalf of others — sharing a discovery process and a maintenance lifecycle. The output is only as good as the discovery, so the methodology spends most of its energy there.

### Stage 1: Frame the dual role

Establish for which flows the organization is a controller, for which it is a joint controller, for which it is a processor, and for which it is a sub-processor. The same organization is almost always all of these for different flows. A SaaS vendor is typically a controller for its own employees, prospects, billing contacts, support contacts, and website visitors; a processor for the customer's end-users; and may be a controller for derived analytics where it determines the purposes and means. Joint controllership shows up in shared marketing programs, co-branded products, and consortium arrangements. Resolve the role question per flow, in writing, before populating any field. Where the answer is ambiguous, capture both candidate roles and the reasoning, and route to counsel before publishing.

The dual role drives field coverage. Article 30(1) controller records require the controller's identity and contact, the purposes of processing, the categories of data subjects and categories of personal data, the categories of recipients including recipients in third countries, transfers including the third country and a documented safeguard, the envisaged retention periods, and a general description of technical and organizational security measures. Article 30(2) processor records require the processor's identity and contact, each controller on whose behalf the processor acts, the categories of processing carried out for each controller, transfers, and security measures. Set up the document with two clearly separated record sets so that the produced artifact tracks the regulation's structure.

### Stage 2: Discovery

Discovery is interview-driven, complemented by system inventories and a careful read of marketing, HR, security, and product documentation. Start with a stakeholder map: marketing operations, sales operations, customer success, finance and billing, HR, talent acquisition, IT, security, legal, product, data engineering, and any function that has a tool with a contact list. Run a short, repeatable interview against each function. The interview asks: what categories of people's data do you touch; what tools and systems hold or move that data; why; for how long; with whom is it shared; where are those recipients physically; what protects it; and which other functions or vendors operate downstream. Capture answers in a working sheet keyed by candidate processing activity, not by tool. A common discovery mistake is to inventory tools rather than purposes; tools support multiple purposes, and purposes are the unit of legal analysis.

Augment interviews with the system-of-record inventory the security team maintains, the SaaS spend ledger from finance, the marketing-stack diagram, the data-flow diagrams the data engineering team uses, and the data-subject rights request logs (which expose flows that customer-facing teams encountered the hard way). Reconcile the interview output against these sources. Surface the deltas as discovery follow-ups; the gaps between what people described and what the system inventory shows are where the real risks live.

Triangulate against the privacy notice. The external privacy notice should be consistent with the RoPA. Where the notice claims a purpose that no interview surfaces, or where an interview surfaces a purpose the notice does not disclose, both documents need attention. Flag these as priority issues, not document inconsistencies; misstated privacy notices are direct regulator triggers under the lawfulness, fairness, and transparency principle.

### Stage 3: Activity definition

A processing activity is a grouping of operations performed on personal data for a coherent purpose or set of related purposes. Too coarse and the record loses its accountability value (a single "marketing" activity is not useful); too fine and the record becomes unmaintainable (every campaign as its own activity drowns the reviewer). A workable granularity is the "purpose family" — the smallest grouping such that the categories of subjects, categories of data, recipients, retention, and security measures are coherent within the group. Examples in a typical SaaS: prospect outreach and lead nurture; customer billing and revenue recognition; product analytics for customer end-users (as processor on behalf of the customer); workforce administration; recruitment; security telemetry and incident response; investor relations. The number of activities for a typical mid-sized SaaS lands between forty and one hundred twenty after discovery. Smaller is suspicious; larger is usually over-decomposition.

For each activity, draft a one-paragraph plain-language purpose statement. This statement is the anchor — every other field flows from it. If the statement contains "and" between two different purposes that would warrant different retention or different recipients, split the activity.

### Stage 4: Field population

For each activity in the controller register, populate the Article 30(1) field set.

Controller identity and contacts. Legal entity name, registered address, representative in the Union if applicable, and DPO contact if appointed. Where multiple group entities are joint or independent controllers for the same activity, capture each.

Purposes of processing. From the activity definition. Avoid hollow terms ("legitimate business purposes") that the regulator and data subjects will not accept; be concrete.

Categories of data subjects. Not individuals — categories. Common categories: prospects, customers' end-users, billing contacts, support contacts, website visitors, candidates, employees, contractors, suppliers, investor contacts. Children, vulnerable adults, and patients are categories worth calling out distinctly.

Categories of personal data. Identifiers, contact data, account credentials, financial data, communications content, browsing and device data, location data, biometric data, health data, special categories under Article 9, criminal data under Article 10. Where the activity involves automated decision-making with legal or similarly significant effects, note it here for cross-reference to Article 22 obligations.

Categories of recipients. Internal teams that receive the data, processors that act on the controller's behalf, joint controllers, separate controllers to whom the data is disclosed (for example a payment network), and statutory recipients (tax authorities, courts).

Transfers to third countries. Each transfer destination with the country and the safeguard relied upon (adequacy decision, standard contractual clauses, binding corporate rules, derogation under Article 49) and the link to the documentation that evidences it. The transfer field is the most common audit finding; SCCs that pre-date the most recent SCC overhaul, transfers under the now-defunct Privacy Shield never updated to the successor framework, and "we host in Europe but the support team is offshore" footnotes are the recurring failure modes.

Envisaged retention periods. Either a numeric duration or the criteria that determine duration. "As long as necessary" is not a valid entry on its own; the criteria must be specific enough that a reviewer can determine when data will be deleted.

General description of technical and organizational security measures. The regulation accepts a general description; the regulator increasingly expects specifics. Capture access controls, encryption at rest and in transit, logging and monitoring, secure development and change control, vendor management, incident response, and physical security at the level a peer security team would respect.

For each activity in the processor register, populate the Article 30(2) field set. The processor record's key feature is the per-controller breakdown — one record entry per controller on whose behalf the processor acts, listing categories of processing performed for that controller. In practice, processors often aggregate by customer-tier or product line rather than per individual customer; the regulator's posture on this varies, so default to per-customer for sensitive industries and to per-product-line for standard SaaS, and route the choice to counsel.

### Stage 5: Cross-cutting checks

Run the populated draft against five cross-cutting checks before publication.

Lawful basis coverage. Article 30 does not require the lawful basis field, but the record is incomplete in practice without it. For each controller activity, capture which Article 6 basis is relied upon and, for special-category data, which Article 9 condition. Where the basis is legitimate interests, link to the legitimate interests assessment; where the basis is consent, link to the consent capture and the withdrawal mechanism.

DPIA trigger sweep. For each activity, check whether the processing is likely to result in a high risk to rights and freedoms and therefore requires a DPIA. The supervisory authority's published list of operations requiring a DPIA is the operational source. Activities that hit triggers but do not yet have a DPIA become a follow-up backlog with named owners.

International transfer sweep. Independently of the per-activity transfer field, produce a transfer matrix listing every destination, every safeguard relied upon, the date the safeguard was last refreshed, and the date of the last transfer impact assessment. This matrix is the single most regulator-relevant byproduct of the RoPA in the current enforcement climate.

Article 28 contract coverage. For each processor in the record, confirm that an Article 28-compliant data processing agreement is in place, current, and includes the regulator-required clauses (subject matter and duration; nature and purpose; type of personal data and categories of subjects; obligations and rights of the controller; documented instructions; confidentiality; security; sub-processor management; assistance with data subject rights; assistance with security, breach notification, DPIA, and prior consultation; deletion or return at end; audit rights). Surface gaps as a contract remediation backlog.

Consistency with public statements. The privacy notice, the cookie notice, the marketing consent text, and the candidate privacy notice should be consistent with the activities listed. Flag inconsistencies.

### Stage 6: Maintenance lifecycle

Publish the record with a change-log header, a last-reviewed date, a next-review date, and a DPO sign-off block. Maintenance is event-driven plus periodic. Event triggers include new product launches, new marketing technology, new HR system, mergers and divestitures, vendor changes, new transfer destinations, breach incidents that revealed undocumented flows, and changes to lawful bases (especially withdrawals of consent that force a basis transition). Periodic review at a half-yearly or annual cadence catches everything the event triggers missed. Hand the record into the wider privacy program: it feeds DPIA prioritization, vendor due diligence, training content, data-subject rights handling, and breach scoping.

## Inputs

A plain-language description of the organization; an optional starting inventory in any form; an optional focus (new record, update, supervisory authority production preparation, or quality audit); and an optional regime overlay where the engagement covers UK GDPR or other regimes alongside the EU regulation.

## Outputs

A markdown RoPA document with separated controller and processor record sets, a transfer matrix, an Article 28 coverage check, an open-questions log, a change log, and a sign-off block. A parallel JSON object mirrors the per-record fields for ingestion into a GRC tool.

## Examples

A Series B SaaS that has grown to 320 employees and a substantial EU customer book runs this skill for the first time. Discovery surfaces forty-seven controller activities and an additional set of processor activities aligned to its product tiers. Two transfer destinations rely on SCC versions that predate the most recent module set; one is to a sub-processor the procurement team did not know existed because the analytics vendor changed its hosting model silently. The DPO uses the produced backlog to drive the next quarter's remediation plan.

A digital marketplace acquires a smaller competitor and inherits an undocumented RoPA. The skill runs in audit mode: it ingests the inherited record, marks every entry that fails one of the five cross-cutting checks, and produces a remediation backlog ordered by regulator-facing risk. The transfer matrix is produced first because the supervisory authority that initiated the contact specified it as a production requirement.

## Limitations

The skill produces an inventory, not a risk assessment. The skill cannot validate the substantive legal basis chosen for each activity; that is counsel's work. The skill cannot bind the organization to its own published record; only the controller can sign the record off. The skill does not maintain itself — the event triggers and periodic cadence depend on an operational program. Where the regulation has been amended or where supervisory authority guidance has shifted (the most common moving target is international transfer mechanics), the skill defers to current guidance and to the DPO.
