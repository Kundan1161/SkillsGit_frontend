# ELN 21 CFR Part 11 Readiness Assessor

> **Mandatory disclaimer.** This skill produces methodology guidance for
> assessing an electronic lab notebook against 21 CFR Part 11
> expectations. It is not legal or regulatory advice. Part 11 readiness
> for any regulated workflow must be reviewed and approved by qualified
> Quality and Regulatory staff. Use the output of this skill as a
> structured starting point for that review, not as a compliance
> determination. EU Annex 11 and other jurisdictional rules have
> overlapping but distinct expectations and must be assessed separately.

## When to use

Use this skill when a team is preparing to run, or is already running,
work on an electronic lab notebook that contributes to records the FDA
might inspect — GLP non-clinical safety studies, GMP development
records, IND-supporting laboratory data, device design history files.
Typical situations:

- A research deployment is about to take on its first regulated
  programme and leadership wants a gap list before the auditor does.
- Quality is preparing a periodic system review and needs a defensible
  structure for it.
- An ELN vendor change is being considered and the team needs an
  apples-to-apples readiness comparison.
- An audit observation has surfaced and the remediation plan needs to
  start from a structured map of expectations to current state.

This skill is the readiness companion to the *ELN System Architect*
skill in this niche. It assumes the architectural primitives exist; it
checks whether they are wired up, controlled, and evidenced to the
standard Part 11 expects.

## How to apply

Work through the seven assessment domains below in order. For each
domain, produce three things in the report:

1. **Expectation summary** — what Part 11 (and Annex 11 if in scope)
   asks for, in plain language.
2. **Current state** — what the deployment actually does today, drawn
   from the deployment description and any artefacts provided.
3. **Gap and risk** — concrete differences, ranked high / medium / low,
   each with a recommended remediation and a verification step.

Do not collapse domains into a single table; the auditor reads them
domain by domain and the remediation owners are different.

### Domain 1 — Unique user identity and authentication

Expectations to check:

- Every user account corresponds to a single, named, real person.
- Account credentials are not shared, ever, including for service
  windows or "the admin password".
- Identity is verified at account creation by a documented procedure
  (HR onboarding link, manager attestation, or institutional identity
  provider).
- Authentication uses individually controlled credentials. Where the
  signature meaning involves a higher assurance level, two
  identification components are used (commonly user id plus password,
  with additional controls).
- Inactive accounts are disabled on a defined schedule; departures
  trigger immediate revocation.

Common gaps to look for:

- A shared "lab" account used to run analysis scripts.
- Service accounts that can sign or that can edit experiment bodies.
- Identity provider not actually federated; local passwords still in
  use because "SSO is flaky".
- No documented procedure linking the corporate identity to the ELN
  account at creation time.

### Domain 2 — Electronic signature semantics

Expectations to check:

- Each signing event captures: the printed name of the signer, the date
  and time of execution, and the meaning of the signature (authored,
  reviewed, witnessed, approved).
- The signature is bound to the specific record state it attests to and
  cannot be excised, copied, or transferred to another record.
- The user re-enters at least one credential at the moment of signing
  (not a passive session token alone) for signatures whose meaning is
  legally binding.
- The system rejects an attempt by a user to apply a witness signature
  to their own authored record.
- The signer's name and meaning print on any rendered version of the
  record.

Common gaps to look for:

- Signature stamp added on save without a separate signing act.
- Witness applied via "anyone in the team can click sign".
- Signature meanings not differentiated (only one "sign" verb exists).
- Rendered exports omit the signature block entirely.

### Domain 3 — Audit-trail integrity

Expectations to check:

- Audit entries are created automatically by the system on every
  create, modify, and delete event affecting electronic records.
- Audit entries record at minimum: timestamp from a trusted clock, user
  identity, action verb, target entity and id, before and after values
  (or hash plus retrievable delta), and the reason where the workflow
  requires one.
- Audit entries are append-only at the storage layer, not merely at the
  application layer.
- Audit entries are retained for at least as long as the underlying
  record retention horizon.
- Audit trails are reviewable. Periodic audit-trail review by a person
  other than the record author is a procedural control.

Common gaps to look for:

- Audit table writable by the application service account.
- "Reason for change" not captured on amendments.
- No periodic audit-trail review procedure or evidence of one.
- Time source is the application host clock with no documented sync
  control.

### Domain 4 — Controlled access and segregation of duties

Expectations to check:

- Access to the system, to records, and to system-level configuration
  is role-based and least-privilege.
- Administrators of the ELN are distinct from the scientists whose work
  they could otherwise edit. Where the same person holds both roles
  (small organisations), compensating controls exist (peer review of
  administrative actions, audit-trail review by an independent party).
- The right to apply approver signatures is granted explicitly and
  recorded.
- Configuration changes (adding fields, changing workflows, modifying
  templates) follow a documented change procedure with approval.

Common gaps to look for:

- One super-admin account that everyone in IT shares.
- Configuration changes pushed directly to production without a record.
- No segregation between the person who can author a record and the
  person who can approve their own training certification.

### Domain 5 — System validation and qualification

Expectations to check:

- The system has a documented validation lifecycle proportionate to its
  risk. For a notebook in regulated use, typical artefacts include
  user requirements specification, functional and design specifications
  (or vendor-provided equivalents), installation qualification,
  operational qualification, and performance qualification.
- Each release that affects regulated functionality is reassessed; the
  reassessment is recorded.
- Validation evidence covers, at minimum: identity and access controls,
  signature semantics, audit-trail behaviour, backup and restore, and
  data integrity across upgrades.
- The validated state is maintained — patch deployments and
  configuration changes are within change control.

Common gaps to look for:

- The vendor's certificate of validation treated as sufficient on its
  own. The deployment must be validated, not just the product.
- No installation qualification record for the current deployed
  version.
- Backup and restore validated once at go-live and never tested again.

### Domain 6 — Training, procedures, and documentation

Expectations to check:

- Users who interact with regulated records are trained on the system
  and on the meaning of their electronic signatures before they are
  granted signing authority.
- Training records are themselves controlled documents with retention
  and traceability.
- Written procedures exist for: account provisioning and revocation,
  password and credential management, signature use and meaning,
  amendment and addendum handling, periodic audit-trail review,
  incident response, and backup-restore drills.
- A statement is on file with the appropriate regulator (for US Part
  11, a certification letter to the agency) that electronic signatures
  in this system are the legally binding equivalent of handwritten
  signatures.

Common gaps to look for:

- Procedures exist on paper but the in-system reality has drifted.
- New starters get a username before they finish the training module.
- No filed certification statement on the legally binding nature of
  electronic signatures.

### Domain 7 — Operational practice and ongoing controls

Expectations to check:

- Backup and restore are exercised on a defined cadence with
  documented results; restore drills include verifying that audit
  trails and signatures survive the round trip.
- Periodic access reviews are run; orphaned and over-privileged
  accounts are remediated and the review is recorded.
- Periodic audit-trail reviews are run on a sample of regulated records
  by someone other than the record author.
- Incident handling covers data-integrity incidents specifically (a
  modified record, a missed signature, a lost attachment) with a
  documented response and a route to corrective action.
- Records of system changes, deviations, and incidents are themselves
  retained for the record retention horizon.

Common gaps to look for:

- Backups never tested by restore.
- Access reviews promised quarterly, last run eighteen months ago.
- Incident process treats data-integrity events the same as outages,
  with no quality-system route.

## Inputs

- A description of the current deployment, including hosting model,
  user counts, and the regulated programmes it does or may support.
- Any existing artefacts the team can share: identity-provider
  configuration overview, signature workflow description, audit-trail
  sample, validation summary, training matrix, SOP list.
- The target regulatory frame and whether open-system controls (data in
  transit across non-controlled networks) are relevant.

## Outputs

A markdown gap report containing:

1. Executive summary with the count of high, medium, and low gaps and
   the headline readiness statement (conservative — "ready for review"
   rather than "compliant").
2. Seven domain sections, each with expectation, current state, gap,
   risk rating, recommended remediation, and verification step.
3. A consolidated remediation plan grouped by owner (system
   administrator, quality, IT identity, scientific user community).
4. An evidence checklist enumerating the artefacts a Quality reviewer
   should expect to see at the end of remediation.
5. An explicit out-of-scope section listing items deferred (open-system
   cryptographic controls, biometric signatures, third-party
   integrations) so they are not lost.

## Examples

**Prompt.** "Research-deployed ELN, sixty users, SAML to corporate IdP,
audit log present, witness signature via a 'review' button. Want to
support a GLP non-clinical safety study starting in five months."

**Expected output shape.** Identity domain low-risk (federated, named
accounts), with a medium gap on service-account audit. Signature domain
high-risk: only one signature verb exists, no signer-cannot-witness-self
control, signing does not require re-entry of credentials. Audit-trail
domain medium: append-only at application level only, no periodic
review procedure. Access and validation domains medium-to-high pending
documented qualification of the deployed version. Training and
operations domains high: no Part 11-specific user training, no filed
certification of signature meaning, no restore drill on record. The
remediation plan would block the GLP start date until at least the
signature and validation gaps are closed.

## Limitations

- The output is a structured assessment, not a determination of
  compliance. Only qualified Quality and Regulatory staff can declare
  a system fit for regulated use.
- Part 11 expectations and FDA enforcement discretion shift over time;
  the assessor must check the current text of the regulation and the
  latest FDA guidance.
- EU Annex 11, MHRA expectations, Japanese PMDA expectations, and ISO
  17025 overlap with Part 11 but are not equivalent. Multi-jurisdiction
  deployments need each frame assessed.
- The skill cannot validate evidence it has not seen. A "current state"
  claim in the input is taken at face value; the verification step is
  the team's responsibility.

## Sources reviewed

Methodology informed by a survey of public regulatory text and the
common compliance posture of widely deployed open-source ELN systems.
No prose was copied; the artefacts below were read to triangulate the
expectations and the typical gap pattern.

- US 21 CFR Part 11 (eCFR) — public regulation, used to anchor each
  domain's expectation summary.
- FDA "Part 11, Electronic Records; Electronic Signatures — Scope and
  Application" guidance — public, used for the risk-based framing.
- EU EudraLex Annex 11 — public, used for cross-frame contrasts.
- eLabFTW project documentation (AGPL-3) — informed the audit-trail and
  trusted-timestamping pattern.
- SciNote ELN repository (MPL-2.0) — informed the role and signature
  pattern in life-science workflows.
- Chemotion ELN repository (AGPL-3) — informed the analysis-link
  pattern relevant to record integrity.
- RSpace open-source platform repository (AGPL-3) — informed the
  inventory-coupled audit posture.
- Indigo ELN v2 repository (MIT) — informed the separation of signature
  service from the notebook core.
