# Robot VLM Perception Stack Architect

## When to use

Use this skill when a robotics team is deciding how to integrate visual-language models (open-vocabulary detectors, scene captioners, instruction-grounding models, generalist visual reasoners) into an existing or planned robot perception stack. The skill produces an architecture — a written design document — that defines which perception responsibilities go to deterministic classical computer vision, which go to compact specialist neural models, and which go to frontier multimodal models, along with the latency budgets, fallback logic, confidence-calibration strategy, and guardrails that hold the assembly together.

The skill is correct at three moments: greenfield design of a perception subsystem that needs open-vocabulary or instruction-following capability, an augmentation pass on a working classical stack that has hit the ceiling of its closed-vocabulary detector, and a post-incident review where the team has discovered that a VLM-augmented behaviour has hallucinated, drifted, or stalled in production. The skill is not the right tool for selecting between specific model checkpoints, for training or fine-tuning models, or for hands-on driver and ROS integration — separate skills cover those.

**Mandatory safety disclaimer.** This skill produces methodology guidance for VLM-augmented robotic perception. VLMs hallucinate, miss safety-critical edge cases, and degrade in low-light, adverse-weather, and out-of-distribution conditions. Every recommendation must be reviewed by qualified roboticists, validated in simulation, and never deployed as the sole perception layer in safety-critical contexts.

## How to apply

The skill walks a sixteen-stage pipeline. Stages 1 through 4 frame the problem; stages 5 through 11 design the layered stack; stages 12 through 14 design the guardrails, observability, and lifecycle; stages 15 and 16 produce the deliverable.

### Stage 1 — Restate the perception job in terms a VLM can or cannot help with

1. Take `platform_description` and `perception_outputs_required` and split the perception job into four columns: *deterministic geometry* (pose, range, free-space, calibration), *closed-vocabulary detection* (a known finite class set the platform has already trained), *open-vocabulary or rare-class recognition* (anything outside the closed set), and *language-grounded reasoning* (asking the scene a natural-language question or grounding a natural-language instruction to a region).
2. Mark which columns the platform actually needs. A warehouse picker may not need column four; a household assistant or an inspection drone almost certainly does. The columns drive whether a VLM is in scope at all. If only columns one and two are needed, this skill is the wrong skill — a classical perception stack suffices and you should redirect to the classical perception-stack architect.
3. Rewrite each required output as a *contract* with its downstream consumer: input modalities, latency, accuracy expectation, failure semantics. Pay special attention to failure semantics — a downstream consumer that assumes a detector either returns a confident result or returns nothing is incompatible with a generative VLM that returns confident-sounding text in all cases.
4. Distinguish *on-the-critical-path* outputs (the controller or safety stop blocks on them) from *advisory* outputs (a tele-op operator or a higher-level planner consumes them). VLMs are far easier to justify on advisory paths than on critical paths.

### Stage 2 — Classify the candidate model families

5. Build a working taxonomy of model families the stack might use: *closed-vocabulary specialist detectors* (compact CNN or transformer detectors trained on a fixed class list), *open-vocabulary detectors and segmenters* (text-conditioned models that take a prompt and localize matching regions), *generalist multimodal vision-language models* (large generative models that take image plus text and return text), and *vision-language-action policies* (end-to-end models that map image plus instruction to robot actions).
6. For each family, record nominal latency on the platform's compute envelope, typical memory footprint, the type of output (bounding box, mask, text, action), the kind of mistakes it makes (false-positive rate versus hallucination versus refusal), and the kind of supervision it can ingest at deployment time (calibrated thresholds, exemplars, in-context examples, fine-tuning data).
7. Reject families that cannot fit. A frontier multimodal model with billions of parameters does not run on an industrial PC at 30 Hz; placing it on-device on the wrong platform is the most common architectural error this skill exists to prevent.
8. Pin a *complementary-failure-modes* requirement: any two layers stacked together should fail in different ways so that one layer's strength covers another layer's weakness. Two open-vocabulary detectors trained on similar corpora are not complementary; an open-vocabulary detector plus a classical depth-and-geometry pipeline is.

### Stage 3 — Set the latency, confidence, and reliability budgets

9. From `latency_budget`, allocate a sub-budget to each layer. A typical layered stack on a 33 ms control loop spends roughly 8 ms on the deterministic geometry layer, 8 ms on the closed-vocab detector, 15 ms on the open-vocab detector or the local VLM, and budgets larger generative VLM calls outside the control loop (async, 200–2000 ms) with results staged into a slower-rate semantic memory.
10. Define *confidence axes*. Classical detectors emit calibrated softmax-like scores. Open-vocabulary detectors emit a similarity score against a text prompt; this score is not directly comparable across prompts. Generative VLMs do not natively emit a calibrated confidence; the design must explicitly add one (token log-probability, self-consistency over multiple samples, agreement with a second model, agreement with a geometric prior).
11. Define *reliability budgets* per output: an acceptable false-positive rate, an acceptable false-negative rate, and an acceptable hallucination rate (a confident answer that does not correspond to anything in the scene). Each budget will pin a guardrail in Stage 12.
12. Define *throughput shape*. Some outputs are needed at the control rate; others are needed only when the robot is paused or stationary. A common architectural pattern is to gate expensive VLM queries on motion state — a stationary robot can afford a 1-second VLM round-trip; the same robot at 1 m/s cannot.

### Stage 4 — Decide where each model lives

13. Lay out the *placement matrix*: rows are model families from Stage 2, columns are on-device versus edge-server versus cloud. Each cell records latency, cost per query, privacy implications, and offline behaviour.
14. Apply the *connectivity rule*: any model that lives on the cloud must have a defined behaviour when the link is degraded — wait, fall back, refuse, cache. The fallback latency-and-quality contract is part of the design, not an afterthought.
15. Apply the *privacy and data-egress rule*: if the platform sees PII (faces in a home, license plates in a warehouse, patient data in a clinical setting), the design must state whether VLM payloads can leave the device, and if so under what redaction or consent. Default to on-device for any sensitive scene.
16. Apply the *cost ceiling*. Frontier VLM API calls are charged per token of image and text. A robot that emits dozens of frames per second through a paid API will exceed reasonable cost budgets within hours. The design caps queries-per-mission and queries-per-minute and documents the rate-limiting strategy.

### Stage 5 — Layer 0: deterministic geometry and safety

17. State the deterministic-geometry layer first. This is the layer that produces freespace, range, pose, and any safety-rated stop signals. It must be classical, calibrated, and never depend on a VLM. The VLM-augmentation pattern leaves this layer untouched.
18. Document the *load-bearing contract* the deterministic layer offers to layers above: a metric obstacle list, a free-space polygon, a 6-DoF pose. Higher layers may *enrich* the obstacle list with semantic labels but may never *delete* an obstacle the deterministic layer detected. This invariant is the single biggest defence against VLM hallucinations causing motion errors.

### Stage 6 — Layer 1: closed-vocabulary specialist

19. The closed-vocabulary detector is the platform's bread-and-butter model — well-trained on the class set the robot encounters daily, calibrated, latency-bounded, and runs at the control rate.
20. For each class in the closed vocabulary, record a per-class confidence threshold tuned to the platform's reliability budget. Calibration must be empirical, not borrowed from a paper.
21. Define the *handoff signal* that promotes a frame from layer 1 to layer 2: low maximum confidence across all classes, multiple classes near the decision boundary, a region of the image with high feature saliency but no detection, a hand-tagged region of interest from another sensor.

### Stage 7 — Layer 2: open-vocabulary detector or segmenter

22. The open-vocabulary layer runs only on frames promoted from layer 1 (or on a slower clock). It takes a prompt vocabulary and produces region proposals matched to the prompts.
23. Define *prompt discipline*. The prompt vocabulary is itself an artifact under version control. Adding a new prompt is a design change, not a runtime decision. Free-form prompt construction at runtime is a major hallucination source and is forbidden by default.
24. Define the *score-calibration* procedure for the open-vocabulary similarity scores. Cross-prompt similarity scores are not natively comparable; the calibration normalizes against a per-prompt threshold computed on a held-out scene set.
25. Define the *non-maximum suppression and merging* policy between layer 1 and layer 2 detections. By default the deterministic layer wins on geometry; the open-vocabulary layer enriches with labels but does not modify boxes outside a tight overlap criterion.

### Stage 8 — Layer 3: scene-level VLM (advisory)

26. The scene-level VLM is the largest, slowest, most capable model in the stack. It answers free-form questions about the scene, captions, grounds instructions, and reasons over context. It does not run on the control loop and does not produce safety signals.
27. Define the *call surface* of the scene VLM. The platform exposes a small set of typed queries: "describe the scene at a high level", "is there an instance of [phrase] visible", "where is [phrase] relative to the robot". Free-form text generation without a typed schema is forbidden by default because the parser downstream is too brittle.
28. Define the *output schema* the VLM is required to produce. A robust pattern is structured JSON with an explicit `unknown` and `refuse` value and a per-field confidence flag. The parser refuses to accept any output that does not validate against the schema.
29. Define the *temperature and sampling policy*. For a perception query, temperature 0 with a single sample is the default; for an ambiguous instruction-grounding query, temperature 0.2 with two samples and a self-consistency check is the standard upgrade.
30. Define the *prompt template* as a versioned artifact. The system prompt names the robot, lists the typed queries, names the forbidden behaviours, and pins the output schema. The user-turn template carries the image and the typed query only. Prompt edits go through review.

### Stage 9 — Optional Layer 4: vision-language-action policy

31. Where a generalist policy is used for closed-loop manipulation — a learned mapping from image plus instruction to action tokens — treat it as a *policy*, not as a perception module. The policy still consumes Layer 0 deterministic outputs as a guard.
32. Define the *action-space whitelist*: the policy emits actions only within a small bounded space; any out-of-space token is dropped and triggers a safe-stop or hand-back to a planner. The whitelist is the primary safety contract for generalist policies.

### Stage 10 — Fusion topology across the layers

33. Specify how each layer's output is consumed. A common topology: deterministic layer fills an obstacle map; specialist detector fills a semantic-class field on detected obstacles; open-vocabulary detector fills additional rare classes on un-classified obstacles; scene VLM fills a slow-clock semantic memory that the planner consults on demand.
34. Specify *write priority*: lower layers always overwrite higher layers' geometry; higher layers append semantics but cannot delete or relocate detections.
35. Specify *staleness*: each layer's outputs carry a timestamp; downstream consumers refuse to act on stale semantic state. The scene VLM's outputs are commonly stale by hundreds of milliseconds and the planner must treat them as advisory.

### Stage 11 — Sensor and modality fusion

36. Identify which sensor modalities each layer consumes. Most VLMs accept a single image or a short sequence. Depth, LiDAR, thermal, and radar are typically fused outside the VLM via Layer 0 and Layer 1.
37. Define *visual conditioning* for the VLM. The image passed to the VLM is usually the front-RGB stream, sometimes annotated with overlays (a coloured outline of an obstacle the platform wants the VLM to reason about) or cropped to a region of interest. Document the annotation policy explicitly; arbitrary overlays change VLM behaviour in surprising ways.
38. Where the platform has multiple cameras, define a *view-selection* policy. Sending all views to the VLM multiplies cost; sending only the view that contains a candidate region of interest is the default.
39. Where the platform has time-of-flight or depth, fuse *outside* the VLM by labelling VLM-returned regions with their depth and rejecting regions whose depth is incompatible with the platform's geometry.

### Stage 12 — Hallucination, refusal, and ambiguity guardrails

40. List the high-cost failure modes specific to VLM-augmented perception: hallucinated objects (the model confidently names a class that is not visible), hallucinated attributes (correct object, wrong colour or count or state), hallucinated relations ("the cup is on the table" when it is on the floor), schema breaks (free-text output that does not parse), and missed safety-critical objects (a model that fluently describes a benign scene without noticing the hazard).
41. For each failure mode, specify a *detector*: cross-model agreement (two model families must agree), geometric-consistency check (the named region must have plausible depth and size), text-grounding check (the model's caption must be verifiable by re-querying with a typed yes/no question), schema-validator (output rejected if not parseable), salience-mass check (a region the deterministic layer marks as significant must appear in the VLM's caption or trigger a flag).
42. For each detector, specify a *response*: prefer a refusal over a guess (the platform tolerates an "unknown" response and falls back to conservative behaviour), prefer a slower-but-checked path over a fast-but-unchecked one, escalate to a human operator for safety-critical mismatches.
43. Define *refusal patterns* the platform exposes. A VLM that does not know what an object is must be allowed and encouraged to return "unknown" in the schema. The downstream planner has a defined policy for "unknown" that is more conservative than for any concrete answer.
44. Define the *forbidden-class list*. Some classes (a child, a knife in a domestic setting, a weapon, an emergency-responder uniform) require human-in-the-loop confirmation before the platform acts on them, even if the VLM is highly confident.

### Stage 13 — Observability and telemetry

45. Specify the per-layer telemetry the platform records: per-frame latency, per-frame confidence, per-frame prompt and response (with PII redaction), per-frame layer-promotion decision, per-frame guardrail trip, per-frame VLM cost.
46. Specify the *replay store*: the recorded inputs and outputs sufficient to reproduce a failed perception event offline. Generative VLMs are non-deterministic; the design must record the prompt, the image hash, the model version, the sampling parameters, and the raw response token-by-token.
47. Specify *drift monitors*. Open-vocabulary similarity scores and VLM token log-probabilities drift across model versions and across changes in lighting and seasonal scene composition. The design includes a regression detector that flags a statistically significant change in the score distribution against a baseline.
48. Specify *cost monitors*. Cloud VLM cost per mission is metered and alerted; a runaway prompt loop is one of the most common production failures and is caught only by aggressive rate-limiting and cost alarms.

### Stage 14 — Lifecycle, evaluation, and model upgrade discipline

49. Define the *evaluation harness*. Before any layer's model is upgraded — even a patch version — the platform replays a curated hard-case set (Stage 13 replay store plus synthetic perturbations) and the new model must meet or exceed the prior model on the reliability budget. A worse score on the hard-case set blocks deployment.
50. Define *shadow-mode rollout*. New VLMs run in shadow on production traffic for a defined window, with outputs logged but never acted on, before being promoted. The shadow period catches drift the offline evaluation does not.
51. Define the *prompt-and-vocabulary version contract*. Prompt templates and open-vocabulary prompt lists are versioned, code-reviewed, and tied to model versions in the manifest. Changing a prompt is a deployment.
52. Define the *deprecation path* for a layer's model: when a closed-vocabulary specialist is retired in favour of an open-vocabulary one, the platform runs both in parallel during a transition and verifies parity on a per-class basis before retiring the specialist.

### Stage 15 — Risk register and open questions

53. Enumerate residual risks: model-vendor lock-in for a cloud VLM, a hidden dependency on a single training-data distribution, a guardrail whose threshold is borrowed and not validated, an output schema whose typed fields the downstream consumer does not actually honour, an evaluation set that has decayed against the field's distribution.
54. Maintain a known-unknowns register: failure modes the team has not yet characterized but suspects. This list is more valuable than the closed risks because the unknown unknowns are where field failures originate.

### Stage 16 — Compose the deliverable

55. Open with a one-paragraph "design intent": what semantic capabilities the platform gains, what the VLM layer is for, and what it is explicitly not for.
56. Render the layered design as a diagram-described-in-prose: Layer 0 contract, Layer 1 contract, Layer 2 contract, Layer 3 contract, optional Layer 4 policy. Include the placement matrix, the per-layer latency budget, and the per-layer reliability budget.
57. Render the guardrail table: failure mode, detector, response.
58. Emit `design_json` with the structured fields. A downstream tool should be able to derive a launch-time configuration for the perception node and a CI test plan from the JSON.
59. Close with the mandatory safety disclaimer restated, an explicit "open risks" list, and a "what this design does not cover" section that points at the open-vocabulary-detection-deployer skill, the instruction-grounding skill, and the multimodal evaluation skill for adjacent decisions.

## Outputs

The skill returns:

1. `stack_design` (markdown) — full layered architecture document with rationales, contracts, and guardrails.
2. `design_json` (JSON) — structured plan suitable for downstream automation.

## Examples

**Input (placeholder):**

`platform_description`: "Indoor inspection robot, 0.5 m/s top speed, operating in commercial buildings after-hours. Existing perception: a calibrated 3D LiDAR plus depth-camera stack with a closed-vocabulary detector for doors, signage, fire-extinguishers, and people."

`perception_outputs_required`: "All existing outputs, plus the ability to describe a scene to a remote operator, ground operator instructions like 'check the third panel on the left' to a region, and flag long-tail anomalies the closed detector misses."

`latency_budget`: "Existing outputs at 20 Hz; semantic outputs may be slower, up to 1.5 s for a scene description, up to 400 ms for a grounded instruction."

`connectivity_profile`: "Reliable Wi-Fi inside buildings; cellular fallback. Operator on a remote console."

`compute_envelope`: "On-device industrial PC with a 30W GPU; cloud is available."

`safety_class`: "supervised-autonomy."

**Plan (abbreviated):**

- Layer 0: existing LiDAR-depth geometry stack untouched, remains the only source of obstacle truth.
- Layer 1: existing closed-vocab detector retained at 20 Hz, calibrated thresholds untouched.
- Layer 2: on-device open-vocabulary detector, run on promoted frames (where Layer 1 confidence is low) at up to 5 Hz, against a versioned prompt vocabulary covering 80 long-tail building objects.
- Layer 3: cloud scene VLM, called on a typed query schema, gated on robot stationary or low-speed, rate-limited to 30 calls per mission, with structured JSON output and an `unknown` value.
- Guardrails: deterministic-layer geometry is load-bearing; VLM-supplied labels enrich but never delete or move boxes. Two-model agreement required for any class flagged "safety-relevant". Forbidden-class list (medical devices, emergency-stop hardware) requires operator confirmation.
- Observability: per-frame replay store, prompt and image hash logged, cost meter, score-distribution drift monitor.
- Offline fallback: when connectivity is degraded, Layer 3 is unavailable; the operator console displays the platform's capability degradation banner and disables instruction-grounding affordances.

**Output excerpt:** the markdown design plus a JSON object whose `layers` array enumerates each layer's contract, whose `guardrails` array enumerates failure-mode, detector, response triples, and whose `observability_signals` enumerate the per-frame telemetry fields.

## Limitations

- The skill produces an architecture, not an implementation or a model selection. Choosing between specific open-vocabulary or generative model checkpoints is a separate exercise.
- The skill is conservative by design and will recommend more guardrails than research platforms strictly need. Researchers may relax recommendations explicitly; production teams should not.
- The skill does not address fine-tuning, RLHF, or any training-time intervention; it treats models as black-boxes that the platform integrates.
- Costs and latencies of frontier models change quickly; the skill flags the budget-and-monitor pattern but cannot pin numeric ceilings that age well.
- The skill assumes the team has access to representative data from the operating environment. Without representative data, calibration of thresholds is impossible and the design cannot meet its own reliability budget.
- The skill does not encode jurisdiction-specific regulatory constraints on autonomous systems or on data egress; the team must layer those on top.
