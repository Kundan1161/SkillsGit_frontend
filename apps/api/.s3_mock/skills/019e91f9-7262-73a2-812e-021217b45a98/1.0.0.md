## When to use

Use this skill when a remote or hybrid team is designing the onboarding portion of its handbook — the pages a new joiner reads on day one, navigates through their first month, and refers back to for the first year. The trigger is typically that onboarding is currently ad-hoc (managers piece it together from their own notes, Slack messages, and improvised calls), the team has noticed that experience varies dramatically between hires, and someone has been asked to fix it. The skill is also appropriate when a team is migrating from in-office onboarding (which leaned on osmosis, hallway introductions, and over-the-shoulder demonstrations) to distributed onboarding, where the same content has to be carried by deliberate handbook structure and async pathways.

The skill produces the handbook section, not the program. The handbook section is the durable artifact — the pages, the cross-links, the milestone definitions, the templates. The program (the manager's actual welcome email, the buddy's actual first conversation, the team's actual social rituals) is run by people using the handbook section as scaffolding. The skill is deliberate about this distinction: a team that drafts good handbook pages and abandons the program executes worse than a team that drafts mediocre handbook pages and runs the program rigorously. The handbook is necessary but not sufficient.

The skill assumes the rest of the handbook exists or is being built; the onboarding section depends heavily on cross-links into broader handbook content (company values, how-we-work pages, function-specific references). If no handbook exists, the skill produces a usable onboarding section but flags it as a stub-rich draft whose links resolve as the rest of the handbook is built. The skill is not the right tool for designing role-specific 30/60/90 plans for individual hires (that is a different skill applied per hire), for replacing learning-and-development curricula (different scope, different specialization), or for compliance training (regulated industries have specific format and tracking requirements outside this skill's scope).

## How to apply

The skill produces the onboarding section through a structured nine-step process. The order is deliberate: the milestone definitions come before the day-by-day plan, because the day-by-day plan is in service of the milestones. The buddy and mentor structures come after the milestones because they exist to support specific milestones. The feedback loops come last because they measure how the rest is performing.

1. **State the onboarding contract.** Onboarding is a two-sided contract. The company commits to giving the new joiner what they need to succeed in a defined period; the new joiner commits to engaging with the material, asking questions, and reaching the milestones. The contract should be stated openly at the top of the onboarding section: this is what we will provide, this is what we expect, this is how long it should take. Vague or implicit contracts produce both the over-stretched new joiner (who feels they should already be productive) and the under-stretched new joiner (who feels nothing is required of them). The contract sets the standard and the timeline.

2. **Define three to five milestones spanning the first ninety days.** Milestones are observable, role-family-agnostic outcomes the new joiner should reach. Useful generic milestones for any role: end of week one — "you can describe what the company does, who your team is, and how you will get help when stuck"; end of month one — "you have completed your first meaningful piece of independent work and received feedback on it"; end of month three — "you are operating without daily handholding; you contribute to team decisions and have begun to form opinions about how things should improve." Role-specific milestones layer on top of these. The milestone set is what the manager and new joiner orient against during one-on-ones; without explicit milestones, those one-on-ones default to status updates.

3. **Sketch the day-by-day structure of the first week.** Day one is logistics, identity (accounts, equipment), an introduction to the team, and the handbook reading list for the rest of the week. Days two through five are progressively more substantive: company-wide reading day two, function-specific reading day three, the first observation-only meeting day four, the first attempted contribution day five. The week-one structure is the most prescribed part of the section because a poorly-handled first week predicts the rest of the experience. After week one, the prescription loosens — the new joiner has enough context to navigate their own time with manager guidance.

4. **Build the first-month and first-quarter tracks as branching paths, not linear schedules.** After week one, the path depends on role family. Engineering hires spend more time on technical context and codebase orientation; sales hires spend more time on product knowledge, deal stories, and shadowing calls; design hires spend more time on customer research artifacts and design-system fluency. The handbook section names the branch point ("end of week one"), names the role family branches, and links each branch to its role-specific track. The track lives as a separate handbook page per family, owned by that function's lead, so changes in one family do not require a single team to maintain all of them.

5. **Design the buddy and mentor structures as two distinct roles with different jobs.** A buddy is the low-stakes daily contact who answers operational questions ("how do I file an expense?" "where is the meeting recording link?") and ensures the new joiner is not isolated. The buddy commitment is small — twenty minutes a day for the first two weeks — and rotates so the burden does not concentrate on a few people. A mentor is the higher-stakes role-model and feedback partner, typically in the same function but not the new joiner's manager, with a longer commitment (weekly thirty-minute check-ins for the first ninety days). The handbook section defines both roles, the criteria for matching, and the suggested questions and topics for each.

6. **Specify the milestone-check meetings explicitly.** Each milestone has a corresponding meeting: the end-of-week-one check between new joiner and manager; the end-of-month-one check; the end-of-month-three check. The handbook section provides each meeting's agenda template, the questions the manager asks, the questions the new joiner is encouraged to ask, and the output of the meeting (a brief written note in the new joiner's onboarding tracker). The check meetings are where milestone reality versus aspiration is reconciled; without them, missed milestones go unnoticed.

7. **Build the feedback loop in both directions.** The new joiner is the freshest set of eyes on the handbook and the company. They notice friction — confusing pages, missing context, jargon that nobody explained. The handbook section includes an explicit ask: at end of week one and end of month one, the new joiner submits feedback on what was confusing or missing. The receiving owner (the head of people operations or a designated handbook steward) treats this feedback as input to handbook improvement and closes the loop by visibly making changes. New joiners who see their feedback acted on contribute more in their first quarter; new joiners who do not, learn that feedback is performative and stop providing it.

8. **Make the social and cultural onboarding deliberate, not assumed.** Remote teams cannot rely on the implicit social onboarding that happens in an office. The handbook section names the deliberate social steps: introductions in the relevant chat channels with a small structured prompt (your role, what you'll be working on, one fact about you), a coffee-pairing program where the new joiner is auto-paired with three to five colleagues across teams in the first month, attendance at recurring company-wide rituals (all-hands, demo sessions), and an explicit invitation to attend the social-spaces that exist (interest channels, virtual co-working sessions). Without this scaffolding, the remote new joiner can complete the technical and role-specific tracks while remaining isolated.

9. **Define the off-boarding of onboarding.** Onboarding ends. The handbook section names when and how. The end-of-month-three milestone check is the formal exit — at that meeting, the new joiner is reclassified as a team member and the onboarding-specific structures (buddy commitment, weekly mentor check-ins, the feedback collection) wind down. Onboarding without a defined end produces either lingering uncertainty about when full-status begins or an awkward fade-out where the structures dissolve unannounced. The handbook section is explicit: at this milestone, this is what changes.

### Section layout the skill produces

The onboarding section of the handbook follows this structure:

1. `# Joining the Team`
2. `## The onboarding contract` — what we provide, what we expect, the timeline
3. `## Milestones` — the three-to-five outcomes the first ninety days aim at
4. `## Before your first day` — what the new joiner does pre-start; what the manager does pre-start
5. `## Week one — day by day`
6. `## After week one — branching by role` — links to role-family tracks
7. `## Buddy program` — how buddies are matched, the buddy's job, the buddy's time commitment
8. `## Mentor pairing` — how mentors are matched, the mentor's job, the cadence
9. `## Milestone checks` — agenda templates for each check meeting
10. `## Feedback loops` — how the new joiner gives feedback, how it gets acted on
11. `## Social and cultural onboarding` — the deliberate social steps
12. `## End of onboarding` — when and how onboarding formally ends
13. `## For managers` — the manager-facing checklist (linked separately, also produced as its own output)

### Composition principles

- **Write for the reader at the moment they will read the page.** A day-one page is read by a nervous new joiner who has had three other people send them links that morning. Keep it short, calm, and concrete. Avoid initialisms in the first paragraph. Name the next action clearly.
- **Every milestone has a name in the document, and that name appears everywhere.** "End-of-month-one milestone" is the recurring phrase; both the new joiner and the manager use the same name. Inconsistent naming ("the 30-day check-in" in one place, "the first month review" in another) makes the structure feel less load-bearing than it is.
- **Cross-link to the rest of the handbook generously.** Onboarding is the moment a new joiner is most likely to read handbook pages they will not read again for a year. Make the cross-links from this section to the broader handbook prominent — values pages, how-we-work pages, function leads' editorial pages. This is also the test of whether the rest of the handbook is in shape.
- **Avoid promises the manager cannot keep.** Pages that say "your manager will pair with you on your first task" are read as commitments. If managers cannot reliably pair, do not write the promise. Underpromising and overdelivering builds trust; the reverse erodes it within a quarter.
- **Make every page editable in the same workflow as the rest of the handbook.** The onboarding section is not a special-case piece of content; it is a regular handbook section subject to the same ownership, freshness, and contribution rules. New joiners are encouraged to suggest edits to the pages they just used.

### Anti-patterns the skill calls out

- **The kitchen-sink trap.** The onboarding section grows to include every piece of context anyone thought a new joiner should have. Within a year it is a 50-page document the new joiner cannot finish. Fix: the day-by-day structure is a sequencing tool; out-of-sequence context lives in the broader handbook and is linked, not duplicated.
- **The set-and-forget trap.** The onboarding section is built and then never updated. Within a year it references tools nobody uses, processes that changed, and a team structure that no longer exists. Fix: an explicit quarterly review owned by the head of people operations.
- **The manager-as-script-reader trap.** The manager checklist becomes so prescriptive that managers feel they are reading a script. They lose their own voice in onboarding, and the new joiner senses the artificiality. Fix: the manager checklist names what must be covered and by when, but leaves voice and method to the manager.
- **The buddy-as-burden trap.** Buddies are assigned without protected time and without exit; the buddy commitment becomes a tax that erodes goodwill. Fix: the buddy commitment is small, time-boxed, and rotates so no one person is asked twice in a quarter.
- **The feedback-without-action trap.** New joiners submit feedback; nothing changes; subsequent new joiners learn the feedback ask is performative. Fix: the feedback owner publishes a short monthly note summarizing the most recent feedback and the changes made in response.
- **The overwhelming-first-day trap.** Day one is loaded with six meetings, four pieces of mandatory training, and an introduction to the codebase or product. The new joiner ends day one exhausted and uncertain whether they have already failed. Fix: day one has logistics, the welcome introduction, the buddy kickoff, the manager one-on-one, and reading time. Substantive context starts day two.
- **The introvert-tax trap.** Onboarding social structures assume comfort with high-contact peer engagement — public introductions, group video, paired coffees. New joiners who find these draining experience onboarding as social labor before they have built any internal goodwill to spend on it. Fix: social structures are invited rather than mandatory, with multiple modes (written introduction posts as an alternative to live introductions; paired coffees scheduled at the new joiner's pace).
- **The time-zone-tax trap.** A globally distributed team builds onboarding around the schedule of the headquarters time zone. New joiners outside that zone do onboarding at midnight or pre-dawn for the first week. Fix: every recurring onboarding element (buddy kickoff, manager check-in, milestone meetings) is offered in at least two time-zone windows, and the new joiner picks. Recorded sessions are an acceptable alternative for company-wide content.

### What changes for cohort hiring

When new joiners arrive in cohorts of five or more, the design changes meaningfully. The handbook section should split into a cohort-shared path and individual paths:

- The cohort shares first-day logistics, the company-wide reading, and at least one substantive group session (typically a leadership Q&A) in their first week.
- The cohort runs a shared retrospective at the end of week one and again at end of month one. Cohort retrospectives produce higher-quality feedback than individual feedback because they aggregate experiences and reveal which issues are systemic.
- Each cohort member still has an individual buddy and individual mentor. The cohort is a social structure, not a replacement for individualized support.
- Cohorts produce a peer network that often outlasts onboarding; the section should name this explicitly and invite it ("you will likely build closer connections with your cohort than with most colleagues in your first year, because you went through the same experience together").

For individual hiring at low velocity (a hire every couple of weeks), the cohort structure is not available and its social benefits need substitution: a stronger emphasis on cross-team paired coffees, an explicit invitation to attend interest-group channels, and a more deliberate buddy match.

### The first-year-anniversary check (a bonus practice)

Beyond the formal end-of-month-three exit from onboarding, a useful practice is a year-one anniversary conversation. This is not part of onboarding proper but is enabled by it: the new joiner, now a tenured team member, reflects on what worked, what did not, and what should change in the onboarding section before the next hires arrive. The handbook section can mention this practice without prescribing it; teams that adopt it find it produces particularly candid feedback because the year-one perspective is far enough removed to be honest but close enough to remember details.

## Inputs

- **Company context (required, text).** Headcount, remote/hybrid posture, time-zone spread, hiring velocity. Calibrates the depth and the cohort design.
- **Existing onboarding practice (optional, text).** Preserves what works and avoids redesigning around imagined problems.
- **Role families (optional, text).** Drives the branching design and the role-specific track stubs.
- **Handbook context (optional, choice).** Determines how heavily the section depends on cross-links versus internal expansion.
- **Cohort or individual (optional, choice).** Cohort hiring enables shared first-week sessions and peer connections that solo hiring cannot replicate; the section design changes meaningfully on this axis.

## Outputs

A complete onboarding section laid out as above; a standalone new-hire quickstart linkable from the offer letter; a manager-facing checklist that pairs with the new-hire-facing pages.

## Examples

### Worked example: a 90-person, fully remote, six-time-zone software company hiring 15 per quarter

**Input company context:** "90 people, fully remote across six time zones (US East, US West, UK, Central Europe, India, Sydney). Hiring about 15 per quarter, mostly engineering and revenue. No cohorts — hires arrive whenever they arrive."

**Input existing practice:** "Manager sends a welcome email with links. There's a Notion page called 'New Hire Onboarding' from 2024 that's mostly accurate. The first day is a series of one-on-ones — usually 5 or 6 of them. Most people say their first week was overwhelming."

**Input role families:** "Engineering, product, design, revenue (mix of AE and CS), people operations."

**Input handbook context:** "immature-handbook" — pages exist but ownership is spotty.

**Input cohort or individual:** "individuals"

**Expected output (excerpt from the new-hire quickstart):**

> # Welcome — your first day
>
> If you're reading this, you've just joined and we're glad you're here. This page is the only thing you need to start with. Everything else is linked from it.
>
> ## What to expect this week
>
> By the end of this week, the goal is for you to:
>
> - Have working accounts and equipment.
> - Know what the company does, who your team is, and who to ask when you're stuck.
> - Have completed three readings: our company values, how we work, and the first reading from your role-specific track.
> - Have a buddy you can message about anything operational.
> - Have a sense of the rhythm of your team's week.
>
> You will not be expected to do meaningful work yet. If your manager or anyone else suggests otherwise, that is a misunderstanding — please tell us through the end-of-week feedback form so we can fix it.
>
> ## Your first morning
>
> 1. Confirm your accounts are working (email, chat, the tools listed in the role-specific equipment page linked below). If anything is missing, message the IT operations channel; replies typically come within an hour during overlap windows.
>
> 2. Read this page in full. Reading time: 8–10 minutes.
>
> 3. Post a short introduction in your team's main channel. There's a template at the bottom of this page if you want a starting point.
>
> 4. Meet your buddy — they will reach out today with a 20-minute kickoff call within your overlap hours. They are the right person for operational questions for the rest of the week.
>
> 5. Meet your manager for a 30-minute one-on-one to set expectations for the week and answer initial questions.
>
> ## Your week
>
> A calendar invite for the rest of the week's readings, meetings, and check-ins has been set up by your manager. You should see it. If you don't, message your manager.
>
> The rest of the week is mostly reading and observation. You'll attend a few meetings to listen, you'll meet a few colleagues for paired coffees, and you'll start working through your role-specific track. The schedule is shaped to leave you breathing room — most days have at least three hours unscheduled. Use it to read at your own pace, take notes on what's confusing, and rest.
>
> ## Milestone for end of week one
>
> By Friday, in a 30-minute check-in with your manager, you should be able to:
>
> - Describe what the company does in two or three sentences in your own words.
> - Name your team members and what each of them works on.
> - Identify three or four specific things from your first week that confused you (these become the input to the week-one feedback form — they help us improve onboarding for the next person).
>
> If any of these feel out of reach by Friday, that is a signal to flag; it usually means something earlier in the week didn't land, and we want to fix it before it compounds.

### Worked example excerpt: the manager checklist for the same company

> # Manager onboarding checklist — for the hiring manager
>
> This checklist runs from offer-acceptance through end of month three. It pairs with the new-hire-facing pages; your role is to ensure each item happens, not to write each item from scratch.
>
> ## Pre-start (offer acceptance through start date)
>
> - [ ] Assign a buddy. Choose someone in the new joiner's overlapping time zones, not from your immediate reports, who has been at the company at least six months. Confirm with the buddy that they have capacity this week.
> - [ ] Identify a mentor candidate. The mentor is typically in the same function but not a direct peer or report. They commit to weekly thirty-minute check-ins for the first ninety days. Confirm before the new joiner's start date.
> - [ ] Send the welcome email three working days before start. Include the link to the new-hire quickstart page and the calendar invite for day-one.
> - [ ] Schedule the week-one calendar in advance: the buddy kickoff (day one, within overlap hours), your one-on-one (day one), the daily fifteen-minute manager check-in (days two through five), the Friday end-of-week-one milestone meeting, and the readings.
> - [ ] Coordinate with IT on equipment shipment timing. Confirm arrival before start date.
>
> ## Week one
>
> - [ ] Day one one-on-one: 30 minutes. Welcome, share what the week looks like, answer initial questions. Resist the urge to load context — there will be time.
> - [ ] Days two through five: brief daily check-in (15 minutes maximum). Listen for confusion and unstuck them.
> - [ ] End of week one milestone meeting: 30 minutes. Use the agenda template in the handbook section. Capture the new joiner's three or four points of confusion as feedback to the handbook owner.
>
> ## Month one
>
> - [ ] Weeks two and three: the new joiner starts the role-specific track. Pair them with someone in your team on a small, contained piece of work that is meaningful but low-stakes.
> - [ ] Confirm the mentor pairing is active and the weekly check-ins are happening. If the pairing is not working, swap rather than persist.
> - [ ] End of month one milestone meeting: 45 minutes. Review the milestone outcomes. The new joiner has completed their first meaningful piece of work; discuss feedback on it, ask what is confusing them now, and adjust the rest of the ramp accordingly.
>
> ## Months two and three
>
> - [ ] The new joiner shifts from buddy-supported daily questions to self-direction. The buddy commitment formally ends at end of week two; the mentor relationship continues through end of month three.
> - [ ] Monthly check-ins with the new joiner are full one-on-ones, not onboarding-specific. Onboarding is winding down; team-membership is winding up.
> - [ ] End of month three milestone meeting: 60 minutes. Use the agenda template. This is the formal exit from onboarding. Confirm with the new joiner that they consider themselves a team member. Conclude the buddy and mentor structures, with thanks.

## Role-family track stubs the section should produce

The branching-by-role structure references role-family tracks that live as their own handbook pages. The onboarding section should produce stubs for the most common role families so the function leads can populate them, rather than waiting for each function to invent its own track from scratch. The stubs share a common spine:

- **Week-by-week reading.** What technical or domain context the role-specific reader picks up across weeks two through six. Sized to roughly two hours per day of dedicated reading time.
- **First contained piece of work.** The role-specific bounded task that produces the end-of-month-one milestone. Engineering: a small bug fix or a small feature. Design: a small contribution to an in-flight feature. Sales: shadowing two discovery calls and producing notes. Customer success: handling two routine inbound tickets under supervision. Each role family's first piece of work is calibrated to produce a real artifact in a low-stakes context.
- **Shadowing or observation paths.** Who the new joiner sits in on (figuratively, for remote teams) during the first month. Each role family has different observation candidates: engineering joiners observe code review and incident response; sales joiners observe deal calls; customer success joiners observe escalation calls; design joiners observe customer research.
- **Function-specific tooling primer.** The two-or-three tools specific to the role's daily work, with a short orientation per tool. Tooling primers should link to canonical tool docs rather than duplicate them.
- **Function-specific people to meet.** Beyond the team, who in the broader function should the new joiner have a thirty-minute coffee with during their first month? Naming three to five people per role provides the cross-team exposure that office-by-osmosis used to produce.

The stubs are owned by the function lead; the onboarding-section steward maintains the common spine but does not write the function-specific content. This separation prevents the onboarding steward from becoming a bottleneck and keeps the role-family content authentic to each function's culture.

## How the section evolves over the first year of use

A newly-built onboarding section is a hypothesis until it has been used. The first cohort of new joiners who run through it produce feedback that drives revisions. The skill encourages explicit iteration cycles:

- **Cycle one (months one to three of use):** the first three to five new joiners run through. Their week-one feedback identifies the unclear pages and the missing pieces. The steward iterates the day-by-day structure based on observed pain.
- **Cycle two (months four to six):** by now, ten-plus new joiners have used the section. Patterns emerge — particular pages that always confuse, particular handoffs that always slip. Larger structural revisions happen at this cycle.
- **Cycle three (months seven to twelve):** the section reaches steady-state shape. Subsequent changes are smaller — tool updates, process changes, content refresh — rather than structural redesign.

Teams that skip the iteration cycles and treat the first version as final will find the section under-performing in year two. Teams that run the cycles produce sections that are visibly better with each iteration and that the new joiners themselves recognize as well-tended.

## Onboarding for non-traditional hires

The section's design assumes a new employee joining a team. Adjacent paths apply with variations:

- **Contractors and freelancers** need a thinner onboarding (a contractor-specific quickstart, access to a narrower slice of the handbook, no buddy assignment but a clear point-of-contact). The handbook section should produce a contractor-onboarding variant rather than expecting contractors to filter the employee-onboarding content.
- **Internal transfers** moving from one team to another already know the company; what they need is team-specific onboarding without the company-context layer. Most internal transfers do not need a buddy but do benefit from a mentor on the new team. The section should produce an internal-transfer variant.
- **Returners** (employees who left and came back) typically need lighter onboarding focused on what has changed since they left. A "what's changed since you were last here" page, maintained quarterly, is the highest-leverage piece of content for returners.
- **Re-orgs in flight** (employees whose team is being restructured around them) need a hybrid: they are not new but their context is new. A short "team-change onboarding" page with the lightweight versions of the milestones works.

These variants should be linked from the main onboarding section's top page, so a manager can find the right path quickly when the hire is non-standard.

## The relationship between the handbook section and the manager's role

The handbook section does not replace the manager; it equips the manager. Three guiding principles for the manager-handbook interaction:

- **The handbook carries the content; the manager carries the relationship.** The handbook says what to read and when; the manager makes the new joiner feel welcome, checks in on how they are doing, adjusts the pace when needed. A manager who treats the handbook as a substitute for personal engagement produces under-supported new joiners; a manager who improvises everything from scratch wastes their own time on what the handbook could have provided.
- **The manager is empowered to deviate.** The handbook section is a default, not a prescription. A new joiner with prior experience in the company's domain may move faster; a new joiner stepping into a brand-new role family may need additional time on context. The manager adjusts; the handbook section explicitly invites this in the manager-facing section.
- **The manager is the feedback loop's primary participant.** New joiner feedback at the milestone meetings goes through the manager to the handbook owner. The manager's filter matters: they distinguish "this confused me, but I got it" from "this seriously needs fixing," and they advocate for systemic changes when patterns recur.

## What the section does not cover

The onboarding handbook section is the durable scaffolding for routine new-hire onboarding. It deliberately does not cover:

- **Executive onboarding.** New hires at director-and-above levels need bespoke onboarding heavy on stakeholder mapping, business-context immersion, and explicit early-decisions support. The general onboarding section's milestones apply but most of the role-specific track is replaced by an executive-onboarding variant designed per-hire.
- **Acquisition onboarding.** Employees joining through acquisition need a hybrid onboarding that respects their existing tenure with the acquired company while integrating them into the new structure. A separate handbook page for acquisition onboarding is appropriate; the general section's structure does not fit.
- **Return-from-leave reintegration.** Employees returning from extended leave (parental, medical, sabbatical) benefit from a structured reintegration that the general onboarding section is too heavy for. A dedicated return-from-leave page typically lives near the leave policies rather than within onboarding.

The handbook section should mention these explicitly so readers do not misapply the general section to non-standard cases.

## Limitations

- The skill produces the handbook section, not the program. A team that drafts the section and does not assign buddies, run the milestone checks, or close the feedback loop will not get the benefit of the design. The skill flags the program-execution requirement but cannot ensure it.
- The skill assumes a baseline of stable team structure. Teams undergoing constant reorganization cannot keep onboarding pages current because the answers genuinely change month to month. In that environment, the skill's recommendations should be lighter (a thin section that points to a manager-maintained per-new-hire artifact) until the structure stabilizes.
- The skill is calibrated for English-language onboarding. Multi-language onboarding introduces translation lag and the risk that non-English versions become stale faster than English; out of this skill's scope.
- The skill does not produce specific cultural-orientation content. The values pages, the rituals, the in-jokes — those are the company's to write. The skill names where they go and how onboarding routes through them; the actual text is authored by the company.
- The skill does not address compliance-mandated onboarding training (anti-harassment training, safety training, regulated-industry training). Those have specific format, tracking, and retention requirements that should be handled in their own dedicated section, with this skill's section linking to them rather than absorbing them.
- The skill cannot account for hiring-velocity surges. A company that scales from 15-per-quarter to 50-per-quarter overnight will find the buddy and milestone-check structures strained; the section needs to be revisited at that scale-up.
