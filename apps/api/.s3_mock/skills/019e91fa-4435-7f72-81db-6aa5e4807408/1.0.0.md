# Medical Evidence Certainty Grader

## When to use

Use this skill once a synthesis has produced a per-outcome effect estimate (pooled or narrative) and the review team is ready to translate that estimate into a defensible body-of-evidence certainty rating. The rating is what readers — clinicians, guideline panels, patients, payers — actually use to decide how confident to be in the result.

**This skill produces methodology guidance only. Outputs are not clinical recommendations. Every conclusion about treatment efficacy or safety must be reviewed by qualified clinical/research staff and confirmed against current clinical practice guidelines and primary literature.**

Typical triggers:

- A review draft has pooled estimates and per-study bias ratings; the team needs the certainty column for the summary table.
- A guideline panel is preparing recommendations and needs explicit certainty ratings for each critical outcome.
- A health-technology-assessment report needs an evidence-profile section.
- A peer reviewer has asked the authors to make their certainty reasoning explicit, including which considerations they downgraded for and how the upgrade conditions for non-randomized evidence were judged.

Do not use this skill for:

- Per-study bias appraisal — that is the upstream activity and lives in the risk-of-bias appraisal skill.
- Producing the pooled estimates — that is the synthesis stage.
- Writing clinical recommendations — the certainty rating informs but does not become the recommendation. A separate decision process weighs benefits, harms, values, preferences, resource use, equity, and acceptability.

## Inputs

- `body_of_evidence_summary` (required) — A per-outcome structured summary including: number of studies, designs, total participants, pooled effect with confidence interval (or, for narrative synthesis, a structured description of the direction and consistency of results), heterogeneity statistics (chi-square, I-square, prediction interval, tau-square if available), per-study risk-of-bias ratings, anticipated absolute baseline risk(s) for the summary table (often two — a low and a high anticipated baseline).
- `review_question` (required) — The structured target. Indirectness considers the gap between the population, intervention, comparator, and outcome of the synthesis and those of the target question.
- `design_class` (required) — The predominant design family for the outcome. Different design classes start at different default certainty levels.
- `decision_thresholds` — Pre-specified minimally important difference and any clinical decision boundary. Imprecision is best judged against decision thresholds rather than against the null alone.
- `known_concerns` — Anything the team has already flagged. The skill should address each in the reasoning even if it ends up disagreeing.

## How to apply

Apply the steps in order. Each downgrade or upgrade is a deliberate, documented decision — record the reasoning even when no change is applied.

### 1. Set the starting certainty level by design class

The default starting points are:

- **Randomized trials:** start at high certainty.
- **Non-randomized studies:** start at low certainty.
- **Mixed bodies of evidence with randomized dominance:** start at high and downgrade as appropriate.
- **Mixed bodies with non-randomized dominance:** start at low and consider upgrades.
- **Diagnostic-accuracy studies:** start at high when the study designs are appropriate for the question (cross-sectional with consecutive enrollment and an applied reference standard).
- **Prognostic-factor studies:** start at high when the studies are cohort designs with appropriate confounding control; otherwise treat as non-randomized starting at low.

State the starting level explicitly and the reason for it. If the body of evidence is heterogeneous in design, note the dominance rule used.

### 2. Consideration one — study limitations (downgrade)

Look at the per-study risk-of-bias ratings, weighted by each study's contribution to the pooled estimate (study weight is approximately inverse-variance for fixed-effect or related for random-effects models). Judge:

- **No downgrade** when the studies weighted heavily in the pool are predominantly low risk and the result is unchanged in a sensitivity analysis that excludes the higher-risk studies.
- **Downgrade one level (serious limitations)** when several studies are at some-concerns or high risk in domains plausibly related to the effect direction, or when a sensitivity analysis excluding high-risk studies materially changes the estimate.
- **Downgrade two levels (very serious limitations)** when most studies weighted heavily are at high risk in decision-relevant domains, and the sensitivity analysis result is markedly different.

Document the dominant per-study limitation that drove the judgment (e.g., "open-label studies of subjective outcomes" or "differential loss to follow-up").

### 3. Consideration two — inconsistency across studies (downgrade)

Examine across-study heterogeneity. Indicators include:

- The point estimates of individual studies (do they line up, or do they spread across no-effect and meaningful-effect ranges?).
- Statistical heterogeneity (chi-square p-value, I-square percentage, prediction interval width).
- The plausibility of clinical or methodological subgroup explanations.

Decision rules:

- **No downgrade** when point estimates are reasonably aligned, statistical heterogeneity is low, and the prediction interval lies on one side of the decision threshold.
- **Downgrade one level (serious inconsistency)** when point estimates span the decision threshold, heterogeneity is moderate to substantial, and no convincing subgroup explanation has been pre-specified.
- **Downgrade two levels** rare; reserved for severe and unexplained heterogeneity where the body of evidence does not support a directional conclusion.

If heterogeneity is explained by a pre-specified subgroup (e.g., effect differs by baseline severity), grade the subgroup-specific estimates separately rather than penalizing the overall pool.

### 4. Consideration three — indirectness (downgrade)

Indirectness is the gap between the question the body of evidence answers and the question the review is asking. Four sources:

- **Population indirectness** — included studies enroll populations that differ from the review target (different severity, age band, comorbidity profile).
- **Intervention indirectness** — included studies use a related but not identical intervention (different agent, different dose, different delivery setting).
- **Comparator indirectness** — included studies compare against a comparator different from the review's target comparator (e.g., placebo when the review target is an active comparator).
- **Outcome indirectness** — included studies measure a surrogate or proxy rather than the patient-important outcome (e.g., laboratory marker for a downstream clinical event).

Decision rules:

- **No downgrade** when the body of evidence aligns with the target on all four dimensions.
- **Downgrade one level (serious indirectness)** when there is meaningful gap on one dimension, or modest gap on two.
- **Downgrade two levels** when gaps are substantial across multiple dimensions.

The most common source for surrogates is outcome indirectness. State the surrogate-to-outcome chain explicitly when downgrading for this reason.

### 5. Consideration four — imprecision (downgrade)

Imprecision is the most decision-driven consideration. The question is not "is the CI wide?" but "does the CI cross a decision threshold relevant to this question?"

Approach:

5.1. Identify the decision threshold(s) for the outcome. Two common patterns: a minimally important difference (MID) on each side of no effect, and a single clinically meaningful boundary (e.g., a hazard ratio crossing 1.0 for a mortality outcome).

5.2. Examine the pooled estimate and its CI relative to the threshold(s).

5.3. Decision rules:

- **No downgrade** when the CI is entirely on one side of the decision threshold.
- **Downgrade one level (serious imprecision)** when the CI crosses one decision threshold (e.g., the CI excludes harm but does not exclude clinically unimportant benefit), or when the total number of events / sample size is below an optimal information size for the comparison.
- **Downgrade two levels** when the CI crosses multiple decision thresholds (e.g., spans clinically meaningful harm, no effect, and clinically meaningful benefit), or when the comparison is severely underpowered.

For rare outcomes, total event counts matter more than total sample size. A common heuristic: fewer than approximately 300 events for a binary outcome warrants careful imprecision review even when CIs look reasonable.

### 6. Consideration five — publication bias (downgrade)

Publication bias is the hardest to judge because the evidence for it is often indirect. Sources of suspicion:

- A skewed funnel plot when at least roughly ten studies are available for visual inspection (smaller numbers do not support reliable inference).
- A pattern of small positive studies and absent small null studies.
- Discrepancies between published reports and registered protocols.
- A field with known industry sponsorship where the published trial set under-represents trials with sponsor-unfavorable results.

Decision rules:

- **No downgrade** when no plausible mechanism for selective publication is identified and the body of evidence includes a defensible set of registered trials.
- **Downgrade one level (strongly suspected)** when the funnel-plot asymmetry is striking, when the set is dominated by small positive industry-funded studies with weak registry coverage, or when known reports are missing.
- **Downgrade two levels** rare; reserved for cases of documented suppression.

State the specific mechanism suspected when downgrading; "publication bias is possible" is not a justification.

### 7. Upgrade conditions for non-randomized evidence

Bodies of evidence starting at low certainty can be upgraded when specific conditions hold. Use sparingly and document carefully.

7.1. **Large effect.** Upgrade one level when the pooled effect is large (commonly a relative risk roughly halved or doubled) and the effect cannot be plausibly explained by residual confounding. Upgrade two levels when the effect is very large (commonly a relative risk roughly quartered or quintupled) and confounding cannot plausibly account for the magnitude.

7.2. **Dose-response gradient.** Upgrade one level when there is a credible dose-response (or duration-response, or intensity-response) relationship across studies or within studies. The gradient should be in the expected direction for the proposed mechanism.

7.3. **Plausible residual confounding biases toward the null.** Upgrade one level when the plausible direction of unmeasured confounding would shrink the observed effect rather than inflate it, so the true effect is likely at least as large as observed. This requires a defensible mechanistic argument, not a hand-wave.

State explicitly which upgrade condition is being applied and why the other plausible interpretations (e.g., unmeasured confounding inflating the observed effect) have been ruled out.

### 8. Combine downgrades and upgrades into a final rating

Rating levels are typically: high, moderate, low, very low.

8.1. Start with the design-class default.

8.2. Apply each downgrade (one or two levels per consideration). Total downgrades cannot push the rating below very low.

8.3. Apply upgrades only for non-randomized starting points where conditions are met.

8.4. State the final rating with the full path: starting level, each downgrade or upgrade with rationale, final level. The path is the audit trail.

8.5. For randomized starting points, multiple modest concerns can compound into a downgrade (e.g., minor study limitations + minor inconsistency + minor imprecision can together warrant a one-level downgrade even if no single consideration is decisive). Note this compounding judgment explicitly.

### 9. Translate the rating into a plain-language statement

Each certainty level has a conventional plain-language statement that travels well to non-methodologist readers:

- **High** — the true effect is likely close to the estimated effect.
- **Moderate** — the true effect is probably close to the estimate, but there is a possibility that it is substantially different.
- **Low** — the true effect may be substantially different from the estimate.
- **Very low** — the true effect is likely to be substantially different from the estimate.

Use these phrasings or equivalent paraphrases when emitting the rating for a reader-facing audience.

### 10. Build the evidence-profile table

Produce a structured table with:

- Outcome name and follow-up window.
- Number of studies and total participants.
- Predominant study design.
- Per-consideration judgments (study limitations / inconsistency / indirectness / imprecision / publication bias / upgrades for non-randomized) with a one-line reason.
- Pooled relative effect (with CI) or narrative summary.
- Anticipated absolute effect at one or two baseline risks (low-risk and high-risk anticipated population).
- Certainty rating with the plain-language statement.
- Footnotes for any judgment calls.

Anticipated absolute effects are calculated from the relative effect and the baseline risk; the calculation should be shown so a reader can verify.

### 11. Differentiate certainty in the effect from certainty in a decision

A high certainty in the effect estimate does not automatically translate to a strong recommendation, and a low certainty does not necessarily translate to a weak recommendation. State this distinction in the output for guideline contexts. Recommendations weigh benefits, harms, values, preferences, resource use, equity, and acceptability; certainty is one input among many.

### 12. Internal validation before emit

Before producing the final output, check:

- Starting level matches the design class with a stated reason.
- Every consideration is addressed; "no downgrade" is documented when no downgrade is applied.
- Each downgrade names the specific source (e.g., "downgrade for indirectness via outcome — surrogate marker").
- Upgrades are applied only to non-randomized starting points and the specific condition is named.
- Final rating follows from the path; compounding of modest concerns is documented.
- Absolute effects in the table are computed from relative effects and stated baseline risks.
- Plain-language statement matches the rating level.
- Open questions list each borderline judgment for the methodology lead.

### 13. Emit

Produce `certainty_rating_markdown` with the full reasoning path, `evidence_profile_table` for reader-facing communication, and `open_questions` for sign-off.

### Decision rules and heuristics

- **Imprecision is a decision-relative judgment.** Wide CIs are fine if they sit on one side of the decision threshold. Tight CIs around the null can still warrant a downgrade if a clinically meaningful effect cannot be excluded.
- **Heterogeneity has direction.** When point estimates all favor the intervention but vary in magnitude, the directional inference may be sound even with statistical heterogeneity present.
- **Upgrades are uncommon.** Most observational evidence stays at low certainty. Document upgrades carefully because they will be scrutinized.
- **Surrogate downgrades require a chain.** State the surrogate, the intended downstream outcome, the evidence that the surrogate tracks the outcome, and where the chain is weakest.
- **Funnel plots need numbers.** Fewer than approximately ten studies is too few for visual asymmetry to support a downgrade on that basis alone; consider mechanism instead.
- **The same evidence can warrant different ratings for different outcomes.** Mortality and quality-of-life from the same trials may end up at different certainty levels because of measurement, follow-up, and imprecision differences.
- **Borderline calls go to the methodology lead.** Document the borderline rather than forcing a decision.

### Edge cases

- **Single trial bodies of evidence.** Even a large single trial may warrant a downgrade for imprecision (no replication) or indirectness (one setting). State the reasoning carefully.
- **Diagnostic-accuracy outcomes.** Sensitivity and specificity have separate certainty ratings; do not conflate them. Imprecision is judged on the CI for each.
- **Composite outcomes.** When the components differ in importance, certainty for the composite may be lower than for any single component. Consider grading components separately.
- **Outcomes measured only at one time point in some studies.** Indirectness via time horizon may warrant a downgrade if the review target is a different time horizon.
- **Sparse-event outcomes (few events in one or both arms).** Standard heterogeneity and imprecision metrics behave poorly. Use methods designed for sparse data and document the methodological caveat.
- **Mixed designs for the same outcome.** Consider grading randomized and non-randomized evidence separately if their conclusions diverge; otherwise apply the dominance rule and document.
- **Outcomes with no eligible studies.** Rate as "no included evidence" rather than very low; the distinction matters to guideline panels.
- **Living reviews with new evidence between rating cycles.** Date-stamp every rating and recompute when new studies cross a pre-specified threshold for re-grading.
- **Network meta-analysis.** Add a transitivity and a coherence consideration to the standard downgrades; this skill does not produce network-meta-analysis-specific guidance but flags the additional considerations.

## Outputs

- `certainty_rating_markdown` — Markdown with the full reasoning path per outcome.
- `evidence_profile_table` — Reader-facing table with study counts, per-consideration judgments, relative and absolute effects, and the final rating.
- `open_questions` — Borderline judgments and missing inputs requiring methodology-lead sign-off.

## Examples

### Worked example (abbreviated)

Input:

> Outcome: composite cardiovascular death + hospitalization for heart failure at 12 months, in adults with HFpEF without diabetes treated with an SGLT2 inhibitor vs. placebo.
> 4 randomized trials, n=8,950 total, low-risk-of-bias for outcome ascertainment (registry linkage + central adjudication).
> Pooled relative risk 0.79 (95% CI 0.70 to 0.89), I-square 14%, prediction interval 0.62 to 1.00.
> Anticipated baseline risk: 14% / year (low-risk profile) and 27% / year (high-risk profile).
> Decision threshold: minimally important reduction ≥ 10% relative.

Output (abbreviated):

```
Outcome: Composite CV death + HF hospitalization at 12 months
Design class: randomized trials → starting level: high

Study limitations: no downgrade. Outcome ascertainment is registry-based with central
  adjudication; risk-of-bias domains are low across the contributing trials.
Inconsistency: no downgrade. I-square 14%, prediction interval narrow on the
  beneficial side, point estimates aligned.
Indirectness: no downgrade. Populations align with the target (HFpEF, non-diabetic);
  outcomes are patient-important; comparator is the target comparator (placebo).
Imprecision: no downgrade. CI (0.70 to 0.89) lies entirely below the no-effect line
  and below the minimally important threshold of 0.90 relative risk.
Publication bias: no downgrade. Trial set aligns with registered protocols;
  no asymmetry in funnel-plot review (n=4 too few for formal test but no missing-trial
  pattern suggested by registry cross-check).

Final certainty: HIGH

Plain-language statement: The true effect is likely close to a roughly 21% relative reduction
  in the composite outcome at 12 months.

Anticipated absolute effects:
  Low baseline risk (14%/yr): 29 fewer events per 1,000 per year (95% CI: 15 fewer to 42 fewer)
  High baseline risk (27%/yr): 57 fewer events per 1,000 per year (95% CI: 30 fewer to 81 fewer)
```

Open questions (abbreviated):

```
- Confirm whether the prediction-interval upper bound (0.99 in some specifications) is close
  enough to 1.0 to merit a soft note in the reader-facing text, even though it does not change
  the certainty rating.
- Verify that the high-risk-profile baseline rate aligns with the team's intended target
  population; the 27% figure is from one cohort source — sensitivity table requested.
- Confirm that the four trials are the full set of trials matching eligibility; registry cross-check
  should be documented in the appendix.
```

## Limitations

- Certainty rating is a structured judgment, not a calculation. Two careful methodologists can reach different ratings for the same evidence; the value of the framework is making the disagreement explicit.
- Imprecision judgments depend on the team's pre-specified decision thresholds. If thresholds are post-hoc, ratings should be treated as exploratory.
- Publication bias is the weakest link in many ratings because direct evidence of suppression is rare. State the mechanism, not the bare suspicion.
- Upgrade conditions for non-randomized evidence are easy to misuse. Apply them only when the specific condition is met and document the reasoning at length.
- The skill does not compute the underlying pooled estimate or its CI; those are inputs.
- The skill produces certainty for one outcome at a time; multi-outcome reviews should invoke it iteratively and assemble the evidence profile from the per-outcome outputs.
- The output is a methodology product, not a clinical recommendation. Recommendation development is a separate process.
