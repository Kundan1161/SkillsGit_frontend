## When to use

Invoke this skill when a hiring manager or recruiter needs a finished job description for an open role and has at least a short brief on what the role does, who it reports into, and what a viable candidate looks like. The skill is calibrated for individual contributor and people-manager roles up through senior director. It works equally well for technical, operational, commercial, and creative roles, and adapts vocabulary to the domain.

Do not use this skill for executive search collateral aimed at board-level recruitment, which has different conventions around confidentiality, scope language, and search-firm involvement. Do not use it for academic faculty postings, which carry institution-specific governance language and committee structures the skill does not model. For freelance or contractor scopes of work, prefer a statement-of-work format rather than the employment-oriented sections produced here.

If the role brief is missing, ask one focused intake question before drafting. The single most common failure mode for job descriptions is overconfident invention of responsibilities that the actual hiring manager does not endorse, which then surfaces during the interview loop as a credibility gap with candidates. A thin brief is worth a clarification round; a fabricated brief is not.

## How to apply

A job description does three jobs at once. It attracts the right candidates by giving them a clear picture of the role and the team. It deters the wrong candidates by being specific about what is required and what is not. And it serves as a contract between the hiring manager, the recruiter, and the interview loop about what is actually being assessed. The methodology below treats all three as primary; skills that optimize for only one tend to produce postings that win on volume and lose on conversion, or vice versa.

### Step 1 — Parse the brief and identify the load-bearing facts

Read the brief end-to-end before writing a single sentence. Identify the four load-bearing facts that the entire posting must serve: the team mission, the candidate problem space, the level of seniority, and the non-negotiable qualifications. Note any factual gaps that would force you to invent: ambiguous level, vague responsibilities, a missing comp band in a jurisdiction that requires one, no clarity on remote policy.

If two or more of those four facts are missing, return a single focused intake question rather than drafting. A common failure mode is to write a polished posting around a hollow brief; the result is a description that reads well but cannot survive the first interview round because nothing in it is anchored to the real team.

### Step 2 — Choose a structural template that matches the role family

The skill maintains three core templates, each tuned to a different reading audience.

- **Builder-track template** (engineering, design, data, applied science). Lead with the technical problem space, the stack or research domain, and a concrete picture of the first two quarters of work. Builders read for substance and skim past corporate framing.
- **Operator-track template** (operations, finance, people, legal, marketing). Lead with the team's mandate and the operating cadence the role will own. Operators read for scope and authority.
- **Commercial-track template** (sales, customer success, partnerships, business development). Lead with the territory or segment, the quota or success bar, and the support structure. Commercial candidates read for fairness of the bar and the credibility of the support.

Pick exactly one. Mixing templates produces posts that feel committee-written and fail to signal which kind of person the role is for.

### Step 3 — Draft the opening section

The opening of a job description is read by every candidate; the rest is increasingly skimmed. Make the opening earn the rest.

- One sentence about the team and what it is responsible for.
- One to two sentences about why the role exists right now. (A new product line, a scale milestone, a gap the team has been working around, a strategic bet.)
- One sentence about what the candidate will own end-to-end. This is the single most powerful retention sentence in the entire post; underspecified ownership is the leading reason senior candidates self-deselect.

Avoid mission-statement openers ("We're a fast-growing company changing the world of X"). Candidates discount them. Open with the work.

### Step 4 — Draft the responsibilities section

Aim for five to eight bullets. Each bullet should:

- Start with an action verb that matches the actual work, not the title. ("Investigate", "design", "negotiate", "ship", "calibrate".) Avoid filler verbs like "support", "assist", and "help" unless the role is genuinely subordinate; senior candidates read those as level signals.
- Describe a category of work, not a single task. ("Lead the on-call rotation for the payments service" is better than "Respond to pages.")
- Land on an outcome or a system. ("...so payment fraud rates stay under target during peak load" is better than no anchor.)

Resist the temptation to enumerate every possible duty. Postings with twenty bullets are read as expectations of being expected to do everything, and the strongest candidates are the most likely to read that as a sign the team has not decided what it wants.

### Step 5 — Draft the must-haves with deliberate restraint

Must-haves are the section where job descriptions silently lose the candidates the team most wants. Two failure modes recur:

- **Inflation.** The hiring manager lists every skill anyone on the team has ever used. The result is a wall of bullets that no one human has all of. The strongest candidates read that and conclude the team is unclear about its needs; weaker candidates apply anyway because they read job descriptions less critically. The selection pressure runs backwards.
- **Proxy stacking.** A must-have like "10+ years of experience" stands in for a real requirement that the manager has not articulated. Translate proxies into the underlying skill ("has led at least one production migration of a stateful service with downtime under one hour") and the posting both shortens and gets more precise.

Keep must-haves to three to seven items. Each one must be something a candidate could fail and disqualify them. Move everything else to preferred.

Research on candidate self-selection has consistently shown that under-represented groups apply at lower rates when they do not meet every listed requirement, even when the listed requirements are inflated. Restraint in this section is the single most leveraged anti-bias action the skill performs.

### Step 6 — Draft the preferred section as genuinely preferred

Label the section "Preferred" or "Nice to have", and prefix the section with a single sentence that explicitly says these are bonuses, not screening criteria. Keep this section short — three to five bullets — and stop there. A long preferred list reproduces the inflation problem under a different header.

### Step 7 — Draft the growth and impact section

This is the section the post most often omits and the section the strongest candidates most often look for. It answers two questions: what will this person learn here that they could not learn elsewhere, and what is the realistic next move from this role in eighteen to twenty-four months.

Write two to four sentences. Avoid clichés about "learning opportunity"; name something specific. Examples of well-formed answers:

- "By month nine, you will have shipped at least one cross-org platform initiative that involves both the data and product orgs; this is the senior-staff to staff-plus transition path on our engineering ladder."
- "This role owns the first international expansion. The candidate who succeeds here is the candidate who runs the region as a GM eighteen months from now."
- "We are intentionally hiring someone hungrier than the title implies; the path to lead is fast for the person who builds the first three customer integrations."

Vague growth language ("opportunities for advancement") reads as boilerplate. Specific growth language reads as a real conversation.

### Step 8 — Compensation, benefits, and total reward

In jurisdictions with pay-transparency laws (Colorado, California, New York City, Washington, Illinois, and a growing list — check the location_policy input against current law before publishing), a salary range is legally required and not optional. The skill always recommends including a range; in jurisdictions without a legal requirement, ranges still measurably increase application volume and reduce candidate-recruiter friction at the offer stage.

Format the range as a single visible number band, plus a one-line explanation of how level placement is determined. Mention equity, bonus, or commission structure if the role has them. Avoid listing every benefit; pick three to five distinctive items that signal company values (parental leave length, learning budget, sabbatical policy, mental health support, equipment budget) rather than commodity benefits that all employers offer.

### Step 9 — Location and work arrangement

State the location policy in plain terms in a dedicated line near the top. Candidates who self-disqualify on location read job descriptions impatiently; burying remote policy halfway down the post is read as evasive.

Use precise terms:

- **Fully remote** — the role can be performed from anywhere in the listed jurisdictions, with no in-office requirement.
- **Remote with periodic onsite** — most work is remote but onsite weeks or quarterly gatherings are expected; specify the frequency.
- **Hybrid** — some days per week in office; specify the number and which office.
- **Onsite** — full-time at a named office.

For remote roles, name the jurisdictions or time zones supported. "Remote" without geographic scope is a recurring source of late-stage candidate churn when a US-based posting turns out to exclude Canada or Europe.

### Step 10 — Application and interview transparency

Add a short closing section that names what the interview loop looks like. This is the second-highest-leverage retention move in the post (after specific ownership in Step 3). Candidates who know what they are signing up for convert at noticeably higher rates than candidates who do not.

A typical short summary:

- One screening conversation with the recruiter, 30 minutes.
- One conversation with the hiring manager, 45 minutes.
- A loop of two to four interviews focused on named signals (problem solving, role-specific craft, collaboration, leadership) with a one-line description of each.
- A reference and offer stage.

Do not over-promise on speed. Promised cycle times that the team cannot hold against an active pipeline create a reputational cost that compounds across candidate networks.

### Step 11 — Run the anti-bias review pass

Once the draft is complete, run a structured review pass before declaring the post done. The pass looks for:

- **Gender-coded vocabulary.** Words like "rockstar", "ninja", "aggressive", "dominant", "competitive", "hacker" are statistically gender-skewed and depress application rates from women. Suggest neutral alternatives ("skilled", "effective", "driven", "results-focused").
- **Coded culture signals.** Phrases like "work hard play hard", "we eat lunch as a team every day", or "thrive under pressure" can read as exclusion signals to candidates who weigh culture differently. Flag rather than auto-remove; the hiring manager may want to keep some.
- **Inflated education or pedigree requirements.** Flag any "Bachelor's degree required" line and ask whether the role actually requires a degree or whether equivalent experience suffices.
- **Age-coded language.** "Recent graduate", "digital native", "high-energy environment" can read as age-exclusionary. Suggest neutral alternatives.
- **Disability-exclusionary requirements.** Physical or sensory requirements (standing, lifting, speaking, hearing) should be listed only when genuinely essential and accompanied by a willingness to discuss accommodations.
- **Inaccessible formatting.** Avoid emoji-heavy bullets, very long unbroken paragraphs, and color-only emphasis. Postings often end up read by screen readers.

Output the bias audit as a short, itemized report alongside the rewritten posting, so the hiring manager can see what was changed and why.

### Step 12 — Tighten and shorten

The final pass is a length pass. Postings of 250 to 600 words consistently outperform longer ones on completed-application rate. Remove:

- Duplicated points across responsibilities and must-haves.
- Adjective stacks ("dynamic, fast-paced, innovative, results-driven").
- Internal team jargon and acronyms unless they are industry-standard.
- Multiple opening sentences when one would suffice.

Read the final draft aloud. If a section sounds like marketing copy rather than a real description of work, rewrite it as a real description of work.

### Step 13 — Produce the posting checklist

Output a short pre-publish checklist for the recruiter, covering:

- Pay-transparency disclosure included where legally required.
- Equal opportunity statement and accommodations contact line included.
- Application URL, ATS configuration, and screening questions confirmed.
- Internal posting period observed if required by company policy.
- Visa sponsorship policy stated if relevant.
- Length within the channel limit (LinkedIn 2,000 characters, careers page longer permitted).
- Confirmation that the hiring manager has reviewed the responsibilities and must-haves for accuracy.

The checklist is a deliverable, not commentary. The recruiter or hiring manager runs it and signs off before posting.

### Step 14 — Output a short debrief

After the deliverables, output a brief debrief: the structural template chosen and why; the must-haves that were tightened; the bias flags that triggered rewrites; and any inputs that, if supplied, would have improved the posting. This is iteration fuel for the next role brief.

## Inputs

- **role_brief** (required) — purpose, team context, six- and twelve-month success picture.
- **must_haves** (required) — the disqualifying-if-absent qualifications.
- **nice_to_haves** (optional) — strengthening but not screening.
- **comp_band** (optional) — base, equity, bonus or commission, total reward framing.
- **location_policy** (optional) — remote, hybrid, onsite, with geographies.
- **company_context** (optional) — stage, mission, distinctive team facts.

## Outputs

- A finished, posting-ready job description in inclusive plain language.
- A bias audit report itemizing flagged language and the rewrites applied.
- A pre-publish checklist covering legal disclosures and channel formatting.

## Examples

### Worked example — Senior Backend Engineer, Series B fintech, remote US

**Inputs given to the skill:**

- role_brief: "We're hiring a senior backend engineer on the payments platform team. The team owns ledger, settlements, and reconciliation. The role exists because we are doubling transaction volume in the next year and the on-call rotation is unsustainable with the current team size."
- must_haves: "Five plus years building distributed systems, on-call experience in production, written language fluency in technical design, has shipped at least one zero-downtime data migration."
- nice_to_haves: "Familiarity with double-entry accounting, payment rails, regulatory environments."
- comp_band: "$180k-$220k base plus 0.08-0.18% equity, US-only."
- location_policy: "Fully remote in the US, quarterly onsite weeks in NYC."
- company_context: "Series B, 80 people, building infrastructure for B2B fintech. Profitable, last raise was top-up not survival."

**Output produced by the skill (abridged):**

> **Senior Backend Engineer, Payments Platform**
>
> Our payments platform team owns the ledger, settlement, and reconciliation infrastructure that every product line at the company depends on. We are doubling transaction volume in the next twelve months, and we are hiring a senior backend engineer because the team's on-call rotation cannot scale with the current shape of the work.
>
> You will own a major surface of the platform end-to-end — most likely the reconciliation pipeline first, then a co-design role on the next-generation ledger as we partition for scale.
>
> **What you will work on**
>
> - Investigate and resolve discrepancies in production payment flows, often spanning multiple internal services and at least one external partner rail.
> - Lead the design of the next major partition strategy for the ledger as transaction volume doubles, in close partnership with the platform tech lead.
> - Own the on-call rotation alongside three other senior engineers, with a shared goal of pages below the team's published reliability target.
> - Ship a zero-downtime migration at least once a year. We have done four in the last two years.
> - Mentor mid-level engineers on the team; we expect senior engineers to actively grow the people around them.
>
> **What we need**
>
> - Five or more years building distributed systems in production environments.
> - On-call experience and a point of view on what makes a humane rotation.
> - Track record of leading at least one zero-downtime data migration end-to-end.
> - Fluent technical writing — we run a design-doc culture.
>
> **Preferred (not required)**
>
> Familiarity with double-entry accounting, payment rails (ACH, card networks, wire), or financial-services regulatory contexts is a meaningful bonus but not a screening criterion.
>
> **Growth path from this role**
>
> Within twelve to eighteen months, this role is the natural staff-engineer track on the platform team. Two of the last three platform-team staff promotions came from roles like this one.
>
> **Compensation and location**
>
> $180,000 to $220,000 base salary depending on level placement, plus 0.08% to 0.18% equity. Fully remote within the United States, with one onsite week per quarter in New York.
>
> **Interview process**
>
> Recruiter screen (30 minutes), hiring manager conversation (45 minutes), three-interview loop (system design, coding, collaboration), reference checks, offer. We target two to three weeks from first conversation to offer.
>
> [Equal opportunity statement and accommodations contact follow.]

**Bias audit report (abridged):**

- "rockstar" removed from earlier draft ("We need a rockstar engineer who can move fast") and replaced with concrete experience requirements.
- "fast-paced" softened to "we are doubling volume" — concrete fact vs. coded culture signal.
- "Bachelor's degree required" never appeared and was not added; the must-haves are skill- and experience-based.
- "competitive comp" replaced with explicit numeric range, per pay-transparency norms.

**Pre-publish checklist (abridged):**

- Pay range included (numeric, not "competitive"): yes.
- Equal opportunity language: yes.
- Visa policy stated: no — confirm with recruiter; US-only language implies no sponsorship but worth explicit statement.
- LinkedIn character count: 1,640 / 2,000.

**Debrief:**

Builder-track template selected because the team is engineering-led and reads for substance. Must-haves were tightened from an initial draft of nine bullets to four; the rest moved to preferred or absorbed into responsibilities. The growth section had to be drafted from scratch because the brief did not include it; recommend the hiring manager review and confirm. The location_policy input was unusually clear and prevented late-stage churn.

## Limitations

This skill writes posting copy. It does not crawl the company's careers site or post to channels; it produces text the recruiter pastes into the ATS. It does not check whether the salary band is competitive against the market; combine with a compensation-benchmarking source for that judgment.

The bias review pass is heuristic. It catches the most-cited coded vocabulary but cannot detect every subtle exclusionary signal, and it cannot validate whether the underlying job requirements themselves are fair. A human reviewer with DEI context should still read the final draft before posting for senior or high-visibility roles.

Pay-transparency law varies by jurisdiction and is changing rapidly. The skill flags the most-cited jurisdictions but is not a legal-compliance tool. The hiring manager or in-house counsel should confirm the final posting meets current local requirements before publication.

The skill assumes the brief is accurate. If the hiring manager has misjudged the level, the market, or the team's actual needs, the posting will be a well-formed expression of a wrong brief. This is the single largest source of failed searches, and no posting copy can fully compensate for it.

The skill does not write executive search collateral, academic faculty postings, or contractor scopes of work; those use different conventions and the methodology here will produce a poor fit if applied to them.
