## When to use

Invoke this skill when an interview loop has completed and the panel needs to converge on a hire/no-hire decision. The skill takes individual interview write-ups, synthesizes them against the rubric, names the points of agreement and disagreement, drives the panel past gut-feeling consensus toward rubric-anchored judgment, and produces a written rationale that can sit in the ATS as the durable record of the decision.

Use this skill when the loop is structured: signals were defined upfront, a rubric exists, and interviewers scored independently. The methodology assumes those preconditions. Without them, the skill can still help the panel reason, but it will produce a lower-confidence decision because the inputs are noisier.

Do not use this skill as a substitute for the human debrief meeting. The skill produces the synthesis and the rationale, but the legal and operational record of the decision is made by the hiring manager and the recruiter, not by an AI tool. The output is decision-support, not decision-replacement.

Do not use the skill when the loop has fewer than three independent interviewers covering the signal set. The synthesis methodology assumes multi-rater coverage; with fewer raters, the math degrades to a single interviewer's impression and any synthesis will be artificially confident.

Do not use this skill to make level decisions when only one level was assessed. If the panel is considering down-leveling or up-leveling the candidate, the rubric must include anchors at both levels. Otherwise the level discussion is anchored only in feeling, which is the exact pattern the methodology is trying to displace.

## How to apply

The debrief is where most well-designed loops fail. Years of behavioral research on group decisions converge on a small set of failure modes: the most senior person speaks first and anchors everyone else; disagreement is averaged rather than investigated; verbal extroverts dominate quiet but careful interviewers; and the panel's collective enthusiasm or fatigue at the end of the loop substitutes for rubric judgment. A good facilitation methodology actively counters each of these.

### Step 1 — Verify the inputs are debrief-ready

Before drafting any synthesis, check the inputs:

- Each interviewer has submitted scoring per signal, with a written justification anchored to the rubric (not just a numeric score).
- The signal set in the rubric matches the signal coverage in the write-ups; no signal is unassessed.
- No interviewer has scored signals they were not assigned to (this is a common quality problem; flag it but proceed).
- The hiring manager has not yet declared a verdict in writing. (If they have, the debrief is already anchored; surface the risk before proceeding.)

If any of these is missing, flag the gap explicitly. The skill is more useful for surfacing a thin loop than for synthesizing it as if it were complete.

### Step 2 — Group the write-ups by signal, not by interviewer

Open the synthesis by reorganizing the inputs. Instead of listing each interviewer's write-up in turn, regroup them so that each signal collects all the evidence the loop generated for it. This is the single biggest mechanical move the methodology depends on; it forces the panel to reason about the candidate one signal at a time rather than about the candidate as a holistic impression.

For each signal, list:

- The interviewers who assessed it.
- Each interviewer's score and the one-to-two-sentence rubric-anchored justification.
- The convergent themes across interviewers, named explicitly.
- The divergent themes, named explicitly and labeled by source.

This regrouping should fit on a page per signal for most loops.

### Step 3 — Name disagreement before averaging it

Where scoring on a signal diverges meaningfully (one rater says strong yes, another says no), do not average. Name the divergence and ask the panel one of three diagnostic questions:

- **Did the two interviewers observe different evidence?** If interviewer A saw the candidate handle a hard cross-functional question well and interviewer B saw the candidate dodge a technical depth question, both are correct; the candidate is strong on one sub-component and weak on another. Update the rubric anchors next loop to separate the sub-components.
- **Did the two interviewers interpret the same evidence differently?** This is the most common case. Both watched the candidate stumble on a design question; one read it as "thinking out loud, healthy", the other as "did not have a framework". This is a rubric calibration gap; the panel discusses what the rubric meant.
- **Is one interviewer's rating drifting from rubric to feeling?** A consistent over- or under-rater versus the panel is information; calibrate that interviewer offline rather than overriding the score.

The right behavior is not to push the panel to consensus. It is to push the panel to understand the disagreement. Forced consensus produces decisions; understood disagreement produces decisions plus calibration for the next loop.

### Step 4 — Apply the bar deliberately

Most teams operate with one of three bar postures:

- **Hire on strong yes or yes across all signals.** The default for senior or high-stakes hires. Any no or strong no on a core signal blocks the hire absent explicit override with rationale.
- **Hire on strong yes on at least one signal, no strong no on any.** Used for junior roles, where the team is hiring potential plus one demonstrated strength rather than a fully proven profile.
- **Two strong yes on the most predictive signals, plus no strong no.** A compromise the panel can adopt for senior roles when the loop has not produced a clear unanimous strong yes.

If the bar_calibration input is supplied, use it. If not, name three candidate postures and ask the hiring manager to pick one before applying it. Applying a bar without naming it is the most common way debriefs accidentally lower the standard over time.

### Step 5 — Probe for gut feelings and demand evidence

The panel will offer assessments that read as gut feeling rather than rubric-anchored judgment. Common phrasings:

- "I just didn't feel like they'd fit on the team."
- "Something was off but I can't quite name it."
- "They're not quite at our bar."
- "I'd hire them but I wouldn't be excited."

Each of these is information, but none is a debrief input. For each, ask the interviewer the diagnostic question: what specific behavior or answer triggered the impression, and which signal does that behavior map to?

If the answer is a specific moment ("when I asked about the cross-team conflict, the candidate spent five minutes blaming the other team without ever naming what they themselves would have done differently"), that is rubric evidence on the collaboration signal. If the answer is "I can't really say specifically", treat the rating as low-confidence and weight other interviewers' more specific evidence more heavily.

This is the highest-leverage facilitation move the skill performs. Without it, debriefs become exchanges of impressions; with it, they become a synthesis of evidence.

### Step 6 — Watch for and counter the standard failure modes

Several patterns recur:

- **Anchoring on the most senior voice.** The VP or hiring manager speaks first, declares an opinion, and the panel converges. Counter by inviting individual writeups before the meeting, walking signal by signal rather than interviewer by interviewer, and having the recruiter (not the hiring manager) run the meeting.
- **Halo and horns.** One striking moment (positive or negative) colors every signal. Counter by forcing per-signal scoring before the meeting and by asking, for each signal, what specific evidence in that station drove the score.
- **Similarity bias.** The interviewer who is most demographically similar to the candidate scores them more favorably; the inverse depresses scoring of underrepresented candidates. Counter by running calibration on similarity-skewed scores across multiple candidates; in this single debrief, surface the pattern if visible and ask the panel to anchor in evidence.
- **Recency bias.** The last interview of the day weighs more than the first. Counter by chronologizing the loop and asking specifically about evidence from earlier stations.
- **Affinity over substance.** "I'd want to work with them" substitutes for rubric judgment. Counter by separating "would I want to work with them" (a real signal, but only one) from craft, judgment, and the other signals.
- **Pity hires and savior hires.** The candidate has been in the loop a long time, the team is tired, "let's just hire". Counter by asking whether the team would extend the offer on this evidence alone if the candidate had walked in fresh today.
- **Reference-prejudgment.** Strong recruiter rapport with the candidate leaks into the technical debrief. Counter by deferring recruiter and reference inputs until the panel has agreed signal-by-signal.

### Step 7 — Drive to a clearly named outcome

The debrief produces one of five outcomes; the skill names which one and the rubric path to it.

- **Hire at the assessed level.** Panel agrees the candidate meets the bar on all required signals. Move to offer.
- **Hire at a different level.** Panel agrees the candidate is strong but at a different seniority than the role was scoped for. Either re-scope or pass.
- **Hold for further information.** A specific signal is under-evidenced and would change the decision. Run one additional structured station targeting that signal, not a full re-loop.
- **Decline with specific rationale.** Panel agrees the candidate does not meet the bar on at least one signal that the team's bar treats as required. Decline with named signal and rubric anchor.
- **Decline with growth feedback.** The candidate is strong enough that a future application should be welcomed; the rationale notes which signals were close and what evidence would tip the next assessment.

Avoid "maybe" as an outcome. "Maybe" usually means the panel has not done the rubric work; the facilitator pushes one more pass through Step 3 and Step 4 before accepting any verdict.

### Step 8 — Make the level decision explicit and defensible

For senior hires, the panel is usually deciding not only whether to hire but at what level. The level decision uses the same rubric, but the anchors are level-specific. A strong yes on judgment at one level is a yes at the next level up. A panel that wants to up-level or down-level the candidate must point to evidence at the proposed level's anchors, not at the assessed level's anchors.

If the rubric does not include the proposed level's anchors, name that gap. Either pull in anchors from the team's career ladder or defer the level decision until the panel can review the next level's expectations.

### Step 9 — Draft the written rationale

Once the decision is named, draft a two-to-four-paragraph written rationale. The rationale is not a transcript of the debrief; it is the durable record. It includes:

- The decision in one sentence, with level if applicable.
- The strongest two pieces of signal evidence for the decision, named by station and rubric anchor.
- Any meaningful divergence the panel resolved, and how. This is read by future calibration sessions.
- For declines, a brief statement of what would change the assessment in a future application, when applicable. (Some declines are categorical — wrong level, missing must-have — and do not need a "come back later" framing.)
- The hiring manager and recruiter who own the decision.

The rationale should read well a year from now to someone who has never met the candidate. If the rationale reads as "we liked them", the methodology was bypassed.

### Step 10 — Output the offer or decline message draft

For hire decisions, the skill drafts the talking points for the offer call (the recruiter delivers the verbal offer; the talking points anchor compensation framing, role specifics, start date, and decision deadline).

For decline decisions, the skill drafts a candidate-facing message that is honest, specific where appropriate, and unfailingly respectful. Decline messages should:

- Name that the team appreciates the candidate's time.
- State the decision clearly without hedging.
- Offer signal-specific feedback only when the candidate has explicitly asked and only when the team is prepared to deliver it consistently (inconsistent feedback delivery is a legal and reputational risk).
- Leave the door open for future application when the assessment supports it.
- Avoid "we went with another candidate" framing, which is rarely true at the signal level.

### Step 11 — Capture calibration learnings for next loop

After the decision, the skill outputs three lines of calibration intelligence:

- Which rubric anchors held up well in this debrief and which were ambiguous.
- Which interviewer's scoring pattern needs follow-up coaching.
- Which signal of the loop is producing the most disagreement and may need re-design.

These three lines are the methodology's flywheel; loops that capture them improve over the next ten candidates, loops that skip them stay at the same maturity for years.

### Step 12 — Run the meta-question before publishing the rationale

Before declaring the decision final, run one meta-question through the panel: if this candidate had a different name, demographic profile, or pedigree but had performed identically in the loop, would the decision change? If the panel hesitates, the rationale needs more rubric anchoring. This is not a check that the decision is fair; it is a check that the rationale could survive an after-the-fact review.

## Inputs

- **role_context** (required) — role title, level, signal set with definitions.
- **rubric** (required) — anchored rubric for the loop.
- **interview_notes** (required) — concatenated, per-interviewer, per-signal write-ups with scores.
- **bar_calibration** (optional) — the team's bar posture.
- **external_context** (optional) — references, comp readiness, competing-offer context.

## Outputs

- A structured debrief meeting agenda.
- A signal-by-signal synthesis with disagreement explicitly named.
- A named decision with rubric-anchored rationale.
- A written rationale for the ATS record, plus an offer-call or decline-message draft.

## Examples

### Worked example — Senior Engineer debrief, mixed panel

**Inputs given to the skill (abridged):**

- role_context: "Senior Engineer (L5), four signals: craft, problem solving, collaboration, communication."
- rubric: anchored rubric provided with three score tiers per signal.
- interview_notes: four write-ups, one per station. Coding station: strong yes on craft, yes on problem solving (staff engineer A). System design: yes on craft, neutral on problem solving (staff engineer B). Behavioral: yes on collaboration, strong yes on communication (engineering manager). Hiring manager screen: yes on collaboration, neutral on problem solving (hiring manager).
- bar_calibration: "We hire on strong yes or yes across all signals; one neutral is permitted if there is a strong yes on a related signal."
- external_context: references positive; comp band $180-220k; no competing offer.

**Output produced by the skill (abridged):**

**Debrief agenda (45 minutes):**

1. Five-minute scoping. Recruiter opens. No verdict declared.
2. Signal-by-signal walk. 30 minutes total. Five minutes per signal plus discussion. (See synthesis below.)
3. Bar application. Five minutes. Pull up the team's bar posture and read it aloud.
4. Decision and rationale draft. Five minutes. Recruiter writes the rationale draft live on screen.

**Signal synthesis:**

**Signal 1 — Craft.** Convergent strong-yes-to-yes. Staff A and B both saw clean execution; specific evidence cited (closed-form solution to the first coding prompt, correct partitioning approach in system design). No divergence. Net: yes leaning strong yes.

**Signal 2 — Problem solving.** Divergent. Staff A: yes (specific moment when the candidate caught an edge case unprompted). Staff B: neutral (candidate did not consider the failure mode of the dependency graph until prompted). Hiring manager: neutral (candidate did not name the strategic implication of the architectural choice on follow-up). Three of four raters in yes-to-neutral range, one in clear yes. Net: yes but with consistent ceiling — the candidate handled tactical depth but did not stretch to strategic framing under pressure.

**Signal 3 — Collaboration.** Convergent yes. Behavioral and hiring manager both saw specific cross-team examples with owned outcomes. No divergence.

**Signal 4 — Communication.** Strong yes from the behavioral interviewer; not directly assessed at the other stations but inferred favorably. Net: strong yes.

**Disagreement named:** the problem-solving spread between staff A (yes) and staff B (neutral) is genuine. Both interviewers observed different prompts. Staff A's "yes" comes from a tactical evidence base; staff B's "neutral" comes from a strategic evidence base. The candidate is a strong tactical problem solver and a neutral strategic problem solver. For an L5 role, both are required, but the team's rubric anchors strategic problem solving at L6 not L5; staff B was inadvertently scoring against L6 anchors. Per the rubric, the right L5 score on problem solving is yes.

**Decision: hire at L5.**

Rationale walk: candidate meets the bar on all four signals. Strong yes on communication and craft, yes on problem solving and collaboration. No strong no anywhere. Per the team's bar posture (strong yes or yes across all signals), this is a hire.

**Written rationale (for the ATS record):**

> We are extending an offer at L5 to this candidate. The loop produced a strong yes on communication, a strong-yes-to-yes on craft, and a yes on both collaboration and problem solving with no strong no on any signal. The most contested signal was problem solving, where one interviewer scored neutral; in the debrief we identified that the neutral was anchored against L6 strategic-framing expectations rather than the L5 rubric, and the candidate's L5-level evidence (tactical depth, edge-case handling, partitioning judgment) was clear. The candidate's communication signal was the strongest signal in the loop, supported by two specific behavioral examples of cross-team escalation. References corroborated the loop's findings.
>
> For future calibration, we will (a) update the problem-solving rubric anchors to make the L5/L6 distinction more explicit, and (b) coach staff B on rubric anchoring against the assessed level. We expect to revisit the strategic-framing signal explicitly when this candidate is up for L6 promotion in 18 to 24 months.

**Offer call talking points:**

- Lead with role specifics: which team, who they will be working with, the on-call rotation reality.
- Comp framing: base $200k, equity range, signing.
- Decision window: aim for one week.
- The candidate asked at the recruiter screen about remote flexibility; reaffirm the remote policy explicitly.

**Calibration learnings:**

- Problem-solving rubric needs sharper L5/L6 separation.
- Staff B reliably tough but anchored against the next level up; valuable rater, but worth a one-on-one calibration.
- Behavioral station's communication-signal scoring was high-confidence; that station is producing the loop's cleanest signal right now.

## Limitations

This skill is decision support, not decision authority. The hiring manager and recruiter own the actual decision and the legal record. The skill produces the synthesis and the rationale; the panel runs the meeting and signs off.

The synthesis is only as good as the inputs. Thin, vague, or feeling-anchored write-ups produce a thin synthesis. The skill flags the input quality problem rather than papering over it; in low-input cases, the output is a structured request for stronger write-ups rather than a confident decision.

The skill does not catch systematic bias across multiple candidates. A panel that consistently down-rates candidates from underrepresented groups will produce per-candidate write-ups that look defensible in isolation; the bias only becomes visible in the cross-candidate pattern. Pair the per-debrief use of this skill with a quarterly review of decision outcomes by demographic dimension; that review is a different methodology.

The skill does not run reference checks or extension negotiations. The offer-call talking points and decline-message draft are anchors for the recruiter, not finished communications.

Some debriefs are inherently hard cases — the candidate the team wants to hire but the rubric does not support; the candidate the rubric supports but the team's enthusiasm is missing. The methodology's value is most visible in those cases: it forces the panel to name what is actually happening rather than convert it to a verdict. The verdict might still be the same; the rationale will be more honest.
