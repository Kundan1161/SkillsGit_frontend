# Color Grading Shot Methodology

## When to use

Use this skill any time the question is "how do I grade this shot?" — whether the colorist is starting from a flat log image, matching a hero shot, or salvaging a problem clip with mixed lighting. It covers the order of operations that keeps grades stable, the node-tree structure that keeps them readable, and the scope discipline that keeps them honest when the monitor is lying because the room is uncalibrated or the colorist's eye has drifted across a long session.

Do not use it as a substitute for the color-pipeline-architect skill — if the input transform is wrong, no grading methodology will save the shot. Confirm the pipeline first, then grade.

## How to apply

1. Confirm the **input transform** is applied. The image on the monitor must be in the working space the grading suite expects (typically a wide-gamut log working space or a scene-linear with view-transform). If the image looks "wrong out of the box" beyond what log encoding normally looks like, stop and verify the IDT before adjusting anything.
2. Set the **view transform** for the delivery target. If grading for Rec.709, the view-transform output should be Rec.709 with the appropriate gamma; if grading for HDR PQ, the output transform must target the mastering peak luminance. The colorist should never grade without the correct view transform downstream, because perception of contrast and saturation depends on it.
3. Read the **scopes** before touching a wheel. Waveform shows luminance distribution top to bottom; RGB parade shows per-channel balance; vectorscope shows hue and saturation; histogram shows distribution mass. Pick the scope that answers the question — exposure questions read on waveform, white-balance on parade, saturation on vectorscope.
4. Apply **primary correction in this order**: exposure first, then contrast, then white balance, then saturation. The reason is sequential dependency — exposure determines what the contrast adjustment is operating on; contrast determines where the white balance reference points sit; white balance determines what saturation reads as "neutral." Reorder this and adjustments fight each other.
5. **Exposure**: set the working midtone where the image expects it. For a neutral subject under key, place the diffuse white reference at the show's chosen reference (a documented IRE for Rec.709, a chosen nit value for HDR). Use the offset or master gain control; avoid using gamma to do exposure work because it warps midtone perception.
6. **Contrast**: set the black point and white point. Pull the deepest legitimate shadow to the working black level (not crushed below it; preserve detail unless the look explicitly calls for crush). Pull the brightest legitimate highlight to the working peak (not clipped; preserve specular roll-off unless the look calls for clipped). Use the contrast control or lift/gain on the wheels. Watch the waveform — the trace should fill the working range without flat-lining at top or bottom.
7. **White balance**: pick a neutral reference in the frame (a gray card, a white wall, a known-neutral skin highlight on a Caucasian or East Asian subject with appropriate skin-tone allowance) and balance the RGB parade so the three channels overlap at that reference. If no neutral exists, use the colorist's eye guided by the vectorscope skin-tone line and the established reference frame for the scene.
8. **Saturation**: pull saturation to the level that matches the reference. The vectorscope is the arbiter — vectors should reach the saturation level of the reference frame, not exceed gamut limits, and skin should align with the skin-tone line. For SDR Rec.709, watch the gamut warning indicators; for HDR, watch that high-saturation highlights do not exceed the display's gamut.
9. Build a **clean primary node** (or layer) holding the four operations from steps 5-8. Name it. The named primary is the colorist's "neutral starting point" — it should produce a balanced, deliverable image without any creative look. Subsequent nodes layer the look on top.
10. Add **secondaries** only after the primary is signed off. A secondary is a targeted correction: a qualifier (HSL key on a hue range), a luma qualifier (a key on a brightness range), or a power window (a shape-based mask, often with tracking). Each secondary lives in its own node so it can be disabled and inspected independently.
11. Use **qualifiers** for color-defined corrections: sky enhancement, foliage shift, skin warmth, costume separation. Pull the qualifier with the matte view active; soften the matte edges; check the matte over motion to confirm it does not break on movement. Apply the correction with restraint — qualifiers are visible the moment they over-correct.
12. Use **power windows** for area-defined corrections: vignettes, eye light, sky darkening above a horizon, sign blowout reduction. Track the window if the camera or subject moves; soften the window edges generously; combine with a qualifier when the area is partially defined by motion and partially by color.
13. Apply the **look** in a final dedicated node or stack. A "look" is the creative tone of the show — film emulation, contrast curve, color cast, vignette. Keep the look as a recallable preset so the same look applies to every shot in the show. Adjust intensity per shot, not look identity per shot, unless the brief explicitly calls for varying looks.
14. **Match against the reference**. Place the reference frame side by side with the current shot. Use a split-screen wipe or a small reference monitor. Compare scope readings: are the diffuse highlights at the same luminance? Are the skin tones at the same point on the vectorscope? Are the shadows the same black level? Adjust the primary, not the look, to land the match.
15. Check **shot-to-shot consistency** by stepping through the cut at speed. Long static studies hide cuts that play poorly at speed. Watch the actual cut — if the eye perceives a jump at the cut, the grade does not match no matter what the scope says. Trust the cut.
16. Verify **legal range**. SDR Rec.709 broadcast typically requires luma 0-100 IRE with limited tolerance for excursion; web SDR allows full range 0-255 8-bit (preserve below 16 and above 235 in 10-bit terms). HDR PQ has its mastering peak (1,000 or 4,000 nit typically); content above the mastering peak will be tone-mapped down by consumer displays. Set scope reference lines for the delivery target and confirm the trace stays inside.
17. Render a **clip and check** in a separate viewing environment. The colorist's grading suite is calibrated; the editorial offline station or producer's laptop is not. A confidence-check render played on a second display, ideally a different display class, surfaces clipping, banding, and color casts that the grading suite hid.
18. **Audit the node graph for cruft** before signoff. A clean shot has a small number of named nodes — input transform, primary, named secondaries, look, output transform — each doing one thing. A shot with twelve unnamed nodes is harder to revise, harder to hand off to a re-grade, and harder to debug when it does not match a reference six weeks later. Combine redundant nodes; delete disabled nodes that no longer serve the shot; name everything.
19. **Save the shot grade as a recallable preset** when it represents a setup that will recur. A two-person interview shot in the same lighting on the same day is the same grade applied twice; the second application is a paste, not a re-grade. Build presets at the scene level when the lighting is consistent.
20. **Flag risk areas for finishing.** Some shots have known fragility — a subject's costume that sits very close to a chroma-key range, a sky gradient that bands at 8-bit delivery, a skin tone that lands at the saturation limit of the deliverable. Note these on the shot record so the finishing pass and the QC team know where to spot-check. The colorist who graded the shot knows where its risks are; nobody else does.

## Inputs

- A shot description with subject, lighting, and what is happening.
- The source encoding and delivery target.
- A reference frame or scene reference if matching.
- Known issues to address (lens cast, mixed lighting, exposure error, white-balance error).
- The shot's position in the cut — hero, match, insert.

## Outputs

- An ordered grade plan with the operations to perform and the scope readings to confirm.
- A described node tree from input transform to output transform.
- Scope checks confirming the grade matches the reference and stays inside the legal range.

## Examples

**Two-camera interview, Camera B looks warm and a half-stop under.** Hero is Camera A graded first: input transform applied, exposure set to land the subject's diffuse white at the show's reference IRE, contrast set, white balance neutralized on the subject's gray T-shirt, saturation pulled to the show level on the vectorscope. Look applied. Camera B grade starts from the Camera A primary preset. Adjust offset (gain) up by the half-stop deficit; reduce the warm bias by shifting the white-balance control toward cyan-blue until the parade matches Camera A on the subject. Verify on a static cut between the two cameras — if the subject's forehead skin tone reads at the same vectorscope position and same waveform height, the match is landed. Apply the show look on top of both, unchanged.

**Exterior shot with overexposed sky and well-exposed subject.** Input transform applied. Primary: pull exposure to land the subject correctly (subject is the priority); contrast set, white balance on the subject; saturation per the show. The sky now blows out on the waveform. Add a secondary node with a luma qualifier keying the brightest 20% of the image. Pull the gain on the keyed region down until the sky's blue and the cloud structure return; soften the qualifier so the transition into the sky is not visible. Add a power window if the qualifier picks up subject highlights — restrict the qualifier to the top of frame with a soft horizon-line window. Apply the look on top. Confirm the sky stays under broadcast peak; confirm the subject is unaffected by the secondary.

**Match a hero film-emulation reference frame for a flashback insert.** Reference frame has lifted blacks, warm midtones, desaturated cyan in the shadows, slightly compressed highlights. Apply the input transform. Primary: exposure to land the subject; contrast set lightly (the look will compress contrast further); white balance neutral; saturation neutral. Move to the look node: apply the show film-emulation preset or build a stack — lift the blacks via the offset shadow wheel, warm the midtones via the midtone wheel, desaturate selectively with an HSL qualifier on the cyan-blue range restricted to the lower luminance band, compress the highlights via the highlight wheel or a curve. Check on the vectorscope that the skin tones still sit on the skin-tone line despite the warm cast — if not, the look is overcorrecting and needs intensity reduction. Compare the reference and the shot on a side-by-side wipe; adjust the look intensity, not its identity.

**Mixed-light interior with a window blowing out behind the subject.** The window reads as clipped white on the waveform, the subject is acceptable but slightly cool from the daylight spill. Apply input transform. Primary: expose the subject correctly (priority), set contrast, warm the white balance modestly (the room is tungsten-dominant where the subject sits; the window is daylight and should remain visibly cooler), set saturation. The window is still clipped. Add a secondary luma qualifier on the brightest 15% — pull gain down and slightly cool to recover any window structure that survives in the camera raw. If nothing is there to recover (the camera clipped the file, not the grade), add a power window on the window aperture only and use it to add a tiny vignette tone that softens the boundary between the clipped window and the surrounding wall, so the eye reads the window as a window rather than a hole. Apply the show look on top.

**Day-for-night conversion.** Source shot is exposed for day exteriors; the brief is a moonlight night look. Input transform. Primary: pull exposure down significantly (offset, not gamma); set contrast with deep crush in shadows (this is intentional, unlike everyday grading); set white balance very cool (deep blue cast, moonlight is perceptually blue); reduce saturation moderately because perception of color drops at low light. Move to secondaries: a power window on practical highlights (lamps, fires, lit windows) that are warmed and brought up a touch above the surrounding moonlight to read as the only light sources. A qualifier on skin to lift its luminance just enough to retain face readability without breaking the night illusion. Apply a soft vignette as part of the look. Compare against a reference moonlight shot from a film known for night photography; do not try to make day-for-night convincing without a reference.

## Limitations

- This skill assumes a calibrated reference display. On an uncalibrated display, the colorist's eye is unreliable and only the scopes can be trusted; this skill flags that constraint but cannot substitute calibration.
- It does not cover the creative authoring of an original show look from scratch — that is a separate creative-direction exercise. It covers applying a defined look to a shot.
- Skin-tone references on the vectorscope are a guide, not a rule. Real skin varies widely; the colorist must judge the subject's actual skin appearance against the established look for the show, not chase a universal "skin line" position.
- This skill does not cover beauty work (digital skin retouching, blemish removal). Those are compositing tasks performed before or after color grading, depending on pipeline.
- The order of operations described here (exposure first, then contrast, then white balance, then saturation) is the recommended starting point. Experienced colorists may legitimately depart from it when a specific shot's problems call for it — for instance, a heavily white-balance-shifted shot may need a coarse white-balance pass before exposure can be judged. The order is a default, not a law.
- Stylized work (heavy color casts, extreme contrast, monochrome treatments) bends every rule in this skill. The framework still applies but its tolerances widen — scope ranges are interpreted relative to the intentional look, not against generic broadcast-legal targets.
