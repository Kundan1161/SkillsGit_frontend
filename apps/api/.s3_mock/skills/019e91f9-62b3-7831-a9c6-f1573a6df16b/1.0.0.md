## When to use

Invoke this skill any time a dashboard needs a quality judgment, not a redesign. Typical situations:

- A dashboard is being submitted for certification under a self-service BI governance model and a steward must accept, reject, or send it back with feedback.
- A dashboard has just been inherited from a departing employee and the new owner needs to know what to fix before relying on it.
- The platform team is auditing the top-viewed dashboards each quarter and needs a consistent rubric.
- A reader has complained — numbers seem wrong, page is slow, they don't trust it — and someone needs to triage the complaint into a specific list of issues.
- A dashboard is being prepared for a recurring executive audience and the author wants a pre-flight review.

Do not invoke this skill to design a new dashboard from scratch (use the dashboard-architect skill). Do not invoke it to debug a specific failing chart (use a chart-debugging or query-tuning skill). Do not invoke it for embedded analytics in a customer-facing product (use the embedded-analytics-architect skill).

## How to apply

The methodology evaluates a dashboard along seven independent axes. A dashboard can be beautiful and wrong, or correct and unreadable. Each axis gets a separate judgment; an overall recommendation is a function of all seven, with correctness and ownership weighted highest because they are unrecoverable from the reader's side.

The seven axes:

1. Correctness — does the dashboard answer the intended question with numbers that match the source of truth?
2. Freshness — is the data new enough for the decision and is the staleness visible to the reader?
3. Ownership — is there a named, reachable human responsible for it, and can they actually maintain it?
4. Performance — does it load fast enough for the way it is consumed?
5. Clarity — can the intended reader interpret it without a phone call?
6. Audience fit — is it the right shape for the intended decision and cadence?
7. Distribution channel — is the way readers reach it the right channel for the audience and urgency?

Apply each axis in turn; do not let a strong impression on one axis color the others.

### 1. Correctness

1. **Trace each metric back to its source.** A metric without a traceable source is, by default, wrong until proven otherwise. The trace should end at a governed table, a modeling-layer model, or a documented source — not at a hand-written SQL block hidden inside the chart.
2. **Confirm one definition per metric.** If two panels show "revenue," they must use the same definition. Compare the underlying queries. Subtle differences (whether refunds are netted, whether deferred revenue is included, which time zone bounds the period) cause silent disagreements and destroy trust.
3. **Reconcile a sample period against a trusted source.** Pick a closed period (last full month) and compare the dashboard's number to the system-of-record, the closed financials, or another certified dashboard. If they disagree by more than rounding, find out why before doing anything else.
4. **Check the dimensions, not just the measures.** A correct revenue total can hide an incorrect by-region breakout if a region mapping is stale or a join drops nulls.
5. **Watch for filter traps.** A default filter that quietly excludes a segment ("active customers only," "exclude internal accounts") changes the answer. The filter must be visible and intentional.
6. **Watch for time-zone traps.** A dashboard that shows "today" in a different time zone than the audience is wrong half the time. Confirm the time-zone choice and document it.
7. **Watch for late-arriving data.** Today's numbers for a partially-loaded day look low because the day is incomplete. Either exclude today, mark it as in-progress, or document the convention.
8. **Re-run the underlying queries directly when in doubt.** A chart can lie if the visualization library aggregates differently than expected; pulling the rows directly is the tiebreaker.

### 2. Freshness

9. **Identify the slowest source.** A dashboard is only as fresh as its oldest panel. If one panel is nightly and the rest are hourly, the dashboard is nightly.
10. **Match freshness to the decision cadence.** An executive who reads on Mondays does not need minute-fresh data; an on-call engineer does. A mismatch in either direction is a defect — over-fresh data is expensive and noisy; under-fresh data is wrong for the use.
11. **Confirm the "as of" indicator is visible.** A reader who cannot tell when the data was last refreshed cannot trust the numbers.
12. **Distinguish refresh from load.** A scheduled refresh failed silently three days ago — the chart still renders, the timestamp is stale. The reader sees a chart they think is current. Confirm the refresh actually ran.
13. **Check for staleness alarms.** Is anyone notified when freshness exceeds the documented threshold? If not, recommend adding one; silent staleness is the most common form of wrong.
14. **Distinguish per-panel cadence from dashboard-level cadence.** A panel that updates faster than its peers should be labeled; otherwise the reader assumes consistency that doesn't exist.

### 3. Ownership

15. **Find a named human.** A team owner ("the analytics team") is not an owner; over time it means no one. A single accountable individual, with a documented backup, is the requirement.
16. **Verify the owner is still in role.** People change teams. An owner who is no longer in the relevant team cannot reasonably maintain the dashboard.
17. **Check the description.** A blank description means the dashboard relies entirely on the original author's head, which is now unavailable. Require a populated description: who this is for, what decision it supports, sources, caveats.
18. **Check the change history.** A dashboard that has not been touched in a year is either perfect or abandoned. Inspect for staleness signs (renamed source tables, deprecated columns, broken filter defaults).
19. **Check the dependency on the owner's specific skills.** A dashboard whose underlying queries only the original author can read is a maintenance trap. Push the logic into the modeling layer where the team can read it.
20. **Check the certification metadata.** If the platform supports a certification flag, confirm the flag matches reality. Certified-looking content that no one actually stands behind is worse than uncertified content.

### 4. Performance

21. **Measure first-paint time on a representative session.** A cold load (no cache) and a warm load (returning user). The cold load is the worst case the reader will see.
22. **Compare to the audience's tolerance.** Executive audiences tolerate 3–5 seconds; operational audiences during incidents tolerate sub-second. The number is contextual.
23. **Identify the bottleneck panel.** Usually one or two panels account for most of the load time. Targeted fixes on those panels are the highest-leverage performance work.
24. **Check for query patterns that scale poorly.** Joins on un-indexed columns, full-table scans on raw event tables, aggregations over "all time" with no filter cap. These get worse as data grows.
25. **Check for caching.** Are the top-of-canvas headline metrics served from a pre-aggregated source, or are they recomputed every load? Recomputing is the cheap-to-build expensive-to-run choice.
26. **Check the default time range.** A default of "all time" on a billion-row table will time out eventually. Tighten the default; the reader can widen it on demand.
27. **Lazy-load below the fold.** Panels the reader scrolls to should not block first paint.
28. **Watch for the N-tab pattern.** A dashboard split across many tabs to "improve performance" often just hides the problem. The first tab is fast because the others are unloaded; the user pays the cost when they click through.

### 5. Clarity

29. **Read the dashboard cold, as if a new reader.** What is the headline finding? Can it be stated in one sentence? If not, the dashboard is a data dump.
30. **Check the titles.** Panel titles should describe the question answered, not the metric name. "How is revenue trending vs target?" is more useful than "Revenue".
31. **Check the chart choices.** A pie chart with twelve slices is unreadable; a bar chart with two bars is overkill; a single-number panel for a metric that fluctuates wildly hides volatility. Mismatched chart-to-data is a clarity defect.
32. **Check the color palette.** Color used decoratively (every series a different shade) competes with color used for meaning (red for "bad"). Reserve color for meaning.
33. **Check the comparison.** Every key metric should be paired with its comparison (vs target, vs last period, vs last year). A naked number is hard to interpret.
34. **Check the annotations.** A meaningful event in the data (a launch, a pricing change, an outage) should be annotated on the chart. Without it, the reader is left to discover and rediscover anomalies on each visit.
35. **Check the units, the locale, and the rounding.** Inconsistent rounding (12.3% on one panel, 12.34% on another), inconsistent currency display, inconsistent date format — each one is a small defect; collectively they corrode trust.
36. **Check the empty and error states.** A panel that renders a blank rectangle when its query fails is a clarity defect masquerading as a quietly broken chart.

### 6. Audience fit

37. **Confirm the dashboard's shape matches the consumption mode.** A 30-panel scrolling dashboard is wrong for an audience who skims on Monday morning. A single-number summary is wrong for an analyst tracing a root cause.
38. **Confirm the panel order matches the reader's priorities.** The first panel must answer the most important question. If the first panel answers a secondary question, the dashboard is misordered.
39. **Confirm the level of detail matches the reader's literacy.** A chart that requires explaining "this is a 7-day rolling mean smoothed with a forward gap fill" to an executive is the wrong chart for the audience. Move the technique into the source, present a clean line.
40. **Confirm the cadence matches.** A live-updating panel on an executive dashboard is a distraction. A daily-refreshed panel on an incident-response dashboard is useless. Match refresh to consumption.
41. **Confirm the dashboard does not bury the action.** If the reader is meant to act, the action prompt must be obvious. "These five accounts crossed the risk threshold this week" is an action prompt; a heatmap of risk scores is not.
42. **Confirm the dashboard is not actually two dashboards.** A common defect: one half serves executives, the other half serves analysts. Split it; one dashboard, one audience.

### 7. Distribution channel

43. **Ask how readers find the dashboard today.** Bookmarked link, portal link, scheduled email, Slack post, embedded panel. Each has a different cost and reach.
44. **Match the channel to the cadence.** A weekly executive view is well served by a scheduled email — readers don't have to remember to look. A real-time operations view is well served by a portal pinned in the team's tools. Mismatches waste the work that went into the dashboard.
45. **Check whether the dashboard is reachable from the place the decision happens.** An on-call engineer in an alerting channel benefits from a deep link in the alert. A pricing analyst in a quote tool benefits from an embedded panel. A dashboard the reader has to remember to open is a dashboard most readers don't open.
46. **Check for scheduled-delivery defects.** Scheduled emails of charts: do they render? Do they include the right time range? Are they sent at the right time? A broken send is a broken dashboard.
47. **Watch for "the dashboard nobody opens" pattern.** Usage analytics is the truth: a dashboard with effort invested and no audience is the wrong dashboard, the wrong channel, or both.
48. **Recommend collapsing into a digest when many dashboards serve the same audience.** A weekly executive who currently opens eight dashboards is better served by one curated digest with the headline numbers and links to the eight for drill-in.

### 8. Score the axes

49. **Score each axis on a four-point scale.** Pass, Pass with minor issues, Fail (must fix before certification or production use), or Not applicable. Resist a five-point scale; the middle becomes a hiding place.
50. **Weight correctness and ownership.** A Fail on either is a Fail overall, regardless of the other scores. The other axes can be merely "Pass with minor issues" without blocking certification, but a chronic accumulation of minor issues across axes is itself a fail signal.
51. **Justify each score in one or two sentences.** A score without justification is unactionable.

### 9. Build the punch list

52. **Sort issues by severity, not by axis.** A reader of the review wants to know what to fix first, not which axis it came from.
53. **For each issue, name the fix.** "Add an owner" is not a fix; "Assign Pat Lee as primary owner; document Sam Cho as backup" is.
54. **For each fix, estimate effort.** Five-minute fix, half-day, multi-week. Distinguish so the owner can plan.
55. **Distinguish blockers from polish.** A blocker prevents certification or recommended use; polish is everything else.
56. **Limit to twelve issues.** Beyond twelve, the punch list is unusable. If more exist, the dashboard probably needs redesign, not review.

### 10. Make the recommendation

57. **One of four outcomes.** Certify now. Certify after listed fixes. Keep as working content (not certified). Deprecate (the dashboard should not exist in its current form).
58. **State the reasoning in one paragraph.** Why this recommendation and not the others.
59. **Suggest the next review date.** Certified content goes on the quarterly cycle; working content can wait. Without a next date, "review later" never happens.

### 11. Self-check before responding

60. **Confirm each axis got an explicit judgment.** No axis was silently skipped.
61. **Confirm correctness was tested against a source of truth.** A subjective "looks right" is not enough.
62. **Confirm the recommended fixes are concrete.** A reviewer who cannot say what to fix has not finished the review.
63. **Confirm the punch list is sorted by severity and is under twelve items.**
64. **Confirm the recommendation matches the scorecard.** A dashboard with two Fails should not be recommended for certification.

## Inputs

- Dashboard description (panels, sources, relationships).
- Intended audience and decision intent.
- Optional: provenance detail, distribution channel today, review intent.

## Outputs

- Rubric scorecard across seven axes with brief justifications.
- Prioritized punch list of issues with fixes and effort estimates.
- Certification recommendation with reasoning.
- Distribution-channel recommendation.

## Examples

**Example invocation**

> "Review our weekly Sales Pipeline dashboard before we mark it certified. Six panels: committed pipeline (big number), pipeline waterfall (created/won/lost/slipped/pushed), at-risk deals table, win-rate by stage by region (small multiples), rep activity heatmap, coverage ratio trend. Sources: opportunities table (warehouse, hourly), win-rate table (warehouse, nightly), rep activity (streamed, 5-min). Audience: VPs of Sales. Decision: which deals to push or slip on Monday morning. Currently linked from a Slack post each Monday at 8 AM."

**Expected high-level output**

Scorecard:
- Correctness: Pass with minor issues. Revenue trace clean to warehouse; one panel (coverage ratio) uses a slightly different "open pipeline" definition than the other panels — flag for steward to reconcile.
- Freshness: Pass. Per-panel freshness labels visible. The 5-minute rep-activity panel is overkill for a weekly cadence; consider downsampling.
- Ownership: Pass with minor issues. Named owner present but no documented backup. Add backup before certification.
- Performance: Pass. Cold first-paint ~2.5s; warm sub-second. Acceptable for the audience.
- Clarity: Pass with minor issues. The waterfall panel's negative bars use the same color as the at-risk-deals red highlight; reserve red for action. Rep-activity heatmap legend hidden behind a tooltip; surface it.
- Audience fit: Pass. Six panels match the weekly skim. Decision prompt (at-risk-deals table) is on the canvas, not buried.
- Distribution channel: Pass with minor issues. Slack post + link works, but consider a scheduled email digest with the top-line numbers inline for VPs who don't open Slack on Monday morning.

Recommendation: Certify after listed minor fixes (definition reconciliation, owner backup, color reservation, legend surfacing). Schedule next review for the next quarterly cycle.

## Limitations

- The methodology produces a judgment, not a redesign. If the dashboard needs to be reshaped, send the user to the dashboard-architect skill after this review.
- The correctness axis depends on the reviewer being able to trace queries to a source. Without access to the underlying definitions, the review can only flag suspicions, not confirm correctness.
- Performance scores are approximate without telemetry; the rubric uses thresholds that match common audience tolerances.
- The distribution-channel axis depends on knowing how readers consume today. Without that input, the recommendation is generic.
- The skill is calibrated for internal self-service dashboards. Customer-facing embedded analytics has additional axes (multi-tenant isolation, SLA, branding) that the embedded-analytics-architect skill covers.
