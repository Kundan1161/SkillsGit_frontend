## When to use

Invoke this skill when a founder, CFO, or chief of staff needs to draft a board update or investor update for a recurring cadence — monthly for early-stage companies, quarterly for later-stage ones. The skill is built for the moment when the leader has the raw material in their head, in a spreadsheet, and in a few Slack threads, but has not yet sat down to write the document. It produces a draft that captures what the leader would have written given two hours, in the structure the board is used to reading.

Use this skill when the user can supply the headline metrics, the wins, the misses, and ideally the asks. The skill is most accurate when the user is willing to share the lowlights candidly — a board update that hides the misses serves no one and damages trust over time. The skill works best when the user knows whether this update is monthly or quarterly, because the format differs (monthly updates are shorter and more operational; quarterly updates are longer and more strategic).

Do not use this skill to spin a bad period into a good one. Boards read updates carefully across periods and notice when the narrative shifts to hide reality; the skill flags such patterns rather than producing them. Do not use it as a substitute for the founder's voice — the best board updates sound like the founder, and the skill produces a draft the founder edits, not a finished document the founder signs blindly. Do not use it to make commitments the leadership team has not actually agreed to; commitments in a board document become accountability the board will revisit.

## How to apply

A good board update does four jobs. It tells the board what happened in the period in numbers they can scan. It tells the board the operating story behind the numbers — what is working, what is not, and why. It tells the board what is coming — both what the leader expects and what could surprise. And it tells the board what the leader needs from them — introductions, decisions, hires, attention, presence.

A board update is not a press release; the audience is sophisticated, informed, and frequently right. It is also not an internal operating doc; the board does not need line-item detail and will not read it. The right register is between those two: candid, specific, structured, and short enough that every board member actually reads it.

### Step 1 — Open with the TL;DR

The first thing the board reads is the TL;DR. Three lines, at most. The headline result, the most important thing to know, and the single biggest ask.

Example: "April was a strong month for new logos, a weak month for retention. ARR up to $14.6M (+$420K net), but enterprise GRR slipped to 91% trailing twelve. Cash $14.2M, runway 16 months. The biggest ask: two intros for an enterprise CRO search."

The TL;DR is what board members read in the elevator before the meeting. If they read nothing else, they should know the headline, the issue, and the ask.

### Step 2 — Lead with the headline metrics

Immediately under the TL;DR, present the headline metrics in a compact table. For a SaaS business, this is typically: ARR, ARR growth (month-over-month and trailing-twelve-month), net retention, new logos, cash, runway. For other business models, adapt — a marketplace adds GMV and take rate, a transactional business adds order volume and ARPU, a services business adds bookings and utilization.

The table should fit in the reader's eye in one glance — three columns at most (current period, prior period, plan), six to eight rows. Avoid the temptation to include every metric the company tracks internally. The board reads the table to anchor on the period; the operating detail comes in the narrative that follows.

Use plain numbers, plain units, and consistent definitions. The board reads many updates; consistency from period to period matters more than nuance within a period.

### Step 3 — Write the highlights section

The highlights section is the operating story of what worked in the period. Three to five items is the right number; fewer reads as a slow month, more reads as desperate self-congratulation. Each highlight should:

- Be specific. "Launched the new pricing tier" is better than "made progress on monetization."
- Be tied to a metric. "Launched the new pricing tier, which converted 14 of the first 90 prospects in the small-business segment at the new mid-tier price" is better than "Launched the new pricing tier."
- Be honest about contribution. If a highlight was the result of one heroic effort by a small team, name it; if it was a product release the team has been working on for nine months, acknowledge the timeline. Boards appreciate the operating texture.

Avoid puffery. Phrases like "industry-leading," "transformational," "best-in-class" do not belong in a board update. The board reads them as low-confidence padding.

### Step 4 — Write the lowlights section

The lowlights section is the most-read section of a good board update. The board has seen a hundred updates and knows that every period has misses; an update that has none is a credibility hit, not a credibility boost.

Two to four lowlights is the typical right count. Each lowlight should:

- Be specific. "Enterprise retention slipped to 91% trailing twelve" is better than "Some retention pressure in the enterprise segment."
- Acknowledge what the leader does not yet know. "We have one consistent root cause across three of the four cancellations; the fourth has a different driver we are still investigating" is better than "Working on it."
- State what is being done. The board wants to know what the operating response is, not just that there is a problem.
- Avoid blame externalization. "Macroeconomic softness affecting enterprise budgets" reads as deflection when it is not specific; "Two of our four lost enterprise deals were in companies that announced layoffs in February; the other two we lost on integration concerns we are investigating" reads as analysis.

Pre-disclose surprises. If something happened in the period that a board member will hear about elsewhere — a key departure, a major customer loss, a legal matter, a near-miss on an outage — the board update should be where they hear about it first. Hiding a known issue and having it surface independently is one of the fastest ways to lose board trust.

### Step 5 — Write the strategic narrative (quarterly only)

Quarterly updates have a section monthly updates do not: the strategic narrative. This is the half-page where the leader stands back from the period and explains the company's progress against its strategic direction. The right structure is usually:

- One paragraph on what the company is trying to do over the next 12-to-18 months, restated for the board's recall.
- One paragraph on what the quarter showed about whether that direction is working — both positive and negative evidence.
- One paragraph on what is changing in the strategy as a result of the quarter's learning, if anything is changing. (If nothing is changing, say so; "no change in direction" is itself information.)

The strategic narrative is what the board chairs the meeting around. It is the section that should reward most time in drafting.

### Step 6 — Write the financials and capital section

A clean board update has a short financials block that covers, at minimum:

- Revenue (or ARR), this period and trailing.
- Gross margin.
- Operating loss or income.
- Cash balance at period end.
- Burn for the period.
- Runway, in months, against the trailing-three-month burn.
- Forecast for the next two periods on the same metrics, with confidence noted.

Then the capital position: status of any upcoming financing (preparation, conversations in progress, term sheet, etc.), status of any debt facility or covenants, any material change in cap table (option grants, secondaries, repurchases, hires with cap-table impact).

Boards care about cash and capital more than any other line. A financial section that omits or underweights this is a flag.

### Step 7 — Write the asks

The asks section is where the leader gets the most concrete value from the board. Boards offer most when asked specifically; vague asks ("any thoughts welcome") produce vague help.

Specific asks look like:

- "Two intros to enterprise CROs in B2B SaaS, ideally with a vertical-software background." (To: full board.)
- "Sign-off on the $1.2M equipment lease, requested under the board approval threshold." (To: board chair.)
- "Twenty minutes of [board member]'s time on the EU expansion question — specifically on the entity setup tradeoffs." (To: named member.)
- "A read of the attached partnership term sheet from [Big Co], with a view on whether the carve-outs are standard." (To: named member.)

Two to four asks is the typical right number. More than four risks dilution; fewer than two risks under-using the board.

### Step 8 — Add a "what to watch" section (optional)

Some updates benefit from a brief forward-looking section: what the leader is watching, what could break or break through next period, what the board might hear about next period that they should be primed for.

This is the section that lets the leader pre-disclose without overcommitting: "We are watching the renewal of [Big Customer] which is up in May. The renewal is currently tracking, but the conversation has been slower than usual. We will know by mid-May." The board would rather hear this in May than be surprised in June.

### Step 9 — Tune for monthly vs quarterly cadence

Monthly updates should be short. 600 to 900 words. The structure is tighter: TL;DR, metrics, highlights, lowlights, asks, financials/capital. The strategic narrative is absent or compressed into a single line. The reader should be done in under ten minutes.

Quarterly updates can be longer. 1,500 to 2,500 words. The strategic narrative gets a full section. Each highlight and lowlight may have a paragraph of context rather than a bullet point. The financials block is more complete and may include a small variance-vs-plan view. The reader spends fifteen to twenty minutes.

Anything longer than 2,500 words is too long. The board will read the first half and skim the rest; important things move to the unread half. If the update wants to be longer, the right answer is usually an attached operating-review document or a deck for the meeting itself.

### Step 10 — Tune for tone

Match the tone preference. "Crisp and numeric" is short, table-heavy, light on narrative. "Narrative and warm" tells the story in prose with metrics as supporting evidence. "Balanced" is the most common — narrative for the highlights and lowlights, tables for the metrics and financials, crispness everywhere else.

Across all tones, certain conventions hold. The first-person plural ("we") is the standard voice. Pronouns referring to the board are second-person plural ("you"); they are not "the board" in third person. Avoid jargon the board will not parse without a glossary; if a metric needs explaining, define it on first use and not after.

### Step 11 — Surface red flags before sending

Some patterns in a draft are worth surfacing before the update goes out. Watch for:

- A highlights section that is much longer than the lowlights section in a period where the financial numbers indicate strain.
- A lowlights section that mentions a problem without saying what is being done about it.
- Asks that have appeared in three consecutive updates without resolution (suggesting the board did not act, or did not understand the ask, or the company stopped pursuing it).
- A cash and runway section that omits the runway number.
- Forward forecasts that vary materially from the prior update without explanation of why the forecast changed.
- Any line that reads as spin — uses adjectives where numbers would do, externalizes blame, or uses jargon to obscure detail.

Surface these in the `red_flag_callouts` output for the user to review, not as a hard refusal. The user decides; the skill makes the issues visible.

### Step 12 — End with a closing line, not a closing flourish

Board updates do not need a peroration. A clean closing — "Thanks for the support; happy to dig deeper on any of the above in next week's meeting." — is enough. Avoid expressions of unbounded confidence ("More to come as we continue to win"); they read as performance.

### Step 13 — Sign off appropriately

The update is signed by the CEO (or, for a quarterly CFO-led update, by the CFO). The signature line is the founder's name; no title or boilerplate. The footer can carry the company name, the date of the update, and a short legend on metric definitions if the metrics block uses any non-standard ones.

### Step 14 — Note distribution

The skill should remind the user to note the distribution list at the top or bottom. Some asks are to the full board, some to named members; some content is for board only, some can be forwarded to advisors or limited partners. Marking the distribution intent in the draft prevents misrouting.

### Step 15 — Refuse to fabricate

Where the user has not supplied a number, do not invent one. Mark the line "[NEEDS METRIC]" and continue. Where the user has not supplied an ask, do not invent one; ask the user, or leave the section short. A board update is a document of record; fabricated content becomes accountability the leader did not actually agree to.

## Inputs

- **period** (required) — the period the update covers.
- **headline_metrics** (required) — the key business metrics for the period.
- **highlights** (required) — the wins of the period.
- **lowlights** (required) — the misses of the period.
- **asks** (optional but strongly recommended) — what the company needs from the board.
- **cash_position** (optional) — cash balance, burn, runway, financing status.
- **update_type** (optional) — monthly or quarterly.
- **tone_preference** (optional) — crisp-and-numeric, narrative-and-warm, balanced.

## Outputs

- A complete board or investor update ready for founder review and final edit.
- A three-line TL;DR for top-of-document use.
- A clean ask list with each ask named to a person or to the full board.
- A list of red flags the draft surfaced for the user to review before sending.

## Limitations

This skill produces a draft. It does not produce a finished document the leader should send without reading. The founder's voice, the founder's judgment about what to emphasize, and the founder's knowledge of board-member preferences are inputs the skill cannot capture from the structured fields.

The skill assumes good faith inputs. If the user supplies misleading metrics or omits material lowlights, the draft inherits those choices. The skill flags inconsistencies it can detect but cannot independently verify the numbers. Board updates are documents of record; verification of the underlying numbers is the leader's responsibility.

Boards differ. Early-stage seed boards read differently from late-stage growth boards. Family-office boards read differently from venture boards. The skill produces a generally well-formed update; tuning to a specific board's preferences (some boards want more detail, some less; some want more forward-looking commentary, some less) is best done by the founder editing the draft.

Legal and regulatory sensitivities differ by jurisdiction and stage. Material information that should be disclosed in a regulated way (for publicly-traded companies, for companies in registered offerings) is outside the skill's scope. Board updates that touch on litigation, regulatory matters, or material transactions should be reviewed by counsel before distribution.

The skill cannot replace the verbal conversation. Some content belongs in a meeting, not a written update — sensitive personnel matters, sensitive customer matters, sensitive financing conversations. The skill flags content of this kind for the user's review rather than producing it written.
