## When to use

Reach for this skill when an analyst, portfolio manager, or research lead needs to design a repeatable workflow for moving from an idea to a defensible investment recommendation. The skill is built for the design problem — the bones of the process, the templates, the gates — rather than for executing a single one-off pitch. Use it when standing up coverage on a new sector, onboarding a new analyst, refreshing an aging research process, or building a workflow that can be run consistently across many names rather than re-invented each time.

This skill is appropriate when the user wants more than a checklist. A workflow that produces good research has named inputs at each stage, owners who hold the work, time budgets that prevent the work from sliding, and exit criteria that decide whether the idea moves forward or gets killed. The skill is calibrated to encode those four elements at every stage.

Do not use this skill for tactical trade execution, options structuring, derivative-specific analysis, or quantitative factor research (a separate skill handles factor-research pipelines). Do not use it as a substitute for the analyst's judgment — the skill produces the scaffolding, not the views. Outputs are educational scaffolding for research process design and are explicitly NOT investment advice; nothing produced by this skill should be acted on in the market without independent review by a licensed professional and a compliance check appropriate to the user's jurisdiction.

## How to apply

A good research workflow is a sequence of stages where each stage takes named inputs, produces a named artifact, and has a clear exit criterion. Most teams collapse this into a single phase of "do research" followed by a memo — and end up with memos that are uneven, unfalsifiable, and difficult to compare against each other. The discipline of the workflow is to keep each stage compact, to enforce the gates between stages, and to leave a paper trail so the work can be audited and improved.

The skill walks through nine stages. The number and the boundaries are negotiable; what matters is that the stages have named outputs and explicit hand-offs.

### Stage 1 — Frame the research objective

The workflow begins by writing down what the research is for. The choice among long thesis, short thesis, pair trade, watchlist trim, and sector overview is not cosmetic. Each objective has a different bar for the kind of evidence that finishes the work and the kind of risk that kills it.

- A **long thesis** requires identifying a divergence between the market's view and a defensible analytical view that resolves in the holding period. The bar is the named catalysts and the named falsifiers.
- A **short thesis** requires the long-thesis bar plus a clear risk model for the cost of being early. Borrow availability, locate cost, and squeeze risk become first-class concerns.
- A **pair trade** requires that the long and short legs share named common factors so the pair isolates the differential view. The trap is constructing pairs where the common factors are unhedged and the pair becomes a directional bet in disguise.
- A **watchlist trim** is the workflow applied to a coverage list with the question "which of these merit the next layer of work" rather than "which should we own." The output is a shorter list, not a portfolio.
- A **sector overview** sets the frame the individual-name workflows run inside of. It looks at the demand structure, the supply structure, the competitive shape, and the regulatory posture before any single name gets researched.

Write the objective in two sentences. Pin it at the top of every later artifact.

### Stage 2 — Inventory the data sources

A workflow is only as good as the data that flows through it. Inventory what is available, what is reliable, and what has known gaps:

- **Primary filings** — annual reports, quarterly filings, proxy statements, registration documents. These are the audited spine and are the highest-confidence source for accounting facts.
- **Investor relations material** — earnings releases, presentations, transcripts, fact books, supplemental disclosures. Useful and well-organized but framed by the company; treat it as a source whose tone has been managed.
- **Sell-side and consensus** — analyst models, consensus estimates, target prices. Useful for knowing what the market thinks; not useful as independent confirmation of any view.
- **Industry data** — trade associations, regulatory data, government statistics. Often the best place to validate or falsify management claims about market share or end-market trends.
- **Channel checks** — primary research with customers, suppliers, distributors, former employees. The highest-information-per-hour source if done responsibly; subject to bias and compliance constraints.
- **Alternative data** — credit card transactions, web traffic, app downloads, satellite imagery, hiring signals, supply chain shipments. Powerful when validated against fundamentals; misleading when used as the sole source. (A separate skill covers alternative-data evaluation in depth.)

For each source, note the latency (how fresh is the data), the cost (subscription or analyst time), and the known biases. Map sources to specific stages of the workflow rather than scraping all of them at every stage.

### Stage 3 — Hypothesis generation

Most analysts under-invest in this stage and over-invest in the work that follows. A well-scoped hypothesis is one or two sentences that name (a) the variable the analyst thinks the market is mispricing, (b) the direction of the mispricing, and (c) the mechanism by which the mispricing resolves.

Useful hypothesis shapes:

- "Margin compression is a transient consequence of input cost rather than a structural change; gross margin returns to the prior range over the next four quarters as the cost dynamic normalizes."
- "Reported unit growth understates pricing-adjusted volume because the mix is shifting to a lower-priced product without the segment disclosure to make that visible; the underlying franchise is healthier than headline trends suggest."
- "The acquisition integration is more complex than management has communicated and synergies will arrive 18–24 months later than guided; consensus is over-modeling the back half of next year."

Disqualifying hypothesis shapes:

- "The company is cheap" — without a named mechanism by which the price catches up.
- "Management is great" — without a named decision the great management is about to take.
- "The story is broken" — without a named driver that will continue to deteriorate over the holding period.

The hypothesis should be falsifiable. Write down what would have to be true in the world over the next six to eighteen months for the hypothesis to be wrong. That set of falsifiers becomes the spine of the risk register in Stage 8.

### Stage 4 — Fundamental layer

The fundamental layer is where the team does the accounting, builds the model, and stress-tests the named drivers. Time-box this stage; teams that let fundamentals consume the entire workflow neglect the comparative, scenario, and risk layers and produce one-dimensional memos.

Within the fundamental layer, work in this order:

- **History first** — pull five-to-ten years of statements and reconstruct revenue, gross margin, opex, working capital, capex, and free cash flow. Look for trends, level shifts, and accounting changes. Note where the company restated or changed segment disclosure.
- **Unit economics** — express the business at the smallest unit that is informative. Per-store, per-customer, per-rig, per-kilowatt, per-subscriber. The unit-economics view is often where the divergence between management's narrative and the underlying franchise becomes visible.
- **Quality of earnings** — reconcile reported earnings to free cash flow. Persistent gaps suggest aggressive accounting; chronic and unexplained gaps are the strongest single signal for a short thesis.
- **Capital allocation history** — what has management done with cash over the last five years. Acquisitions and their returns, buybacks at what prices, dividends, debt paydown, capex. Past behavior is the strongest signal of future behavior.
- **Forward model** — build a three-statement model out to the horizon set by the research objective. Forward assumptions should be tied to the named drivers in the hypothesis. Each driver should be challengeable in one cell.

The fundamental model is a working artifact, not a deliverable. The deliverable is the set of three or four numbers that fall out of the model that are different from consensus, and the mechanism that explains the difference.

### Stage 5 — Technical and flow layer

Most research workflows neglect this layer or over-rely on it. The skill recommends a light touch — a few signals that inform timing and positioning rather than drive the thesis.

- **Trend and momentum** — where is the stock relative to its long-term moving average and recent range. A long thesis bought at the bottom of a multi-year downtrend has different timing risk than the same thesis bought at the top of an extended run.
- **Volatility regime** — is implied volatility elevated or compressed relative to history. Affects sizing for any position with a defined exit horizon and may affect whether to express the view in cash equity, options, or a basket.
- **Ownership and flow** — short interest, days to cover, institutional concentration, recent insider activity. Crowded names carry squeeze risk on shorts and abandonment risk on longs.
- **Event calendar** — earnings dates, investor days, regulatory hearings, scheduled supply or demand events. The calendar shapes the holding period and the likely catalyst path.

This layer should be one or two pages in the memo, not the spine. The discipline is to use it for timing and sizing, not for the underlying view.

### Stage 6 — Peer comparable analysis

Peer comp is the most-misused stage in equity research. Done well, it triangulates the view by forcing the analyst to confront the cross-section. Done badly, it produces a table of multiples whose only function is to make the recommendation look quantitatively grounded.

Principles for useful peer comp:

- **Curate the peer set deliberately.** The peer set should reflect the business model and the investor base, not just the industry classification. Two companies that share an industry code but have different end markets, different mixes, or different capital structures are not actually comparable. Three to seven peers is usually the right number; ten is too many to discuss substantively.
- **Choose metrics that reflect the value creation pattern.** A capital-light recurring-revenue business is best compared on growth-adjusted margin and capital efficiency, not on a price-to-book multiple. A heavy capex industrial is best compared on returns on invested capital and free-cash-flow conversion, not on revenue multiples. Match the metric to the model.
- **Normalize before comparing.** Adjust for one-time items, accounting choices, leverage differences, and fiscal-year-end differences. A peer comp on unadjusted reported metrics is comparing apples to fruits that may or may not be apples.
- **Show the cross-section, not just the average.** The peer set's average and median matter less than the position of the target within the cross-section, and the reasons it sits where it sits.

The output of peer comp is two paragraphs: where the target sits in the cross-section on growth, returns, and capital efficiency, and why. The multiples table is an appendix to the paragraphs, not a substitute for them.

### Stage 7 — Scenario analysis

Build three to five scenarios that span the realistic range of outcomes over the horizon. Each scenario should be a coherent story, not a numerical perturbation.

A useful scenario set for a long thesis:

- **Base case** — the central path consistent with the named hypothesis. State the implied terminal value or multiple and the implied return.
- **Bull case** — the hypothesis plus a coherent set of positive optionalities (a new product reaches scale, a regulatory tailwind arrives, a contested market consolidates). Quantify the upside and name what would have to be observed in the next two to four quarters to indicate the bull case is materializing.
- **Bear case** — a coherent negative story, not a uniform haircut. Could be the named falsifiers in the hypothesis being true, plus secondary deterioration. The honest bear case is usually where the analyst learns whether the thesis can survive a real miss.
- **Tail scenarios** (optional) — events that are low-probability but high-consequence. A regulatory outcome, a litigation resolution, a takeout. State the probability the analyst assigns and the value impact.

Each scenario should be tied to specific named drivers and to specific observable signposts. Scenarios without signposts are decorative.

### Stage 8 — Risk register

The risk register is the discipline that prevents the analyst from talking themselves into the view. For each thesis-killer identified in Stage 3, capture:

- **Risk name** — a short label.
- **Mechanism** — how the risk would manifest in the operating or financial results.
- **Monitoring metric** — the observable signal that the risk is materializing.
- **Threshold** — the value or change of the monitoring metric that triggers action.
- **Action** — what the analyst would do (trim, exit, downgrade) if the threshold breached.

Risk registers without thresholds and actions are commentary, not risk management. The point of the discipline is that the analyst pre-commits to the action before the risk arrives, so the action is taken when the data shows up rather than rationalized away.

Three to seven risks is the right number. A register of fifteen risks indicates that the analyst has not chosen which risks are thesis-defining.

### Stage 9 — Recommendation memo

The memo is the last stage, not the entire workflow. A good recommendation memo is short, sharp, and pre-committed.

Recommended structure:

- **One-line view** — the recommendation, the price level at which it was struck, the holding period, and the expected return.
- **Claim** — the one or two sentences of the hypothesis from Stage 3.
- **Evidence** — three to five paragraphs of the strongest evidence supporting the claim. Should mostly be specifics from Stage 4 (fundamental) and Stage 6 (comp).
- **Mechanism** — how the divergence resolves into return over the holding period. Includes catalysts and the order in which they are expected.
- **Key risks** — the three to seven items from Stage 8 with their thresholds.
- **Position sizing** — the size relative to the portfolio, with the rationale tied to conviction and the asymmetry of bull versus bear cases.
- **Exit triggers** — the pre-decided conditions under which the position is reduced or exited.

Memo length: two to five pages for the core, with the model, the peer comp, and the scenario detail as appendices. Memos that exceed this length tend to dilute the view rather than strengthen it.

### Cross-cutting practices

A workflow needs a few practices that span the stages:

- **Time-box every stage.** A typical mid-cap deep dive runs three to eight working days. Stages that consume more than their budget should produce an explicit hand-off note rather than continue into the next stage.
- **Maintain a research log.** Every call taken, every primary document read, every channel check completed. The log is the audit trail that lets a later analyst (or the same analyst six months later) reconstruct why the view was held.
- **Run a pre-commit checklist before the memo lands.** Have all the named falsifiers been addressed? Is the peer set defensible? Are the scenarios coherent? Is the risk register actionable? A 60-second checklist catches most of the avoidable errors.
- **Schedule the post-mortem.** Within six months, the analyst should revisit the memo against what actually happened. The post-mortem is the only mechanism that compounds skill across many ideas.

### Calibrating the workflow to the team

The workflow scales differently for different teams. A solo analyst will compress the stages (often combining fundamental and comp into a single stage) and will lean more heavily on the templates to avoid omissions. A pod or fund will distribute the stages across roles — a junior on fundamentals and comp, a senior on hypothesis and risks, a portfolio manager on sizing and exit. The skill produces a workflow design and not a fixed allocation; calibrate the ownership grid to the team's shape.

### Calibrating to the time horizon

The workflow's emphasis shifts with the holding period.

- **Intraquarter** — Stage 5 (technical and flow) and the event calendar dominate. The fundamental layer is light and the scenario layer collapses to "data print versus consensus."
- **Six-to-twelve-month** — the canonical case. All nine stages get roughly the weight described above.
- **One-to-three-year** — the fundamental layer expands, the scenario set widens, and the risk register pushes toward structural risks (competitive position, regulatory regime, management quality).
- **Three-plus-year** — the workflow blurs into a quality-of-business framework. Sector dynamics, capital allocation history, and management track record become more important than near-term modeling.

## Examples

### Worked example — Designing a workflow for a US mid-cap industrial coverage list

**Inputs given to the skill:**

- coverage_universe: "US mid-cap industrial machinery names with $1B–$10B market cap and ROIC above 12% over the trailing three years."
- research_objective: "long-thesis."
- time_horizon: "one-to-three-years."
- data_access: "Primary filings, paid fundamentals vendor, two channel-check sessions per name."
- team_size: "Two-person pod — one senior, one associate."

**Output (abridged workflow design):**

> **Stage 1 — Frame.** Long-thesis bar: a named divergence resolving in 12–24 months with three falsifiers. Hypothesis written in two sentences and pinned to every artifact. Owner: senior. Time budget: half a day.
>
> **Stage 2 — Data inventory.** Filings, IR materials, trade association data, channel calls. Latency map noted; alt-data not used at this stage. Owner: associate. Time budget: half a day.
>
> **Stage 3 — Hypothesis generation.** Three candidate hypotheses, ranked. Each with stated mechanism and three falsifiers. Senior selects one. Time budget: one day.
>
> **Stage 4 — Fundamental layer.** Ten-year history reconstructed; unit economics expressed per machine and per service contract; quality of earnings reconciled; capital allocation pattern characterized; forward model out to 36 months with driver cells. Time budget: three days.
>
> **Stage 5 — Technical and flow.** One-page summary. Trend, volatility regime, ownership, event calendar. Time budget: half a day.
>
> **Stage 6 — Peer comp.** Five named peers, justified. Adjusted ROIC and free-cash-flow conversion as primary metrics; price multiples as appendix. Time budget: one day.
>
> **Stage 7 — Scenarios.** Base, bull, bear, plus one tail (large strategic acquisition). Each tied to two or three signposts. Time budget: half a day.
>
> **Stage 8 — Risk register.** Four to six thesis-killers with monitoring metrics and pre-decided trims. Time budget: half a day.
>
> **Stage 9 — Memo.** Two-page core, four-page appendix. Owner: senior writes; associate fact-checks. Time budget: one day.
>
> **Total budget:** Eight working days from kickoff to memo. Pre-commit checklist before publication.
>
> **Post-mortem:** Calendared at six months from publication.

The corresponding peer-comp template, scenario grid, risk register, and memo template would each be delivered as separate sections, with the field labels filled out generically and the workflow notes attached.

## Limitations

This skill produces a workflow design, not the actual research. The quality of the research that comes out of the workflow depends on the analyst's skill, the data available, and the honesty of the team in enforcing the gates. A bad analyst with a good workflow still produces bad work; a good analyst with a bad workflow does better than the workflow but worse than they would with a better one.

The skill does not select coverage. The coverage universe is an input; the skill assumes the user has already decided what to look at and is now designing how to look at it.

The skill is calibrated to discretionary fundamental equity research with a holding period of weeks to years. It does not address high-frequency strategies, event-driven arbitrage, distressed credit, or quantitative factor portfolios. For factor-based equity work, a companion skill on factor-research pipelines is more appropriate.

The skill is generic across geographies but the data sources, regulatory regimes, and disclosure conventions vary. A workflow designed against US filings needs adaptation before it is run against companies disclosing under different regulatory regimes — including different fiscal calendar conventions, different segment-disclosure requirements, and different audit standards.

Outputs are educational. Nothing produced by this skill is investment advice. Recommendation memos drafted using the template are research artifacts and should be reviewed by a licensed professional and subjected to a compliance check appropriate to the user's firm and jurisdiction before any position is taken.
