# Visual-Inertial SLAM System Architect

## When to use

Use this skill when a roboticist, perception engineer, or systems architect must design a **visual-inertial SLAM** system from the ground up — meaning the platform fuses one or more cameras with an inertial measurement unit to estimate its own pose and build a map of its surroundings. Common triggers:

- A new platform program is selecting sensors and must decide between mono+IMU, stereo+IMU, and depth+IMU configurations.
- A team has settled on a camera and IMU pair but has not committed to a tight-vs-loose coupling strategy, a keyframe policy, or a back-end optimizer style.
- An existing visual odometry stack is being upgraded with inertial sensing to handle aggressive motion, low-texture regions, or temporary occlusion.
- A persistent-mapping product is being added on top of a previously single-session-only VI-SLAM front-end and the team needs a map-server and re-localization plan.
- An audit of an existing design is needed before a hardware tape-out, a software architecture review, or a safety review.

The skill produces an **architecture document**, not code. It is method-level: which subsystems, which interfaces, which constraints, in which order. It deliberately stays library-agnostic in the body and reserves library citations for the source list at the end.

**Safety disclaimer (mandatory):** This skill produces methodology guidance for visual-inertial SLAM design. SLAM failures cause physical harm in autonomous systems. Every recommendation must be reviewed by qualified robotics engineers, validated in simulation, and bench-tested in a safe enclosure before any deployment near people or property. The recommendations below are starting positions for design discussions — they are never substitutes for hardware-in-the-loop testing, hazard analysis, or third-party review.

## How to apply

The steps below are sequenced because they are coupled: a sensor choice changes the calibration plan, which constrains the front-end, which determines the back-end, which determines how loop closure can be integrated. Skipping forward without resolving an earlier step almost always leads to rework.

### Step 1 — Frame the platform's motion and compute envelope

Before any sensor selection, write down the motion regime in concrete numbers. The motion regime is the single largest driver of the rest of the architecture.

Ask for:

- **Linear velocity envelope.** Maximum and typical speeds in each axis. Aerial platforms hovering at near-zero translation behave very differently from highway-speed ground vehicles.
- **Angular velocity envelope.** Maximum yaw, pitch, and roll rates. High angular rates dominate IMU error budgets and stress feature tracking through motion blur and inter-frame parallax loss.
- **Acceleration profile.** Sustained accelerations matter for bias observability; impulsive accelerations matter for IMU saturation and feature persistence.
- **Vibration spectrum.** Legged platforms, propeller-driven aerial platforms, and tracked vehicles all have characteristic vibration signatures that must be either mechanically isolated or modelled in the IMU front-end.
- **Compute envelope.** Onboard or offboard. If onboard, what is the CPU class, the available SIMD or vector units, the GPU or neural accelerator presence, the thermal headroom, and the power budget. These set a hard ceiling on front-end frame rate and back-end optimization frequency.
- **Latency tolerance.** Some downstream consumers (a closed-loop controller for a small aerial vehicle) demand pose estimates with single-digit millisecond latency. Others (an offline reconstruction pipeline) tolerate seconds.
- **Failure cost.** Be explicit. The cost of a tracking failure on a research scanner is annoyance; the cost on an unmanned aerial system flying near a person is injury. The architecture must scale paranoia to consequence.

The output of Step 1 is a one-page motion-and-compute brief that all later steps reference.

### Step 2 — Select the visual sensor configuration

The choice space is mono, stereo, RGB-D, multi-camera (often four to six cameras around the platform), and event-based. The decision is rarely free — it interacts with platform geometry, lighting, baseline, and budget.

Heuristics:

- **Monocular.** Cheapest, smallest, lowest power. Scale is unobservable from vision alone, so the IMU must carry scale. Mono+IMU is acceptable when the platform sees enough excitation (translational acceleration along multiple axes) to keep scale observable.
- **Stereo.** Provides metric scale directly from disparity, so the IMU is freed up to be a motion-smoothing aid rather than the scale anchor. The baseline must be matched to the working distance — a 10 cm baseline is appropriate for indoor distances of one to ten metres, much wider baselines are needed for outdoor and aerial use.
- **RGB-D.** Indoor structured-light or time-of-flight depth modules give dense geometry cheaply but degrade badly outdoors and on specular or transparent surfaces. Treat as an indoor solution.
- **Multi-camera rigs.** Improve coverage at the cost of calibration burden. Worth it when the platform must operate while moving sideways or backwards relative to its primary heading.
- **Event-based sensors.** Useful for very high-rate motion or extreme dynamic range scenes. Methodology is less mature; treat as a research bet, not a default.

Shutter type matters: rolling shutter introduces a per-row pose offset that must either be modelled in the front-end or avoided by selecting global-shutter optics. For platforms with angular rates above a few tens of degrees per second, choose global shutter or accept measurable accuracy loss.

### Step 3 — Select the inertial sensor

Categorize the candidate IMU by:

- **Bias instability** (typical units: degrees per hour for gyros, micro-g for accelerometers). Lower numbers mean longer dead-reckoning windows when vision drops out.
- **Random walk** (angle random walk for gyros, velocity random walk for accelerometers). Drives the noise floor of the integration.
- **Sample rate.** A few hundred hertz is the floor for most VI-SLAM. Higher rates help motion compensation and rolling-shutter correction.
- **Saturation limits.** Aggressive platforms can clip a consumer IMU. Match limits to the motion envelope from Step 1.
- **Temperature behaviour.** Bias drift with temperature can be the dominant error source on outdoor platforms across a working day.

For most ground and indoor platforms, a well-specified consumer MEMS IMU is sufficient. For aerial platforms with aggressive manoeuvres or for outdoor systems with long unaided segments, step up to a tactical-grade unit. The pairing between IMU class and camera is what determines the bias-observability story below; do not select these sensors independently.

### Step 4 — Lay out the calibration regime

Calibration is the silent cause of most "the system mostly works but drifts in odd ways" complaints. Treat it as a first-class subsystem.

Three layers, in order:

1. **Camera intrinsics.** Focal length, principal point, distortion. Performed once per camera with a planar target. The error tolerance is sub-pixel reprojection in the operating image region.
2. **Camera-to-IMU extrinsics.** The rigid-body transform between the camera frame and the IMU frame. Estimated from joint motion data with a calibrated target and a careful excitation pattern that excites all six degrees of freedom. Errors here masquerade as state-estimation bias and are notoriously hard to diagnose after the fact.
3. **Time synchronization.** The offset between the camera shutter timestamp and the IMU sample timestamp. Often modelled as a single scalar offset plus per-sensor jitter. Either hardware-synchronized (preferred) using shared trigger signals or estimated online as part of the state.

Document the calibration as a procedure, including the target type, the motion routine, the rejection criteria for a bad calibration, and the cadence at which it must be re-run (after mechanical stress, after temperature excursions, after firmware updates that change exposure timing).

For multi-camera rigs, add a cross-camera extrinsics step before the camera-to-IMU step. For RGB-D sensors, add a depth-to-colour alignment step.

If the platform also has wheel encoders or GNSS, define those alignment procedures in the same document — they will become factors in the back-end.

### Step 5 — Choose a front-end style

The front-end consumes raw image and inertial data and emits constraints suitable for the back-end. The major axes of choice:

- **Indirect (feature-based) vs direct (photometric).** Indirect methods extract sparse keypoints and descriptors, match across frames, and feed reprojection residuals to the back-end. They are robust to illumination changes and well-understood. Direct methods minimize photometric error on selected pixels and can extract more information per frame but are more sensitive to exposure, gain, and rolling-shutter artefacts.
- **Sparse vs semi-dense vs dense.** Sparse uses a few hundred features per frame, semi-dense uses thousands of high-gradient pixels, dense uses every pixel. Compute cost scales accordingly; the back-end design must match.
- **Keyframe selection policy.** When is a new frame promoted to a keyframe? Common policies include translation threshold, rotation threshold, parallax to nearest keyframe, time since last keyframe, and tracking-quality drop. A typical policy combines two or three of these with hysteresis.
- **Feature tracker.** Pyramidal optical flow for indirect-sparse front-ends, or descriptor matching for wider-baseline jumps. Track lifetime, outlier rejection (RANSAC variants), and minimum-track-length filters should all be specified.
- **IMU pre-integration window.** The IMU samples between two keyframes are summarized into a single relative motion constraint with a bias-aware Jacobian. The window must align with the keyframe policy.

Output of Step 5 is a written description of: what features, what tracker, what pre-integration scheme, what keyframe policy, what output is handed to the back-end and at what rate.

### Step 6 — Choose a back-end style

The back-end refines state estimates over a window of recent (or all) keyframes. Major styles:

- **Filtering, sliding-window EKF family.** A small fixed set of states is maintained, with old states marginalized out as new ones are added. Computationally cheap, well-suited to embedded compute, but information loss at the marginalization step is permanent. The multi-state-constraint filter approach is a common choice for feature observations across many frames without keeping all features in the state.
- **Sliding-window nonlinear optimization.** A bounded window of recent keyframes and features is jointly optimized at each update. More expensive than filtering, but the optimization can iterate to convergence at each step and benefits from second-order solvers.
- **Full smoothing with incremental solvers.** All keyframes and features ever observed are part of one factor graph, with incremental solvers re-solving only the affected subgraph after each update. Provides the lowest theoretical error and is the standard choice for offline mapping; viable in real time with careful engineering.

Match the back-end to the compute envelope and the failure cost:

- Aggressive aerial with single-digit-millisecond compute budget — filtering with sliding-window marginalization is usually the right starting point.
- Ground or handheld with a generous compute budget — sliding-window optimization is a good default.
- Persistent map with multi-session merging — full smoothing with incremental solvers is appropriate, because the back-end is the map.

### Step 7 — Decide tight vs loose coupling

A loosely coupled system runs vision and inertial pipelines independently and fuses their pose estimates downstream. A tightly coupled system jointly optimizes visual residuals and inertial residuals against a shared state.

The default recommendation for any modern VI-SLAM is **tight coupling**. The advantages compound:

- Bias estimates from the back-end inform the IMU pre-integration the next time around.
- The visual front-end can use IMU-predicted pose to constrain feature search regions, recovering tracking through fast rotations.
- During visual dropouts the IMU keeps the state estimable, and during inertial saturation the vision keeps the state observable.

Loose coupling remains acceptable when:

- The two pipelines are sourced from different teams with hard interface boundaries.
- The platform is required to certify each subsystem independently.
- The compute budget cannot accommodate joint optimization.

In all cases, document the coupling decision and the data-flow diagram before writing any back-end code.

### Step 8 — Design the loop-closure and place-recognition layer

Without loop closure, visual-inertial odometry drifts. With it, the system becomes a SLAM stack capable of bounded long-term error on revisits. Components:

- **Candidate generation.** A global appearance descriptor per keyframe (bag-of-words style or learned embedding) is stored in a searchable index. New keyframes query for top candidates.
- **Geometric verification.** A candidate is verified by attempting a relative-pose estimate (perspective-n-point with stored landmark triangulations, or relative essential-matrix recovery) and accepting only when the inlier count and reprojection error pass thresholds.
- **Consistency screen.** Single loop closures are not trusted; require N mutually consistent closures (often three) within a small region before injecting them into the back-end.
- **Robust factor formulation.** Loop closure factors enter the back-end with a robust kernel (Huber, Cauchy) or with a switchable-constraint variable that lets the optimizer reject mis-detections without diverging.
- **Rate control.** Run candidate search every K keyframes; run geometric verification only on a few top candidates; accept at most one new loop into the optimization per update step.

Design the layer to never block the front-end. Loop closure runs asynchronously; verified loops are queued and merged when the optimizer's next iteration starts.

### Step 9 — Plan drift-mitigation layers

Tight coupling and loop closure do not by themselves eliminate drift in long-running deployments. Add layered mitigations:

- **Auxiliary factor sources.** Wheel-odometry pre-integration on wheeled platforms; GNSS factor on outdoor systems with intermittent open-sky exposure; barometric altitude on aerial systems with persistent vertical drift.
- **Sub-mapping.** Break long sessions into local sub-maps with rigid transforms between them; constrain those transforms only when a verified loop closure ties two sub-maps together. This bounds the cost of an optimizer re-pass.
- **Pose-graph keyframe pruning.** Identify low-information keyframes (high overlap, low parallax change) and merge or drop them to keep the graph compact.
- **Bias observability monitors.** Continuously assess whether motion excitation has been sufficient to observe IMU biases. If not (e.g. a hovering aerial vehicle in still air), surface a warning that bias estimates are likely stale.
- **Health gauges.** Track innovation magnitudes, residual chi-squared statistics, feature-track counts, and pre-integration uncertainty. Expose these as runtime telemetry.

### Step 10 — Design the map representation and persistence layer

The "map" in a VI-SLAM system has at least two faces: the optimization map (keyframes, landmarks, factors) consumed internally, and the consumer map (occupancy, mesh, semantic layers) handed to planners or visualization. Decide both.

- **Internal map.** Keyframes with poses, landmark estimates with their host-keyframe references, and the factor graph itself. This is what the system carries forward across sessions if it persists.
- **Consumer map.** Occupancy grid (2D or volumetric) for planners; textured mesh or signed-distance field for visualization; semantic overlays if a perception layer feeds them.
- **Storage format.** A versioned on-disk representation with explicit schema migration rules. Maps live a long time; the format will outlast the team that wrote it.
- **Re-localization on startup.** When the platform restarts, it must locate itself in the persisted map before resuming SLAM. Define a re-localization procedure: gather K initial keyframes, query the place-recognition index, perform geometric verification, accept only with a confidence threshold, and otherwise fall back to a search-and-explore pattern under explicit operator awareness.
- **Multi-session merging.** Decide whether sessions append to a shared graph or each session is reconciled offline. Append-only with quality gates is the safer default; full online merging is a research-grade undertaking.
- **Multi-platform sharing.** If many robots share one map, version it, gate writes, and never let one platform's drift corrupt a shared baseline. Keep an immutable golden reference and treat all session contributions as candidates for promotion.

### Step 11 — Specify observability monitors and degradation policy

A VI-SLAM system that fails silently is more dangerous than one that fails noisily. Specify, for each failure mode, the monitor and the response.

Failure modes to enumerate:

- Feature track count collapses below a threshold.
- Pre-integration uncertainty grows beyond a threshold relative to recent history.
- Reprojection-residual chi-squared diverges.
- Place-recognition queries return no candidates over a long interval.
- IMU saturation flags assert.
- Camera exposure clips for many frames.
- Time-synchronization estimate jumps abruptly.

For each, define a response: drop to dead-reckoning, halt motion, escalate to operator, or shed accuracy for robustness (e.g. expand RANSAC thresholds and accept lower accuracy until conditions improve). Document the explicit hand-off interface to any downstream planner or controller.

### Step 12 — Validation rubric

Refuse to call the design done until the user commits to a validation plan with concrete numeric targets:

1. **Public-dataset replay.** Run the candidate stack on at least two independent ground-truth datasets that resemble the deployment regime. Report absolute trajectory error and relative pose error at multiple time horizons.
2. **In-house ground-truth runs.** Record on the actual platform with an external truth source (motion capture, RTK GNSS, surveyed fiducials) along a course that covers the expected operational range.
3. **Stress regression suite.** Targeted tests for the failure modes most relevant to the deployment (texture-poor halls, dynamic scenes, low light, aggressive motion). The suite must be automated and regressed on every release.
4. **Hardware soak.** A long-duration run in a representative environment, watching for slow divergence and resource leaks.
5. **Failure-mode rehearsals.** Deliberately trigger each monitored failure and verify the documented response is taken.

The architecture document is finished when every step above has a written answer and the validation rubric has explicit pass criteria.

## Inputs

The skill expects four bundles of information:

- **Platform brief.** Class, motion envelope, compute budget, power and thermal envelope, failure-cost rationale.
- **Sensor brief.** Cameras (count, type, shutter, FOV, frame rate, optics), IMU (class, noise spec, rate), auxiliary sensors and their interfaces, synchronization mechanism.
- **Environment brief.** Indoor/outdoor mix, texture and structure regime, lighting conditions, dynamic-actor density, scale, single- vs multi-session.
- **Requirements brief.** Real-time vs offline, accuracy targets, localization-only vs full SLAM, multi-platform sharing, certification or regulatory constraints.

When the user supplies less than this, the skill must enumerate the missing inputs and ask before producing a design.

1. Platform and motion summary (one page).
2. Sensor selection rationale with explicit trade-offs.
3. Calibration procedure with cadence and rejection criteria.

## Examples

> **Indoor inspection drone, stereo + tactical IMU, dim repeated environment, persistent map.**
>
> One-paragraph sketch the skill might produce:
> Global-shutter stereo at modest resolution and 30–60 frames per second, tactical IMU at the hundreds-of-hertz range, hardware-triggered shutter aligned to IMU sample edges. Tight coupling, indirect sparse front-end with pyramidal optical flow, keyframe policy combining translation, rotation, and parallax thresholds. Sliding-window nonlinear back-end with bounded keyframe window plus a parallel full-smoothing background process for persistent map maintenance. Bag-of-features style place recognition with strict three-loop consistency screen and switchable-constraint factors. Persistent on-disk graph with append-only multi-session merging and an explicit promotion step gated by a nightly batch verification. Observability monitors covering track count, residual chi-squared, and bias-observability score. Validation against a public stereo-inertial benchmark plus a recorded in-house dataset captured with motion-capture truth in a mocked-up inspection cell.

> **Wearable handheld scanner, mono + consumer IMU, indoor, single-session, embedded ARM compute.**
>
> Mono with a wide field of view and global shutter, consumer MEMS IMU at a few hundred hertz, software time-sync with online offset estimation. Tight coupling, indirect sparse front-end, sliding-window filtering back-end with a short window to fit the compute envelope. Loop closure only at user-trigger or at session end (offline batch). No persistent map; the operator captures one scan per session and post-processes. Observability monitors emphasising track count and bias observability since hand motion can be unintentionally still for long intervals. Validation against handheld-style public datasets plus indoor truth-fiducial captures.

## Limitations

- The skill targets architecture and methodology, not source code. Translating the design into a specific library still requires implementation and tuning work.
- Coverage of learning-based front-ends (learned feature detectors and descriptors, neural depth predictors, end-to-end pose networks) is intentionally light; the field moves quickly and any specific recommendation would age fast.
- Map representations such as neural radiance fields and Gaussian-splatting are out of scope for this skill; they are evolving research areas without settled best practice for safety-critical deployment.
- Calibration procedures are sketched at the methodology level only — a separate calibration skill should be used to operate them.
- License notes are point-in-time. Several widely-cited reference systems use restrictive licenses; re-verify each repository's license before vendoring code into a proprietary product.
