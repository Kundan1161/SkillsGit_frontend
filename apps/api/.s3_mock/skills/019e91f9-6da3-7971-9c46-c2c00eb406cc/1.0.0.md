## When to use

Reach for this skill when an agent is asked to plan or document the construction of a logo system. Typical situations:

- A new brand has a primary mark and needs the full variant set the world demands of a real logo.
- An existing logo works at hero size but breaks at favicon size and the team needs a responsive cascade.
- A brand is preparing for a trademark filing and needs the artifact set and specimen plan ready for counsel.
- A brand owner needs the production file matrix to hand to vendors — print, embroidery, etching, partner distribution.
- A logo refresh requires that old marks be retired cleanly while new ones land everywhere.

Skip this skill when the request is to design the primary mark itself from scratch (that work belongs to a designer or an originator, not a methodology checklist), when the request is purely aesthetic critique of a candidate mark, or when the deliverable is a single asset rather than a system.

## How to apply

Work through the construction and production layers in this order. Optical and reproduction issues usually surface at steps 8 through 14; do not skip them to get to file generation.

1. Restate the brief.
   Note the brand idea, the personality words, the surfaces the mark must reach, and the constraints.
   Do not begin construction without the brief — it determines whether a responsive cascade is needed and how aggressive the small-size variant can be.
   Where the brief leaves the smallest target unstated, ask; the smallest-size requirement constrains every other decision downstream.

2. Identify the mark archetype.
   The common archetypes are wordmark only, symbol only, combination mark (symbol plus wordmark), monogram, lettermark, emblem, and abstract mark.
   The archetype determines the variant set and the construction grid.
   Match the archetype to the brand idea rather than to current visual trends; archetypes survive across decades, trends do not.

3. Choose the construction grid.
   A grid expresses the mark's geometry — circles tangent to squares, baselines aligned to a unit, optical curves overshooting mathematical curves.
   The grid is the discipline that lets future extensions stay coherent.
   Express grid units as a fraction of cap height or x-height so the grid scales with type rather than with pixels.
   Document the grid on a hidden layer of the source file so the next designer can see what was intended.

4. Construct the primary mark on the grid.
   Establish the mathematical curves, the corner radii, the stroke widths, and the relationships of letterforms to symbol.
   Where the wordmark is set in a typeface, decide whether kerning is custom or default; custom kerning is canonical at logo sizes and must be preserved through file exports by converting text to outlines.

5. Apply optical correction.
   The eye does not see mathematical curves and the eye is what matters.
   Apply overshoot to round shapes that sit on baselines, optically center counters that look light, balance stroke weights so vertical strokes are heavier than horizontal where the family demands it, and adjust spacing where letter pairs collide.
   A logo that looks correct on the grid usually looks wrong in use.
   Optical decisions are designer judgment; the methodology names the corrections to consider but cannot specify the exact amount.

6. Define the secondary mark.
   The secondary mark is what runs where the primary does not fit — typically a monogram, a symbol, or a stacked variant.
   The secondary should derive from the primary, not be unrelated.
   Specify the contexts each is used in so future operators do not have to guess.

7. Define the responsive cascade.
   List the size buckets the mark must cover — from favicon (sixteen pixels) up through hero (several thousand pixels).
   For each bucket, name the variant that runs there.
   Common cascades go: at sixteen to twenty-four pixels, a simplified symbol or initial; at thirty-two to forty-eight pixels, the symbol; at sixty-four to a hundred and twenty pixels, the symbol with a short wordmark or a stacked combination; above that, the full combination or wordmark.
   Confirm legibility at every bucket against the actual rendering target, not just the source vector.

8. Define minimum sizes per medium.
   Express digital minima in pixels at 1x device pixel ratio, and print minima in millimeters.
   Distinguish minimum legible from minimum identifiable — at very small sizes a wordmark loses its glyphs and becomes a silhouette; at that size, the symbol or initial is the only option that works.
   Test minima on representative substrates and devices, not just on the design surface.

9. Define clear space.
   Express clear space as a multiple of a glyph height or a logo-internal unit so the rule scales with the mark.
   Common rules are one cap height, one x-height of the wordmark, or one symbol unit.
   The rule must be the same on every side or the asymmetry will be applied inconsistently in the wild.
   State whether the clear space measures from the bounding box of the mark or from optical edges; the two produce different results.

10. Define lockups.
    A lockup is the locked relationship of the mark to a tagline, a sub-brand, a product name, or a partner mark.
    Define horizontal, stacked, and any specialty lockups.
    Lockups have their own clear-space and minimum-size rules; carry those through.
    Provide a small finite set of named lockups rather than a recipe that produces an unbounded number.

11. Define color variants.
    Specify a full-color variant, a single-color light variant for use on dark backgrounds, a single-color dark variant for use on light backgrounds, an inverted variant if the mark uses negative space, and a knockout variant for embroidery, etching, or single-ink print.
    Confirm that every variant is independently constructed and not auto-derived; mathematical inversion often produces unusable results, especially when the mark uses negative space or fine detail.

12. Define extreme-size variants.
    At the smallest sizes, simplify — drop strokes that disappear, increase stroke weight to maintain visibility, replace fine letterforms with their cap forms.
    At the largest sizes, audit for joints, kerning, and registration artifacts that only show at scale.
    Where the mark will be reproduced at billboard or signage scale, audit the construction at one-to-one and account for distance-of-view, contrast at angle, and reproduction tolerances of the medium.

13. Define the favicon and app-icon set.
    The favicon set must cover at minimum sixteen, thirty-two, forty-eight, and a high-resolution PNG at one hundred and ninety-two pixels for modern browsers, plus a vector SVG favicon and a Safari pinned-tab mono variant.
    The app icon set includes platform-specific masks (iOS rounded, Android adaptive with foreground and background layers, Windows tile sizes).
    Confirm each icon is designed at its target size, not scaled from a single master, because optical correction does not survive arbitrary scaling.
    Document the alignment grid for each platform mask so the mark sits where the platform expects.

14. Define dark and light treatments.
    Beyond inverting color, decide whether the mark sits on the same background it would in light mode, or whether the surrounding rule changes.
    Decide whether brand color shifts in dark mode — many brand colors are too saturated to sit comfortably on dark surfaces and need a tint variant.
    Audit the treatment at the smallest size buckets; what works at hero scale on dark mode often loses contrast at favicon scale.

15. Build the don't-use catalog.
    Enumerate the most likely abuses — rotation, color substitution, gradient overlay, drop shadow, distortion, recoloring of partner adjacency, placement on busy imagery, replacement of the wordmark with another typeface.
    Show each as a labeled negative example.
    The catalog should be short and specific; long catalogs are skimmed.
    Refresh the catalog quarterly with observed misuses from audits.

16. Define the construction documentation.
    Every variant has a source file in vector format with the grid visible on a hidden layer, with text converted to outlines for production files, with naming that encodes the variant, color treatment, and aspect, and with a versioned export history.
    Without the grid layer, future extensions cannot stay coherent.
    Store source files in a tracked repository, not on a single designer's drive.

17. Define the production file matrix.
    List the formats, their use cases, and their export settings:
    - SVG (cleaned, optimized, with viewBox set, with embedded title and description for accessibility) for web.
    - PNG at standard sizes with transparent background for raster digital use.
    - PDF-X (or PDF/A as required) with embedded color profile for print.
    - EPS for legacy print and vendor compatibility.
    - ICO bundle for legacy favicon support.
    - PNG sets for app-icon platforms.
    - Mono single-ink variants (vector, knockout) for embroidery and etching.
    - High-resolution TIFF with color profile for special print where vector is not accepted.
    Confirm each format renders correctly in the consumer it targets; an SVG that opens in a browser may fail in a vendor's print workflow.

18. Define color profiles.
    Web exports use sRGB.
    Print exports use CMYK with a documented profile (FOGRA, GRACoL, or whatever the print pipeline accepts), and any spot colors are documented with Pantone or equivalent equivalents.
    Specify the conversion rule when a single-source vector must be reproduced in CMYK from an RGB source — let the print vendor convert, but specify the target profile.
    Where wide-gamut surfaces (Display-P3) are supported, document the gamut-mapped variant alongside the sRGB master.

19. Define the naming convention.
    A filename should encode the mark family, the variant, the color treatment, and the format.
    A pattern like `brand-mark-primary-full-onlight.svg`, `brand-mark-secondary-mono-ondark.svg`, `brand-favicon-32.png` reads cleanly at a glance and sorts predictably.
    State the convention and apply it without exceptions; exceptions accumulate into chaos.

20. Set up the generation pipeline.
    Source files are authored in vector.
    A pipeline (a script or an asset-management workflow) generates the derivative formats and sizes on demand and produces the favicon and app-icon bundles automatically.
    Manual export is a source of drift; generated derivatives stay coherent with the source.
    Confirm the pipeline is idempotent — running it twice produces identical files, byte-for-byte where the format allows.

21. Plan trademark prep.
    Counsel performs the filing; the methodology produces the artifacts counsel needs.
    Prepare the master vector file in the form that will be filed (color or black-and-white per counsel's recommendation), a specimen showing the mark in use in commerce, a list of the goods and services classes that will be filed, evidence of first use in commerce dates, and a list of jurisdictions and their order.
    Flag any names or marks already in use by others in the same class; that search is counsel's work, not the agent's.
    Confirm the specimen shows the mark used as a trademark, not as decoration, since trademark offices in many jurisdictions distinguish the two.

22. Plan partner distribution.
    Decide which variants partners may use, in which contexts, and under what license.
    Bundle the partner-permitted files into a distinct download with a usage rules document.
    Do not ship source files to partners unless the relationship requires it; ship optimized, watermarked-if-needed derivatives.
    Log distributions with partner identifier, asset identifier, and version so audit can trace any misuse to its source.

23. Plan retirement of old marks.
    When a new mark lands, the old mark stays in circulation for months or years.
    State the retirement schedule per surface (digital first, then print, then merchandise, then partner-distributed), set an end date past which the old mark is no longer permitted, and notify partners with enough lead time to comply.
    Move retired masters to an archive folder rather than deleting them; legal and audit need them.

24. Plan an audit cadence.
    Quarterly is reasonable for digital surfaces; annual for everything else.
    Note observed misuses and feed them into the don't-use catalog.
    The catalog is most useful when it reflects what people have actually done, not what someone imagined they might do.

25. Produce the deliverables.
    The final outputs of the methodology are the construction documentation, the variant and lockup spec, the don't-use catalog, the production file matrix, the favicon and app-icon plan, the partner distribution bundle, the trademark prep packet for counsel, and the retirement plan for any outgoing mark.

## Anti-patterns to avoid

The methodology fails when any of these creep in. Flag them explicitly when reviewing a logo system.

- A single source file with every variant in different layers, no grid, and inconsistent naming. The next designer cannot extend the system.
- Auto-derived color variants (a single source rendered through a fill swap). Negative-space marks and fine-detail marks break under mechanical inversion.
- Favicons generated by scaling the hero mark down to sixteen pixels. Optical correction does not survive scaling; the result is a blur where strokes should be visible.
- A clear-space rule measured in pixels rather than in an internal unit. The rule cannot scale to print or to signage.
- A production file set that includes only PNG and JPG. Print vendors need vector, embroidery vendors need single-ink knockout, and apps need platform-specific masks.
- Source files distributed to partners. Partners lose the source, modify it, and the modified version appears in production with no audit trail.
- A retirement plan that deletes the old mark from the archive. Legal and audit need the history; archive but do not delete.
- A trademark filing prepared without a specimen showing use in commerce. Many jurisdictions require evidence of use, and the filing will fail without it.

## Examples

A request like "construct the logo system for a developer-tools brand whose primary mark is a wordmark with a glyph initial" should produce:
- A wordmark-plus-glyph combination as the primary mark with the glyph at a documented size relationship to the wordmark.
- A glyph-only secondary mark for tight spaces.
- A four-bucket responsive cascade from favicon to hero with the variant for each bucket named explicitly.
- A sixteen-to-thirty-two-pixel optical variant that thickens strokes and drops fine letterform detail.
- A favicon set including SVG, ICO bundle, high-resolution PNG, and Safari pinned-tab mono.
- An app-icon set with iOS rounded master, Android adaptive layers, and Windows tile sizes.
- A print file set that includes PDF-X with the documented CMYK profile and EPS for legacy vendors.
- A don't-use catalog with labeled negatives.
- A naming convention applied consistently across the file set.

A request like "prepare our refreshed logo for trademark filing in the US and EU" should produce:
- The master vector in the form counsel requested (color or black-and-white).
- Two specimens showing use in commerce on the brand's surfaces.
- A list of goods-and-services classes proposed for filing.
- A list of jurisdictions and the filing order.
- A flag that the trademark availability search must be performed by counsel and that the methodology does not constitute legal advice.
- A partner notification plan for the retirement of the previous mark, with retirement dates per surface.
- Archive of the previous master files with a status flag indicating retired.

## Limitations

- The skill produces a methodology and a plan; it does not draw the primary mark. A designer or an originator is required for that work.
- Trademark filings must be performed by qualified counsel; the skill produces the artifact set counsel needs and lists the decisions counsel must make.
- Optical-correction decisions are ultimately human judgments. The skill names the corrections to consider, not the exact correction amounts.
- Print color reproduction is a vendor- and substrate-specific problem. The skill names the profiles and the conversion rule but cannot guarantee match without proofing on the actual substrate.
- App-icon platform requirements change over time. The skill names the current platform masks but specific size and corner-radius requirements must be verified against the current platform documentation.
- Trademark availability search, classification advice, and filing strategy are counsel's work. The skill prepares artifacts but does not perform searches or recommend strategy.
- Output should be reviewed by a designer and, for trademark prep, by counsel before being treated as final.

## What this skill does not do

To keep scope clear, the skill explicitly does not:

- Originate the primary mark. The mark is designer work; the methodology surrounds it with the variants, files, and rules that turn it into a system.
- Author the brand voice or imagery direction. Use the brand-system-architect skill for those layers.
- Author the guidelines document. Use the brand-guidelines-authoring skill for that.
- Operate the asset pipeline that distributes the files. Use the brand-asset-pipeline skill for that.
- Conduct a trademark search or any other legal clearance.
- Negotiate vendor proofs or perform print color calibration on a specific press.

## Handoff artifacts

The methodology produces a set of artifacts that downstream consumers can use without re-interpreting the system:

- A designer extending the system needs the construction grid (visible on a hidden layer of the source file), the optical-correction notes, and the variant matrix with the rationale for each variant.
- A token engineer needs the brand color values per variant, the favicon and app-icon manifest, and the SVG title and description templates.
- An asset-pipeline operator needs the production file matrix, the naming convention, the generation script or pipeline configuration, and the partner-distribution bundle definition.
- A guidelines author needs the don't-use catalog, the clear-space and minimum-size rules, the lockup set, and the responsive cascade.
- Counsel preparing a trademark filing needs the master vector in counsel's preferred form, specimens of use in commerce, and the classification list.
- A vendor producing a print run needs the PDF-X with the color profile and any spot-color specifications.
